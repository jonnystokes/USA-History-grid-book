# Research Bank — Chapter 15: Food and Farming (`food-farming`)

**Status: RESEARCHED 2026-08-07.** Every date, number, and name in `outlines/food-farming.md` traces to an entry here. Web-verified this session unless marked as carried from another chapter's verified bank. Journalism labeled. Ranges and disputes stated, never silently resolved.

Angle: what Americans ate and how they grew it. Keep out: farm machinery as invention (`technology`); farm economics (`economy`); farm labor as organizing (`work-workers`); the Dust Bowl exodus (`migration`); the soil itself (`land-environment`).

## Era 01 — Before 1500: skilled farming, regional plenty

- **Maize was bred from teosinte.** Corn's wild ancestor is teosinte (Zea mays subspecies parviglumis), a grass whose seed head is a few hard kernels; domestication began about **9,000 years ago in the Balsas River valley of southern Mexico** (states of Guerrero/Michoacán), confirmed by genetics and archaeology. Thousands of years of Native selection turned it into corn — a plant that cannot even reseed itself without people. Corn then spread north over millennia into what is now the U.S. (Sources: Smithsonian Magazine "Rethinking the Corny History of Maize"; Science/AAAS coverage of maize-origin genetics; Earth@Home evolution pages; UNM archaeology release on early maize.)
- **The Three Sisters — why the three plants help each other.** Corn, beans, and squash planted together: the corn stalk is the pole the beans climb; bean roots carry nitrogen-fixing bacteria that feed the soil all three grow in; the squash's broad leaves shade the ground, hold moisture, and smother weeds. Grown together they also feed people better together — corn plus beans supplies more complete protein. (Sources: USFWS "Three Sisters planting method"; Soil Science Society of America "How do the three sister plants work together?"; Native Seeds/SEARCH; Farm Bureau Foundation for Agriculture. `land-environment` uses the same system as a soil story; ours is the food and the technique.)
- **The Eastern Agricultural Complex — farming invented independently in the East.** Native peoples of eastern North America domesticated their own crop package thousands of years before corn arrived: squash (Cucurbita pepo, independently domesticated in the East ~5,000 years ago), sunflower, goosefoot (Chenopodium berlandieri), sumpweed/marsh elder, maygrass, little barley, erect knotweed. Eastern North America is one of the world's independent centers of plant domestication. (Sources: Smith, "Eastern North America as an independent center of plant domestication" (PNAS); NPS "Goosefoot" article; Royal Society Phil Trans B 2025 synthesis "The sleeping crops of eastern North America.")
- **Regional foodways.** Plains: bison (roughly 30 million in the early-1800s baseline — carried from the verified `land-environment`/`native-nations` banks). Northwest coast and rivers: salmon as the staple. Great Lakes: **manoomin** (wild rice, Zizania palustris) — "the good berry" in Ojibwe, harvested by canoe; in Anishinaabe prophecy the people were led to "the food that grows on water," and manoomin remains central to Ojibwe identity and diet today (Native continuity thread — carry it past 1900). Coasts: shellfish; Northeast: maple sap. (Sources: Michigan Sea Grant "Manoomin"; Great Lakes Indian Fish & Wildlife Commission; Wisconsin Wetlands Association; Native Harvest / White Earth Land Recovery Project.)
- **Maize fed towns.** Cahokia (near present-day St. Louis) peaked about 1100 at roughly 10,000–20,000 people, fed by corn agriculture — carried from the verified `native-nations`/`migration` banks.
- **No named farmer survives from this era at story zoom.** Say so plainly; the book does not invent people. (Present-day Native seed keepers carry these crops' continuity — told in era 10.)

## Era 02 — The 1500s: the Columbian Exchange in food

- **The exchange, named.** The term "Columbian Exchange" comes from historian Alfred W. Crosby's 1972 book *The Columbian Exchange: Biological and Cultural Consequences of 1492*. (Sources: Smithsonian Magazine interview with Crosby; Britannica "Columbian exchange.")
- **What left the Americas:** corn (maize), potatoes, tomatoes, beans, squash, peanuts, peppers, cacao (chocolate), vanilla, turkeys. These remade eating worldwide — corn and potatoes became staple calories across Europe, Africa, and Asia. **What arrived:** wheat, rice, sugarcane, cattle, pigs, sheep, goats, chickens, horses (returned 1493, Columbus's second voyage), honeybees, citrus. (Sources: Britannica "Columbian exchange"; World History Encyclopedia; Newberry Library "Foods of the Columbian Exchange.")
- **Honest framing of who carried it.** The exchange was not a friendly trade: Europeans carried American crops home from lands they were invading, and much of what "arrived" came to feed colonizers and, soon, sugar plantations worked by enslaved people. Africans — most brought in chains — carried African crops and food knowledge to the Americas: okra, watermelon, yams, sorghum, black-eyed peas, and African rice, plus the skills to grow them. Scholarship explicitly credits enslaved Africans as agents of these crop transfers, not just cargo. (Sources: Britannica; Carney & Rosomoff scholarship on African crops in the Atlantic world; "African Rice in the Columbian Exchange" (Journal of African History / ResearchGate abstract).)
- **Pigs as walking food supply.** De Soto's expedition landed near Tampa Bay in May 1539 with a small herd of pigs (chronicles say about 13, grown to ~700 by 1542); escapees went feral across the Southeast. Spanish colonists planted wheat and citrus and ran cattle in Florida (St. Augustine founded 1565) and New Mexico (1598). (Sources: carried from verified `land-environment` bank; Britannica on St. Augustine.)
- **Thin era for American eating itself** — the land's people still ate as before; the century's food story is the exchange crossing the ocean. Frame it that way, honestly.

## Era 03 — The 1600s: Native crops save the colonies; tobacco displaces food

- **Jamestown's Starving Time, winter 1609–10.** The colonists planned to live partly on trade with the Powhatan and on supply ships; in autumn 1609 Powhatan cut off food trade and besieged the fort. Of about 500 colonists at the start of that winter, roughly **61 were alive by spring** — they ate horses, dogs, rats, leather, and there is archaeological and documentary evidence of survival cannibalism (the girl researchers call "Jane," announced by the Smithsonian in 2013). Before and after, corn bought, seized, or grown from Powhatan stock kept the colony alive. (Sources: Historic Jamestowne "The Starving Time"; Encyclopedia Virginia; Britannica; George Percy's account (National Humanities Center).)
- **Squanto (Tisquantum) — the verified chronology.** A Patuxet man, kidnapped in **1614** by the English captain Thomas Hunt and taken to be sold in Spain; reached England, learned English, and returned to New England in **1619** with an English expedition — to find Patuxet emptied by the 1616–19 epidemic. In spring **1621** he taught the Plymouth colonists to plant corn, and served as interpreter with the Wampanoag; he died in **1622**. The colonists' first corn harvest (autumn 1621) is why Plymouth survived its second winter. (Sources: World History Encyclopedia "Squanto"; History of Massachusetts Blog; Yale University Press history blog; Encyclopedia.com.)
- **The fish-fertilizer story is disputed — state it honestly.** The schoolbook detail that Squanto taught the Pilgrims to bury fish with corn seed is real in the colonial record (Winslow reported it), but whether it was a *Native* practice is contested: anthropologist **Lynn Ceci argued in *Science* (1975)** that fish fertilizer was likely a European technique Squanto picked up during his years among Europeans, since evidence for it as a widespread Native practice is thin; other scholars defended the traditional account, and the debate has run for decades without resolution. Tell it as: he taught them; where he learned it is argued. (Sources: Ceci, "Fish Fertilizer: A Native North American Practice?", *Science* 188 (1975); languagehat and academic summaries of the ensuing controversy.)
- **Tobacco displaces food.** John Rolfe's sweet-strain tobacco (first successful Virginia crop, 1612 — carried from standard Virginia histories; Encyclopedia Virginia "Tobacco in Colonial Virginia") paid so well that planters grew it instead of food; Virginia's assembly repeatedly tried to force balance — the tobacco inspection law of **1629–30 limited families to 2,000 tobacco plants per family member**, in part so food would still be planted. Tobacco is farming but not food: the chapter tells what it *did to food growing*; the money is `economy`'s, the labor system `slavery-freedom`'s. (Sources: Encyclopedia Virginia; virginiaplaces.org tobacco pages; FEE "Crop Controls in Colonial Virginia.")
- **What colonists actually ate:** corn ground into hominy, samp, and johnnycakes; beans; pork once hogs multiplied; cider. Corn dishes were learned from Native cooks and stayed on American tables for centuries (Amelia Simmons's 1796 cookbook still centers cornmeal — see era 05). The 1621 harvest gathering itself is `holidays`' story (the myth-vs-record work is the Afterword's).

## Era 04 — 1700–1750: rice built on African knowledge; the breadbasket

- **Carolina rice.** Rice became the Lowcountry's great crop from the 1690s onward; by this era South Carolina's economy ran on it, and the variety later called **Carolina Gold** made planters some of the richest people in the colonies. The fields were built and worked by enslaved Africans, and planters **advertised for and paid more for captives from the West African "Rice Coast"** (Senegambia, Sierra Leone) precisely because they knew rice. Documented African skills in Carolina fields: embanked tidal fields, sowing by pressing seed with heel and toe, winnowing with coiled fanner baskets. (Sources: Lowcountry Digital History Initiative "Rice in the Lowcountry" (College of Charleston); SAPIENS "Rice Reveals Enslaved Africans' Agricultural Heritage"; Carolina Gold Rice Foundation materials.)
- **The "Black Rice" debate — state it, don't resolve it.** Geographer **Judith Carney's *Black Rice* (2001)** argued African knowledge was the foundation of the Carolina rice economy. In 2005 historians **David Eltis, Philip Morgan, and David Richardson** countered that the African contribution had been exaggerated. The middle position most scholars now hold: the industry grew from a mix of European capital and coercion and genuine African expertise — and the expertise is documented. Tell the range. (Sources: Harvard UP *Black Rice* page; SAPIENS; EH.net review; the 2007 *AHR* exchange as described in those summaries.)
- **Enslaved rice growers are real but rarely named in this era's records.** Keep the story slot a target: tell the documented *skills* (fanner baskets, heel-and-toe sowing) collectively rather than invent an individual. The enslavement system itself is `slavery-freedom`'s subject.
- **Indigo and Eliza Lucas Pinckney.** Eliza Lucas (later Pinckney), left managing three South Carolina plantations **at about age 16** when her father was posted to Antigua, experimented with indigo seed from 1739 and in **1744** produced the colony's first successful indigo dye crop, shared seed with neighboring planters, and indigo exports rose from about **5,000 pounds in 1745–46 to 130,000 pounds by 1748** — a major export until the Revolution. Indigo is a dye, not a food — include as farming, one span, alongside rice. The skilled work again drew on enslaved and Caribbean indigo knowledge (her indigo-maker from Montserrat is documented in her letters). (Sources: South Carolina Encyclopedia "Pinckney, Eliza Lucas"; Encyclopedia.com; NSDAR chapter biography. No feature film or documentary about her verified — Movie line omitted.)
- **The middle colonies as breadbasket.** Pennsylvania, New York, and New Jersey grew wheat beyond their needs and shipped flour through Philadelphia and New York — the standard "breadbasket colonies" account (Britannica; colonial-economy references). Keep general; no single verified number carried.
- **The potato arrives.** The first permanent North American potato patches are credited to Scots-Irish settlers at **Londonderry, New Hampshire, 1719**. (Sources: New Hampshire historical accounts (Cow Hampshire; Nutfield Genealogy); Maine Organic Farmers "Solanum Tuberosum." The potato is American in deep origin — domesticated in the Andes — and came back via Europe; say so to close the exchange loop.)
- **Meals by class:** most tables held corn, pork, bread, and cider; the rich ate imported tea, sugar, wine, and wheat bread. General framing from colonial-foodways references — keep unnumbered.

## Era 05 — 1750–1800: a nation of farmers; the first American cookbook

- **A nation of farmers.** At the first census (1790), the U.S. counted about 4 million people, and **about 90 percent of the labor force farmed**. (Sources: USDA/agricultural-history timelines as reproduced in extension summaries (Ohio State extension "History of American Agriculture"); Human Progress/ERS-derived series. Widely used USDA figure; label as approximate.)
- **The farm as republican ideal.** Thomas Jefferson, *Notes on the State of Virginia* (written 1781–84, privately printed 1785, first public edition 1787), Query XIX: "Those who labour in the earth are the chosen people of God, if ever he had a chosen people." The idea that independent family farms make free citizens shaped land law for a century (the survey grid and later the Homestead Act are its offspring — `land-environment` and this chapter share that thread). (Sources: Monticello/Jefferson Quotes; Teaching American History full text.)
- **Whiskey moves the corn crop.** A jug of whiskey travels where a wagon of corn cannot; backcountry farmers turned surplus corn into whiskey to get it to market. **`drugs-alcohol` leads** (whiskey as money and the 1794 Whiskey Rebellion); this chapter takes one sentence.
- **Amelia Simmons, *American Cookery* (Hartford, 1796)** — the first cookbook written by an American for American kitchens. On the title page she calls herself only "an American orphan"; **nothing else about her life is known — say so plainly.** Innovations verified: first known printed recipes using American cornmeal (johnnycake, "Indian slapjack," Indian pudding); first printed use of **pearlash** (a wood-ash salt) as a chemical leavening — the ancestor of baking powder; early printed appearances of Dutch-American words like "cookey" and "slaw." (Sources: Library of Congress / American Antiquarian Society facsimile editions (Andrews McMeel); Connecticut History.org; NPR The Salt "New Nation, New Cuisine." No film exists — Movie line omitted.)
- Farm diets: corn, pork, and cider remain the base; farm daybooks and probate inventories are the documentation. No single named household verified this session — story slot stays target with that honest note.

## Era 06 — 1800–1850: the prairie opens; food is preserved for strangers

- **The steel plow and the reaper — our angle is what they meant for farming.** Prairie sod was dense and sticky; iron plows gummed up and stalled. Blacksmith **John Deere, Grand Detour, Illinois, 1837**, made a polished steel plow that scoured itself clean and cut the prairie; by 1846 he was selling near a thousand a year. **Cyrus McCormick** first successfully demonstrated his mechanical reaper at Steele's Tavern, Virginia, **1831** (patent 1834): grain that had been cut by hand with sickle and cradle could now be harvested many times faster, so a family could plant far more wheat. **The machines as inventions are `technology`'s story** (their slug: mccormick-deere); ours is the farm: the prairie became wheat and corn country within a generation. (Sources: Britannica "John Deere"; ASME; Virginia Museum of History & Culture / Science Museum Group on the 1831 demonstration.)
- **Canning arrives.** Nicolas Appert (France) proved sealed-and-heated food kept, 1809; canning crossed to America — **William Underwood opened a Boston cannery in 1820–21**, and **Thomas Kensett** (New York) canned seafood and produce and received an American **patent for tin cans in 1825**. Preserved food could now be sold to strangers far away — the start of food as a shipped, branded product. (Sources: can-industry histories (Can Manufacturers Institute "History of the Can"); National Archives "Tin Cans & Patents"; Food Ingredient Facts canning history.)
- **The ice trade.** Boston merchant **Frederic Tudor** shipped his first cargo of New England pond ice to **Martinique in 1806**; the business grew until in **1833** he landed **180 tons of ice in Calcutta, India**. Ice cut from ponds (including Walden) cooled city food, fresh fish, and drinks, and made the icebox a kitchen fixture. (Sources: New England Historical Society; Saturday Evening Post ice-trade history; Wikipedia "Frederic Tudor" cross-checked for dates.)
- **Cotton displaces food in the South.** Cotton's spread pushed food growing to the margins across the Deep South; plantations often bought Midwestern pork and corn rather than grow enough. The cotton economy is `economy`'s and `slavery-freedom`'s; this chapter states only what it did to food farming. (General framing — standard histories; keep unnumbered.)
- **The potato blight crossed FROM America.** The late-blight water mold (Phytophthora infestans) first broke out around the ports of **Philadelphia and New York in 1843**, spread through eastern American potato fields, and reached Europe by 1845 — where it destroyed the potato crop of an Ireland dependent on it (the Great Famine, 1845–52, about 1 million dead). American farmers lost crops too but ate other food; Ireland's poor had nothing else. The famine emigration is `immigration`'s story. (Sources: Clemson HGIC "Biogeography of the Irish Potato Famine"; *Scientific Reports* 2024 text-analytics reconstruction; Irish Times coverage of the 2024 mapping study; Britannica "Great Famine.")

## Era 07 — 1850–1900: homesteads, cattle, and the beef machine

- **The Homestead Act, May 20, 1862**: 160 acres of surveyed public land to anyone (including women and, after 1868, Black Americans; not yet most Native people) who lived on it and farmed it five years. Over the law's life: about **4 million claims filed, about 1.6 million patents earned, about 270 million acres — near 10 percent of U.S. land** — transferred. Roughly half of claimants failed to prove up. (Sources: National Archives "Homestead Act of 1862"; NPS Homestead National Historical Park; Britannica. The free-land politics is `economy`'s; the settlers' journeys `migration`'s; ours is the farm and the food year.)
- **The Oblinger family — the documented homesteader food year.** **Uriah Oblinger** left Indiana and filed a Nebraska homestead claim (Fillmore County) in 1872; his wife **Mattie** and baby daughter Ella joined him in May 1873. The family's **~3,000 pages of letters (1862–1912, Nebraska State Historical Society; digitized by the Library of Congress as "Prairie Settlement")** describe the food life in detail: building the sod house, planting corn and garden, "we will all be poor here together," grasshopper years, the Easter blizzard of 1873, low crop prices. Mattie died in childbirth in 1880. One of the best-documented ordinary farm families in the country's archives. (Sources: LOC "About the Letters from the Uriah W. Oblinger Collection"; Nebraska State Historical Society RG1346.AM; History Matters (GMU) letter excerpts.)
- **Cattle drives.** About **5.2 million Texas cattle were driven north between 1866 and 1884** (U.S. Bureau of Statistics figure via the Texas State Historical Association); **Joseph McCoy** built the railhead market at **Abilene, Kansas, 1867**, and the Chisholm Trail carried herds to it. Cowboy demographics: in Texas **as many as one in four cowhands was Black**, and a large share were Mexican or Mexican-American — the LOC and Smithsonian both carry this. The drives ended as **barbed wire (Glidden patent, November 24, 1874)** and quarantine lines closed the open range. The business is `economy`'s (their bank already carries the $5→$40 price spread); the drovers as movers are `migration`'s; ours is beef as food and how it reached a city plate. (Sources: TSHA "Chisholm Trail"; Abilene/Kansas historical society pages; LOC "Black Cowboys at Home on the Range"; Smithsonian Magazine; National Archives Glidden patent lesson.)
- **Charles Goodnight invented the chuckwagon, 1866** — a surplus Army wagon rebuilt with a hinged pantry box that dropped flat into a work table: a rolling kitchen feeding a crew for months of trail (beans, bacon, biscuits, coffee; the cook ranked second only to the trail boss). (Sources: NPS "The Chuckwagon"; Texas Trail of Fame; Buffalo Bill Center of the West. No verified film about Goodnight — the *Lonesome Dove* characters are fiction partly inspired by him; that is an inspiration note, not a Movie line.)
- **Beef becomes an industry.** Chicago's **Union Stock Yards opened December 25, 1865**, and became the world's largest livestock market; **Gustavus Swift** shipped dressed beef east in refrigerated railcars — engineer **Andrew Chase designed the practical ice-cooled car for Swift in 1878** — so an animal could be raised in Texas, killed in Chicago, and eaten in New York. (Sources: Chicago Encyclopedia "Union Stock Yard"; Britannica "Gustavus Swift"; Wikipedia "Swift Refrigerator Line" cross-checked.)
- **Farmers organize: the Grange.** **Oliver Hudson Kelley**, a Minnesota farmer and Agriculture Department clerk, founded the Patrons of Husbandry (the Grange) in **1867**; it became the first great national farmers' organization — social halls, cooperative buying, and a political voice against railroad rates. The rate fights and farm-price politics are `economy`'s/`big-business`'s; ours is farmers joining together. (Sources: LOC exhibition "The Grange"; Minnesota Historical Society Kelley Farm; History.com "Oliver Kelley organizes the Grange" — journalism.)
- **George Washington Carver's story begins here** (born enslaved in Missouri c. 1864; Iowa State degrees 1894/1896; arrived Tuskegee October 1896) but his working life is the next era's — the story block sits in 1900–1950 per the boundary rule, with the origin told inside it.

## Era 08 — 1900–1950: the government reaches the plate

- **Upton Sinclair and *The Jungle*.** Sinclair spent about **seven weeks undercover** in Chicago's packinghouses (1904); *The Jungle* ran as a serial in 1905 and appeared as a book in **February 1906**. He wrote it to expose how immigrant packinghouse workers lived; readers fixed on what was in the meat. His own verdict, published later in 1906: **"I aimed at the public's heart, and by accident I hit it in the stomach."** On **June 30, 1906** Theodore Roosevelt signed BOTH the **Pure Food and Drug Act** (no more misbranded or adulterated food and medicine in interstate commerce — the law that grew into the FDA) and the **Meat Inspection Act** (federal inspectors in packing plants). (Sources: FDA history pages "Part I: The 1906 Food and Drugs Act"; USDA FSIS "Our History"; Britannica "Meat Inspection Act"; History.com — journalism — for the quote's common citation (Cosmopolitan, 1906). The law-as-law angle is `government-politics`'s; the packinghouse job is `work-workers`'s; what was in the food and what the law required is ours. No verified biopic of Sinclair — Movie line omitted.)
- **George Washington Carver — verified.** Born enslaved near Diamond, Missouri, about 1864 (his birth date is not known exactly — say so); first Black student and later faculty at Iowa State; ran Tuskegee Institute's agriculture program from **October 1896** until his death in 1943. Verified work: **crop rotation** — alternating soil-feeding legumes (peanuts, cowpeas, sweet potatoes) with soil-draining cotton — taught to poor Southern farmers through **44 free practical bulletins** and a mule-drawn demonstration wagon (the Jesup wagon); his most famous bulletin, *How to Grow the Peanut and 105 Ways of Preparing It for Human Consumption* (Bulletin 31 — first issued 1916; some sources give the 1917 printing; say "1916, reprinted for decades"), and hundreds of experimental peanut and sweet-potato products. **He did NOT invent peanut butter** — peanut pastes are ancient (Aztec), and the first U.S.-adjacent patents belong to Marcellus Gilmore Edson (1884, Canada) and John Harvey Kellogg (1895). State his real achievement: soil, self-reliance, and teaching. (Sources: USDA NAL Carver exhibit (the bulletins); Tuskegee University; American Chemical Society landmark; National Peanut Board and History.com on the peanut-butter myth.) **Movie:** *George Washington Carver: An Uncommon Life* (Iowa PBS documentary, premiered April 30, 2018) — verified via Iowa PBS.
- **Cecile Steele and the broiler chicken, 1923.** Ocean View, Delaware: Cecile Long Steele (1900–1940) ordered 50 chicks, was shipped **500** by mistake, raised them indoors and sold them at 16 weeks for 62 cents a pound — the accidental start of the **commercial broiler industry**. She ordered 1,000 on purpose in 1924, 10,000 by 1926; by 1928 some 500 Delmarva growers were raising broilers. Chicken's road from Sunday luxury to the country's cheapest everyday meat starts at her door. (Sources: Delaware Public Archives historical marker "Ocean View — Birthplace of the Commercial Broiler Industry"; Delaware Women's Hall of Fame; Wikipedia "Cecile Long Steele" cross-checked.)
- **Tractors replace horses.** 1920 farm census: **246,000 tractors** on U.S. farms — and about **26 million horses and mules** still doing most work (horse numbers peaked around 1919–20). Ford's **Fordson (1917)** made tractors cheap; International Harvester's **Farmall (1924)** could also cultivate row crops, removing the last job that kept horses. Every horse retired freed roughly five acres of feed land to grow human food. The machines are `technology`'s; the changed farm — bigger acreage, fewer hands, no oat fields — is ours. (Sources: Wessels Living History Farm "Horses Lose Their Jobs"; Farm Collector centennial pieces; NBER tractor-diffusion paper w7947.) **Henry A. Wallace founded Pioneer Hi-Bred, the first hybrid seed-corn company, in 1926** (Farm Progress; Wallace Centers of Iowa) — the seed revolution that pays off next era.
- **The Dust Bowl from the farm.** WWI wheat prices drove the plow-up of the southern Plains grasslands; drought from 1931 killed the wheat, and the exposed soil blew (Black Sunday, April 14, 1935 — the storms' fullest telling and soil science are `land-environment`'s; the exodus `migration`'s). Ours: crops failed year after year, and farm families lived on garden truck, canned goods, government commodities, and jackrabbits. (Framing carried from the verified `land-environment` bank; Ken Burns *The Dust Bowl* (2012) is that chapter's topic documentary.)
- **New Deal farm programs — what they did to food.** The **Agricultural Adjustment Act (May 1933)** paid farmers to cut production to raise prices: about **10 million acres of already-planted cotton were plowed under in 1933**, and about **6 million pigs were bought and slaughtered** — while people stood in breadlines. The public anger at that contradiction produced the **Federal Surplus Relief Corporation** (surplus food to relief families) and, in **1939, the first Food Stamp Program** (launched May 16, 1939, Rochester, New York; first buyer Mabel McFiggin; orange stamps bought food, bonus blue stamps took surplus). The AAA was struck down in 1936 and rewritten in 1938; paying farmers around production became permanent policy. The economics are `economy`'s; the programs as law `government-politics`'s; what got grown and who got fed is ours. (Sources: Britannica "Agricultural Adjustment Act"; Living New Deal; USDA FNS "A Short History of SNAP"; EBSCO "First U.S. Food Stamp Program Begins.")
- **School lunches.** Local and charity school-meal programs grew for decades; Depression surplus buying fed them; WWII draft rejections for malnutrition pushed Congress; the **National School Lunch Act was signed by President Truman on June 4, 1946**, making federal school lunch permanent. (Sources: Truman Library signing statement; the Act's text (Richard B. Russell NSLA); Georgia Encyclopedia exhibition "The Story of School Lunch.")
- **Victory gardens and rationing.** WWII home-front food: sugar was rationed first (**May 1942**), coffee (November 1942), then meat, cheese, fats, and canned goods (**March 1943**) under a points system with ration books for every person, babies included. Americans planted about **20 million victory gardens**, which by 1943–44 grew **about 40 percent of the country's fresh vegetables** (roughly 9–10 million tons). (Sources: National WWII Museum "Victory Gardens" and "Rationing"; NPS "Food Rationing on the WWII Home Front"; History.com — journalism.) **Ordinary-story slot: target** — a documented ration-book household or victory gardener from an archived oral history (leads: National WWII Museum oral histories; Smithsonian Victory Garden materials; LOC American Folklife Center). Not yet named — left as target rather than half-verified.
- **Cold and self-service food.** **Clarence Birdseye's** flash-frozen foods first went on retail sale **March 6, 1930, in Springfield, Massachusetts** (18 stores). The first supermarket by the Smithsonian's criteria — **King Kullen, Queens, New York, opened August 4, 1930** — is `marketplace`'s story (parked there); ours is the one-line arrival of frozen food in kitchens. (Sources: Mass Moments; National Inventors Hall of Fame "Clarence Birdseye"; King Kullen corporate history/Smithsonian recognition.)
- **Territories thread.** Hawaii (annexed 1898): sugar plantations dominated the islands' economy, and **James Dole founded the Hawaiian Pineapple Company in 1901**, canning pineapple at industrial scale — American-plate fruit grown on Pacific territory; the plantation workers' arrivals are `immigration`'s, the islands' politics `america-world`'s. (Sources: Britannica Kids "James Drummond Dole"; University of Hawaii scholarship on Dole; Jamaica Plain Historical Society biography.)

## Era 09 — 1950–2000: cheaper, faster, farther from the farm

- **The crossover.** The **1954 Census of Agriculture was the first in which tractors outnumbered horses and mules** on U.S. farms (about 4.6 million tractors; horses/mules had fallen from ~25–26 million in 1920 to ~4 million). Farm numbers fell from the 1935 peak of **6.8 million** toward 2 million; farms grew bigger, more specialized, more chemical. **Hybrid corn** (from Wallace's 1926 Pioneer on) covered nearly all corn acres by the 1950s; corn yields that had sat near 25–30 bushels an acre for a century climbed past 100 by the 1970s (state as approximate — USDA/ERS series). (Sources: USDA Census of Agriculture via Farm Collector/AgWeb accounts of the 1954 flip; ERS farm-numbers series; Farm Progress hybrid-corn history.)
- **Norman Borlaug and the Green Revolution — verified.** Iowa-born agronomist; from **1944** he bred wheat in Mexico for the Rockefeller Foundation program, crossing thousands of strains to make short-strawed, disease-resistant, high-yield **semi-dwarf wheat**; Mexico became self-sufficient in wheat by 1956; his varieties broke famine forecasts in India and Pakistan in the 1960s; **Nobel Peace Prize, 1970** — the only agricultural scientist so honored. He is **often credited with saving as many as a billion lives — repeat that as the common estimate, not a count.** The same package (dwarf grains + fertilizer + irrigation) is also criticized for chemical dependence and lost crop diversity — both truths stated. (Sources: NobelPrize.org; World Food Prize biography; Britannica.) **Movie:** *The Man Who Tried to Feed the World* (PBS American Experience, 2020) — verified.
- **Fast food — the McDonald's chronology, verified.** Brothers **Richard and Maurice McDonald** opened their San Bernardino, California drive-in in **1940**; in **1948** they rebuilt it around the assembly-line **"Speedee Service System"** — a short menu of 15-cent hamburgers, fries, and shakes, no carhops, food in seconds. Milkshake-machine salesman **Ray Kroc** visited in 1954, became their franchise agent, opened his first McDonald's in **Des Plaines, Illinois, April 15, 1955**, and **bought the brothers out in 1961 for $2.7 million**. The handshake promise of royalties the brothers said they were owed, and Kroc's opening of a store to squeeze their original stand, are part of the record — the film dramatizes them. (Sources: Britannica "McDonald's"; History of McDonald's references; PBS SoCal "The Real McDonald's.") **Movie:** *The Founder* (2016) — verified (Michael Keaton as Kroc; the McDonald brothers' story).
- **The TV dinner — tell the dispute.** Swanson's aluminum-tray frozen turkey dinner went on sale in **1953** (company lore ties it to a surplus of Thanksgiving turkeys); it sold for about 98 cents, and Swanson sold **over 10 million in 1954**, 25 million the next year. **Who invented it is genuinely disputed:** salesman **Gerry Thomas** claimed the idea for decades and was widely celebrated for it; relatives of company owners **Gilbert and Clarke Swanson** — and Swanson bacteriologist Betty Cronin — said the brothers developed it in-house and Thomas took credit. Earlier frozen meals (Maxson's 1945 airline "Strato-Plates") predate them all. State all of it. (Sources: Smithsonian Magazine "A Brief History of the TV Dinner"; History.com "Who Invented the TV Dinner?"; LOC Everyday Mysteries.)
- **Farmworkers.** The people picking the cheap food organized: Cesar Chavez, Dolores Huerta, the Delano grape strike and United Farm Workers — **`work-workers` leads and carries their story (slug chavez-huerta)**; this chapter takes one sentence at the table's edge.
- **The 1980s farm crisis — verified numbers.** After the 1970s export boom ("plant fencerow to fencerow") came high interest rates and falling land: **farmland values fell as much as 60 percent in parts of the Midwest between 1981 and the mid-1980s**; farm debt reached about **$215 billion by 1984** (double 1978); an estimated **300,000 farms defaulted** during the decade and about **300 rural banks failed**. **Farm Aid, September 22, 1985** (Champaign, Illinois): Willie Nelson, John Mellencamp, and Neil Young played to about 80,000 people and raised over $7 million for farm families — and Farm Aid continues today. The finance mechanics are `economy`'s; the lost family tables are ours. (Sources: 1980s-farm-crisis summaries citing USDA/Fed data (MNopedia; Iowa PBS; agricultural press); Farm Aid organizational history.)
- **The Jordan family — the documented farm loss.** Russel and Mary Jane Jordan farmed land near Rolfe, Iowa, that had been in the family 125 years; carrying about $220,000 of debt out of the crisis years, their bank called their note, and in the mid-1990s they sold cattle, machinery, and household goods at auction to pay it off — filmed by their daughter Jeanne Jordan and Steven Ascher. (Sources: PBS American Experience film page; Wikipedia/IMDb cross-check; Deseret News review.) **Movie:** *Troublesome Creek: A Midwestern* (1995; Sundance Grand Jury Prize and Audience Award 1996; Academy Award nominee) — the film IS the documentation; note it is about the family.
- **Organic — from fringe to law.** J.I. Rodale began *Organic Farming and Gardening* magazine in **1942** (Emmaus, Pennsylvania) and spent decades promoting compost-and-soil farming; the **Organic Foods Production Act (1990)** ordered national standards, and the **USDA organic seal took effect October 21, 2002**. (Sources: Rodale Institute timeline; Congress.gov S.2108 (101st); USDA AMS organic-seal pages.)
- **Alice Waters.** Opened **Chez Panisse** in Berkeley, California, **1971**, built around fresh, local, seasonal, organically grown food — a founding restaurant of "California cuisine" and the **farm-to-table** idea; founded the **Edible Schoolyard** at Berkeley's Martin Luther King Jr. Middle School in **1995** (children grow, cook, and eat their own food). (Sources: Smithsonian Magazine 50th-anniversary piece; Edible Schoolyard Project "About"; Britannica/Wikipedia cross-check.) **Movie:** *Alice Waters and Her Delicious Revolution* (PBS American Masters, 2003) — verified via IMDb/PBS.

## Era 10 — 2000–today: arguing about food

- **Fewer, bigger farms.** The 2022 Census of Agriculture counted **1.9 million farms — below 2 million for the first time since before the Civil War** — down 7 percent from 2017, versus the 1935 peak of 6.8 million. (Sources: USDA ERS charts on the 2022 Census; USDA NASS.)
- **Eating and health, plainly.** CDC: adult obesity rose from 30.5 percent (1999–2000) to **41.9 percent (2017–March 2020)**, easing slightly to **40.3 percent (August 2021–August 2023)**. Diet-linked illness (type 2 diabetes, heart disease) is a leading health burden — `health` leads on the medicine; the food is ours. (Sources: CDC Adult Obesity Facts; NCHS Data Brief 508.)
- **Food deserts — the USDA definition, verified.** USDA maps "low-income, low-access" census tracts: low-income tracts where at least 500 people or a third of residents live **more than 1 mile from a supermarket in cities, or more than 10 miles in rural areas**. By that 1-mile/10-mile measure, USDA's 2019 data put **about 19 million people — about 6 percent of the population — in low-income, low-access tracts** (earlier, broader measures gave larger figures — 39.4 million in 2015 — because the yardsticks differ; state that the number depends on the measure). (Sources: CRS In Focus IF11841 "Defining Low-Income, Low-Access Food Areas"; USDA ERS Food Access Research Atlas.)
- **Farmers' markets boom, then plateau.** USDA count: **1,755 markets in 1994 → 8,771 in 2019**, with growth near 7 percent a year until about 2011, then flattening. (Source: USDA ERS chart of note.)
- **GMOs — both truths.** More than **90 percent of U.S. corn, soybeans, and cotton** are genetically engineered. The **National Academies of Sciences 2016 report**, reviewing about 1,000 studies, "could not find persuasive evidence of adverse health effects directly attributable to consumption" of GE foods — the scientific consensus is that approved GE crops are as safe as conventional ones. Public controversy continued anyway, and since **January 2022** federal law requires "bioengineered" labeling on foods with detectable modified DNA. First GE food on sale: the Flavr Savr tomato, 1994 (industry histories; label as background). (Sources: NAS *Genetically Engineered Crops: Experiences and Prospects* (2016); USDA AMS National Bioengineered Food Disclosure Standard; UConn Extension GMO pages.)
- **New foods.** The **Impossible Burger debuted in 2016**; **Beyond Meat's 2019 IPO** was the year's biggest first-day gain; **June 2023**: USDA gave UPSIDE Foods and GOOD Meat the first U.S. approvals to sell **cell-cultivated ("lab-grown") chicken**. **Vertical farming** boomed on investment and stumbled — pioneer **AeroFarms filed Chapter 11 in June 2023** (and later restructured to profitability) — an honest boom-and-struggle story, not a triumph story. (Sources: SEC/news coverage of Beyond IPO; Forbes/NBC/Time on the June 2023 cultivated-meat approvals; CoStar/Fast Company on AeroFarms — business journalism, labeled.)
- **COVID-19 jolt (2020).** Meatpacking plants became infection hot spots and closed; milk was dumped and crops plowed under while food-bank lines stretched for miles — widely documented in 2020 news reporting (label journalism; one line only).
- **Farm labor today — verified numbers.** The **H-2A** seasonal farmworker visa grew from about **48,000 certified positions in FY2005 to about 372,000 in FY2022 and ~385,000 in FY2024** — around **15 percent of U.S. crop-farm employment**; over 90 percent of H-2A workers come from Mexico. Most hired crop workers are foreign-born (USDA ERS/NAWS). (Sources: USDA ERS Farm Labor and H-2A charts; American Farm Bureau; UC Davis Rural Migration News.)
- **Lucas Benitez — the farmworker story.** Born in Guerrero, Mexico; came to **Immokalee, Florida at 17** and picked tomatoes; co-founded the **Coalition of Immokalee Workers in 1993**; the CIW's **Fair Food Program** (agreement with Florida tomato growers announced November 2010, program running from 2011) gets buyers like McDonald's and Walmart to pay a premium and enforce a code of conduct — pay floors, shade, water, protection from abuse — verified by audits. Wallenberg Medal 2023. (Sources: CIW; University of Michigan Wallenberg Medal; RFK Human Rights; Bill Moyers profile — advocacy/journalism mix, labeled.) **Movie:** *Food Chains* (2014, produced with Eric Schlosser) — about the CIW as a group; Benitez appears.
- **Native food sovereignty — continuity, not a museum piece.** Tribes run bison herds, seed banks, and traditional-food programs; Native chefs and seed keepers are rebuilding Indigenous cuisines. **Nephi Craig** (White Mountain Apache/Diné), chef, opened a café centered on Apache foods and founded the Native American Culinary Association; featured with forager **Twila Cassadore** (San Carlos Apache) and student scientist **Elsie DuBray** (Cheyenne River Sioux) in the documentary ***Gather* (2020)** — a NYT Critic's Pick. `native-nations` leads on sovereignty as sovereignty; the food and farming is shared ground. (Sources: Gather film site; Wikipedia "Gather (film)" cross-checked; Yes! Magazine and Native Business coverage — journalism.)
