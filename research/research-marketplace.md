## 1. Before 1500

### The Dalles / Celilo Falls — the documented trade gathering
- The ten-mile reach of the Columbia between Celilo Falls and The Dalles was the largest trading place in the Northwest and among the largest in western North America. Traders came from British Columbia to California and, by some accounts, from as far as the Great Lakes. (Oregon Historical Society, *The Columbia River Trade Network*; Confederated Tribes materials.)
- Goods recorded: coiled baskets, skins, and berries from the north; obsidian, bows, and captives from the south; horses, camas, and buffalo hides from the east; wapato, whale oil, and dentalium shells from the west. Dentalium shells passed so widely they served as a common measure of value. (Oregon Historical Society.)
- Lewis and Clark reached the reach in October 1805 and Clark's journal calls it the "Great Mart of all this Country." **Boundary note:** the quote is 1805 — the outline states plainly that the trading long predates the record. (Clark's journal via Oregon Historical Society; lewis-clark.org, "Celilo Falls.")
- Archaeological occupation: commonly given as at least 10,000 years, with some accounts saying 15,000. **State the range** — the higher figure appears mostly in journalism and museum copy. (Oregon Historical Society; photographic-collection essays — the 15,000 figure is journalism, labeled.)
- Celilo Falls was drowned by The Dalles Dam in **1957**. That loss is `native-nations`' story; this chapter needs only the trade.

### Goods that traveled without a store
- Lake Superior copper, Gulf shells, western obsidian, and Southwestern turquoise all found hundreds of miles from source. Cahokia (peak c. 1050–1150) controlled Mill Creek chert hoe blades found more than 400 miles away. (Illinois State Museum; Cahokia Mounds State Historic Site — verified in `research/research-economy.md`.)
- **Keep-out check:** `economy` leads Cahokia as an economy, `native-nations` as a city, `money` leads wampum and shell as currency. This chapter takes only the point that a person could obtain distant goods with no market or price system.
- **No named individual trader is documented before 1500 from this angle.** The outline says so and adds no story.

## 2. The 1500s

### What Native customers actually chose (the discerning-customer point)
- Copper and brass kettles were among the most sought European goods in the Northeast and Great Lakes in the later 1500s; Basque, French, and Dutch traders carried them because buyers asked for them. (Ehrhardt, "Colonial Encounters, European Kettles, and the Magic of Mimesis…", *International Journal of Historical Archaeology*, 2011.)
- Kettles were frequently **not used as kettles**. Excavated examples were cut into sheet and reworked into beads, pendants, tubes, and projectile points — forms long made from native copper. (Ehrhardt; Ehrhardt, "Metal-forming practices among the seventeenth century Illinois, 1640–1682"; Museum of Ontario Archaeology, metal artifacts identification guide.)
- Basque banded copper kettles and their distinctive fragments are found from the Canadian Maritimes to the lower Great Lakes, dating to the late 1500s and early 1600s — evidence that Native traders redistributed the metal far inland. (Fitzgerald et al., "Late sixteenth-century Basque banded copper kettles," *Historical Archaeology*; Turgeon's Basque-trade work.)
- Other steady wants: woolen cloth, linen shirts, glass beads, iron knives, awls, axes, fish hooks, blankets, guns and powder. (Minnesota Historical Society, "The Fur Trade"; North Dakota Studies, "Indians and the Fur Trade.")
- **Selectivity, documented:** the Hudson's Bay Company sent gunsmiths to its posts to assess quality and repair firearms in response to Native customers' demands. Historians describe Native traders as effective bargainers who demanded practical goods and luxuries alike. (Bill of Rights Institute, "The Fur Trade"; EH.net, "The Economic History of the Fur Trade: 1670 to 1870.") **HBC dates from 1670 — cited here for the pattern, not for the 1500s.** The outline places the gunsmith detail as "later," not in the 1500s.
- **Keep-out:** the July 1534 Chaleur Bay exchange (Mi'kmaq holding furs up on sticks to Cartier's crew) is `economy`'s era span — verified in `research/research-economy.md` from the Canadian Museum of History. Not repeated here.

## 3. The 1600s

### Market days
- **Boston, 1634**, by order of Governor John Winthrop — the first officially recorded European-style public market in the English colonies. Then **Hartford 1643**, **New York City by 1686**, **Philadelphia 1693**. (City of Boston, "Part 1: Brief History of Farmers Markets in the United States"; also Lancaster Farmland Trust summary.) **[VERIFY cleared]**
- Markets existed because town dwellers could not travel to farms for meat, dairy, and produce. (Same sources.)

### Trading posts
- **Aptucxet, 1627** — Plymouth Colony's trading post on the Manomet River, upper Cape Cod, about 20 miles from the colony, built for trade with the Wampanoag (corn and beans) and with Dutch traders. Wampanoag name meaning "little trap in the river." (Bourne Historical Society, The Museums at Aptucxet; Plymouth Archaeological Rediscovery Project.)
- **Isaack de Rasieres**, secretary to the Dutch colony, brought wampum into Plymouth's trade in 1627; Bradford confirmed the Dutch introduced it. (Cape Cod Life history of wampum; Bourne Historical Society.) **Keep-out:** wampum as money is `money`'s; wampum's meaning to the nations is `native-nations`'.

### The ledger and book credit
- Colonial merchants kept a **journal** (chronological sales and purchases) and a **ledger** (one account per customer; purchases debited on the left page, payments credited on the right). (*Accounting Historians Journal*, "Accounting in Colonial America: Observations on Money, Barter and Bookkeeping.")
- **Book credit** was the dominant domestic credit form, alongside promissory notes; credit was usually extended for no more than a year. Payment came in commodities, personal services, and cash. (EH.net, "Credit in the Colonial American Economy.")
- Account books are the primary evidence for what ordinary people bought. (Ian Brabner, "Daily Records: 18th & 19th Century Manuscript Account Books"; East Hampton Library's published **Robert Townsend account books, 1773–1785**.)
- **Story gap, honest:** no *1600s* named ordinary customer was verified this pass. Outline carries a `target` with the research route (state historical societies, American Antiquarian Society, published daybook transcriptions). Do not invent.

## 4. 1700 to 1750

### The consumer revolution
- Historians' term for the eighteenth-century rise in colonial purchases of British manufactured goods, driven by improvements in manufacturing, transport, and credit. Colonists shifted from making tools, clothes, and utensils to buying them; items moved from luxury to commonplace as incomes rose and prices fell. (Khan Academy, "The consumer revolution"; Lumen Learning / OpenStax *U.S. History*, "The Consumer Revolution"; Library of Congress research guide, "Colonies in America: Commerce, Business, and the Economy — Consumers.")
- Tea became "the drink of the Empire," carrying a whole set of goods (pot, cups, table, sugar). Spending on consumer goods became a marker of respectability. (Same sources.)
- **T. H. Breen** is the historian most associated with this argument (*The Marketplace of Revolution*), but the general-audience sources above do not cite him directly; if his name is used in prose, cite the book itself.

### Shops and shopkeepers
- **Elizabeth Murray** came to Boston from Scotland in **1749 at age 23** and set up a shop selling imported cloth and dry goods, backed by her brother's mercantile connections; she traveled to London to select stock and became self-supporting. She helped other women set up shops of their own. (Boston Women's Heritage Trail; The Elizabeth Murray Project, California State University Long Beach; Patricia Cleary, *Elizabeth Murray: A Woman's Pursuit of Independence in Eighteenth-Century America*, UMass Press.)

### Newspaper advertising begins to pay
- Franklin bought the *Pennsylvania Gazette* from Samuel Keimer on **October 2, 1729**. (American Antiquarian Society, *The News Media and the Making of America, 1730–1865*; Library of Congress item record for the Gazette.)
- Under Franklin, advertising became a major revenue source; he used more white space and varied type sizes; the paper expanded **from two pages to four** partly to fit commercial notices. Historians attribute much of his wealth and early retirement (1748) to advertising revenue. (American Antiquarian Society; Benjamin Franklin Historical Society; Franklin's *Autobiography*.)
- **Hard part, included:** Gazette advertisements included notices for runaway servants and for the sale of enslaved people. (Standard in the Gazette's run; the Adverts 250 Project documents colonial advertising practice.)
- **Movie:** *Benjamin Franklin* (2022, Ken Burns, PBS) — already verified in `research/research-money.md` and used in `outlines/money.md`, `science.md`, `technology.md`, `work-workers.md`. Covers the whole life.
- **Keep-out:** `news-communication` leads the press, printers, Zenger, and the post. This chapter leads advertising as selling.

## 5. 1750 to 1800

### Non-importation
- **Boston Non-Importation Agreement, August 1, 1768** — about **sixty** merchants and traders signed; within two weeks all but **sixteen** of Boston's merchants had joined. It barred importing most British goods from **January 1, 1769 to January 1, 1770**, in protest at the Townshend Revenue Act of 1767. (Massachusetts Historical Society, "Coming of the American Revolution: Non-consumption and Non-importation"; Avalon Project text; American History Central.)
- **Effect, measured:** the value of British goods imported into Boston fell from about **£420,000 in 1768 to about £208,000 in 1769** — roughly half. (American History Central; Massachusetts Historical Society.) Some summaries state the halving as a colonies-wide figure; the £420,000/£208,000 pair is specifically Boston. **Use it as Boston.**
- Repealed after Parliament withdrew most Townshend duties in 1770.

### Spinning bees / homespun
- At least **46 public spinning meetings in New England, 1768–1770, involving 1,644 women**; ministers hosted **30** of them and those drew 94 percent of participants. A broader count gives **more than 60 spinning meetings over 32 months from March 1768**, from Harpswell, Maine to Huntington, Long Island. (Museum of the American Revolution, "Age of Homespun"; American Battlefield Trust, "The Daughters of Liberty"; the underlying scholarship is Laurel Thatcher Ulrich's *The Age of Homespun*.) **The two counts measure different things (New England only vs. a wider region and window) — state whichever is used.**

### The Cuming sisters — VERIFIED ordinary story
Source: **National Park Service, "Cuming Sisters: 'She-Merchants' of Boston"** (nps.gov/articles/000/cuming-sisters.htm), read in full 2026-08-08.
- **Ame (Amy) and Elizabeth Cuming**, unmarried sisters in their mid-30s, raised in Concord by Scottish parents, relocated to Boston by **1765**; financially independent shopkeepers importing fashionable wares from London; also taught embroidery and boarded young ladies. Elizabeth Murray helped set them up.
- Late **1769**: a **£300** shipment of wrought silk, sewing silk, and haberdashery arrived from London. **Mid-November 1769**: men from the Merchants' Committee visited and warned of "consequences" for selling goods "contrary to the [agreement] of the Merchants."
- The sisters refused, saying "We have never [entered] into [any] agreement not to import," and described themselves as "two industrious Girls who [were] Striving in an honest way to [get their] Bread."
- The **Boston Gazette** denounced them as "enemies to their country" who "preferred their own little private Advantage to the Welfare of America."
- **March 19, 1770**: Boston's Town Meeting voted to record their names so "POSTERITY may know" they "opposed their Country in a struggle for the Rights of the Constitution."
- They left Boston with Loyalist refugees in **March 1776** for Halifax, Nova Scotia; by **1780** they were "more prosperous than ever they were in Boston."
- **Why this is the right story:** it tells the boycott from the shop counter, which no other chapter does, and it does not flatten the Revolution into a single side.

### The country store and crop credit
- One store served a rural neighborhood; payment in crops, labor, and promises; the ledger carried the balance until harvest; a bad year carried debt forward. (EH.net, "Credit in the Colonial American Economy"; account-book scholarship above.)
- **Documented example of surviving books:** Robert Townsend's account books, Oyster Bay and New York, **1773–1785**, transcribed and published by the East Hampton Library.

## 6. 1800 to 1850

### Peddlers
- Peddlers carried tinware, clocks, pins, needles, combs, books, and patent medicines on foot and by wagon through country districts; Connecticut tin peddlers are the archetype. (*Journal of Antiques and Collectibles*, "The Tin Peddler"; Gregory LeFever, "Era of the American Peddler.")
- **James Guild**, of Tunbridge, Vermont, set out peddling in **1818** with a small trunk of goods and kept a journal through **1824**, in which he moved from peddling to tinkering, schoolmastering, portrait painting, and finally London. Published by the **Vermont Historical Society** as *From Tunbridge, Vermont, to London, England: The Journal of James Guild, Peddler, Tinker, Schoolmaster, Portrait Painter, from 1818 to 1824* (also catalogued in WorldCat; excerpted in *American Heritage*, April 1956, "Pack-Road to Yesterday").
- **Open item:** the full PDF of the journal (vermonthistory.org) is a scanned image file and could not be text-read this pass. **Do not quote Guild or state earnings figures until the printed journal is read.** The outline says so explicitly.

### The first department store
- **A. T. Stewart's "Marble Palace," 280 Broadway, New York, opened September 21, 1846** (built 1845–46, Tuckahoe marble). Retail across multiple floors — previously retail was ground floor only, with upper floors for wholesale or living quarters. Stewart set standard prices to prevent haggling and used sales to draw women customers. Building still stands (the Sun Building). (Village Preservation, "The 'Marble Palace'"; Untapped New York; Smithsonian Libraries blog on Alexander Turney Stewart; Visualizing 19th-Century New York, "Palaces of Consumption.")
- **Dispute, stated honestly:** Stewart's is widely called the first American department store; Wanamaker's Grand Depot (1876) and Macy's are also called firsts, and definitions of "department store" differ. The outline calls it a claim and names the competing claims.

### Selling by spectacle — P. T. Barnum
- **Joice Heth, 1835**: Barnum exhibited an enslaved, blind, elderly Black woman, advertising her as the **161-year-old** former nurse of George Washington. When interest fell he planted a rumor that she was a mechanical automaton, and audiences returned to check. She died in **1836**; a public post-mortem, to which Barnum sold tickets, concluded she was no more than about **80**. (hoaxes.org archive, "The Hoaxes of P.T. Barnum"; *Smithsonian Magazine*, "P.T. Barnum Isn't the Hero the 'Greatest Showman' Wants You to Think.")
- **Feejee Mermaid, 1842**: exhibited at Barnum's American Museum in New York, advertised with images of a beautiful bare-breasted creature; the object was a monkey's head and torso sewn to a fish tail. Ran about a month in New York with large crowds. (hoaxes.org; Live Science, "The Feejee Mermaid.")
- Barnum called himself the "Prince of Humbug." (*Smithsonian Magazine*.)
- **Movie:** ***The Circus*** (PBS *American Experience*, Season 30, two parts, aired **October 8–9, 2018**; written/directed/produced by Sharon Grimberg). Covers the American circus from the late 1700s to 1956 and treats Barnum substantially — **a documentary about the topic that features him, not a biography of him.** (PBS American Experience official site; IMDb.)
- ***The Greatest Showman*** (2017) is a real film but a musical that invents most of its story and omits Joice Heth. **Do not use it as the Movie line.** The outline names it only to warn.

## 7. 1850 to 1900

### Department stores
- **R. H. Macy**, Sixth Avenue at 14th Street, New York, opened **October 28, 1858**. First-day sales given as **$11.06** in most accounts and **$11.08** in others; first-year gross about **$85,000**. **State the discrepancy.** The red star came from Macy's own tattoo; the 1858 emblem was a rooster and the star is dated to about 1862. (Macy's corporate and company histories; CBS News "Almanac"; Britannica.)
- **John Wanamaker**: opened a menswear store with brother-in-law Nathan Brown in Philadelphia in **1861**; opened the **Grand Depot** in a former Pennsylvania Railroad freight building at 13th and Market in **1876**.
- Wanamaker is **credited with popularizing** the money-back guarantee, the one-price policy, and price tags on goods. Sources say "credited with," "one of the first," and "popularized" — **not** an undisputed first, and A. T. Stewart had fixed prices in 1846. (Wanamaker company/museum histories; Vintage Fashion Guild; Philadelphia historical collections.)
- Wanamaker was **U.S. Postmaster General 1889–1893** and pushed rural free delivery — which then carried his mail-order competitors' catalogs. (USPS history; Wanamaker biographies.)
- **No documentary film about Wanamaker found.** Movie attribute left empty.

### Mail order
- **Aaron Montgomery Ward**: first catalog mailed **August 18, 1872** — a **single sheet**, **163 items**. Late 1872 the **Illinois Grange** named him its purchasing agent. **1875** catalog carried "satisfaction guaranteed or your money back." Grew to **32 pages in 1874** and **152 pages with about 3,000 items in 1876**. (Chicago History Museum, "Montgomery Ward's First Catalog"; Britannica Money; Encyclopedia.com; Montgomery Ward company history.)
- **Richard Warren Sears**: railroad station agent at **North Redwood, Minnesota**; bought an unclaimed shipment of watches in **1886** and sold them at a profit to other agents; expanded into jewelry and moved to Chicago. With watchmaker **Alvah C. Roebuck**, the firm became **Sears, Roebuck and Company in 1893**; that year's catalog ran **196 pages**. Sears retired in **1908 at 44**. (Britannica Money, "Richard W. Sears"; Encyclopedia.com; Chicagology.)
- **Catalog scale (peak, approximate):** the PBS documentary gives about **1,500 pages, 6 pounds, 100,000 products, 20 million recipients** at its height. Treat as approximate — company records and retellings differ. (PBS *American Experience*, *Mr. Sears' Catalogue*, 1989; Video Librarian review.)
- **"Wish Book"** is specifically the **Christmas catalog**, first published **1933**, officially titled *Christmas Wish Book* in **1968** — **not** the general catalog. Do not call the 1890s general catalog the wish book. (Sears Wish Book histories.)
- **Rural free delivery began October 1, 1896** on routes from **Charles Town, Halltown, and Uvilla, West Virginia** (Postmaster General William L. Wilson's home state). **44 routes in 29 states within a year.** County-wide experiment in Carroll County, Maryland, ordered December 1899. (U.S. Postal Service, "Rural Free Delivery"; e-WV: The West Virginia Encyclopedia.) **[VERIFY cleared: 1896 confirmed.]**
- **Movie:** ***Mr. Sears' Catalogue*** (PBS *American Experience*), directed by Edward Gray and Kenneth Levis, **aired November 14, 1989**. About Richard Sears and the catalog directly. (IMDb; PBS episode lists; Video Librarian.)

### Chains, brands, advertising
- **A&P**: partnership of **George Gilman and George Huntington Hartford, 1859**, New York (as Gilman & Company, retail tea and coffee). **70 stores by 1878**, nearly **200 by 1900**, **1,600 by 1915** after the 1912 "economy store" format. (A&P company histories; Encyclopedia.com; FundingUniverse.)
- **F. W. Woolworth**: five-cent store, **Utica, New York, February 22, 1879** — succeeded about two weeks then failed. Second store, **Lancaster, Pennsylvania, June 21, 1879** — succeeded. (Library of Congress, *This Month in Business History*: "Woolworth's Five and Dime"; Woolworths Museum, "The (not so) Great 5¢ Store in Utica.")
- **N. W. Ayer & Son**: founded **1869 in Philadelphia** by **Francis Wayland Ayer**, then 21, with **$250** from his father Nathan Wheeler Ayer, whose name the firm took. Generally called the first U.S. advertising agency; the firm's "open contract" put it on the advertiser's side by disclosing what publishers charged. Records held at the **Smithsonian National Museum of American History** (NMAH.AC.0059). (Smithsonian SOVA record; Historical Society of Pennsylvania; agency histories.)
- **"A Diamond Is Forever"** was written for De Beers by Ayer copywriter **Mary Frances Gerety**; sources date it **1947** and **1949** — **state the dispute**. *Ad Age* named it the most memorable slogan of the twentieth century in 1999. (Wikipedia/Ayer records; WBUR *Here & Now*, "The Ad-Woman Who Made Diamonds 'Forever'" — journalism, labeled.) **Era note: this is a 1900–1950 item; parked here with Ayer for continuity.**
- **Lydia E. Pinkham's Vegetable Compound**, sold from the mid-1870s: advertised as curing almost any "female complaint"; **18 to 20 percent alcohol by volume** depending on the source, with the label calling the alcohol a solvent and preservative. Her sons put her portrait in newspaper advertisements; she invited customers to write to her personally and used the correspondence to sell more. Cited before the 1906 Pure Food and Drug Act as an example of patent-medicine excess. (Smithsonian NMAH object records for the Compound and the Herb Medicine; Britannica; New England Historical Society; Organization of American Historians teaching exercise.) **Keep-out:** `health` leads what the medicines did and the 1906 law; `drugs-alcohol` leads the alcohol.

### Door-to-door — Persis Foster Eames Albee
- **David H. McConnell**, a former door-to-door bookseller, found the perfume he gave away as a premium outsold the books, and started the **California Perfume Company** (later Avon).
- In **1886** he hired **Persis Foster Eames Albee**, then **50**, of **Winchester, New Hampshire**, as his first General Traveling Agent — the first "Avon lady." She sold the **Little Dot Perfume Set** (violet, white rose, hyacinth, lily-of-the-valley, heliotrope) door to door across Cheshire County and then the Northeast, and recruited and trained other representatives.
- Frequently noted: this was **34 years before** the Nineteenth Amendment. (Avon corporate history, "135 Years of Avon" and the McConnell page; *Keene Sentinel* / *Sentinel Source* local history feature, "Winchester's P.F.E. Albee: the world's first Avon lady.")
- **Source caution:** Avon's own history is promotional. Verify any earnings or recruitment figures separately before printing them. No independent archival source located this pass.

## 8. 1900 to 1950

### Woolworth at national scale
- **November 12, 1911**: agreements signed merging four rival chains (Knox, Kirby, Charlton, C. S. Woolworth) with F. W. Woolworth; the consolidated **F. W. Woolworth Co.** had **596 stores** and **$65 million** in capital, the largest merchandising operation in the world. Sources split between 1911 (agreements) and 1912 (incorporation) — **state the pair**. (Library of Congress, *This Month in Business History*; Britannica; Encyclopedia.com.)
- **Woolworth Building**, New York, completed **1913**, **792 feet**, then the tallest building in the world, built at a cost of **$13.5 million paid personally in cash by Frank Woolworth**. (Library of Congress; Woolworth Building histories.) `landmarks` leads the building.
- **A&P peak**: **1930 — about 15,000 stores, $2.9 billion in sales**, the largest retailer in the world; largest U.S. grocery retailer 1915–1975 and largest U.S. retailer of any kind until 1965. (A&P company histories; Encyclopedia.com.)
- **Lunch counters**: Woolworth's seated lunch counters were segregated in the South, which is why the sit-in movement began at one. **Greensboro, February 1, 1960**: Ezell Blair Jr. (later Jibreel Khazan), Franklin McCain, Joseph McNeil, and David Richmond sat at the Woolworth counter and were refused service; the campaign grew to hundreds and the counter was desegregated **July 25, 1960**. A section of the counter is at the Smithsonian NMAH. (Library of Congress, African American Odyssey; Smithsonian NMAH; Britannica.) **`rights-movements` leads.** This chapter gives the counter's commercial origin only.

### Self-service and the supermarket
- **Piggly Wiggly**, first store **September 6, 1916**, **79 Jefferson Street, Memphis, Tennessee**, founded by **Clarence Saunders**. Patent for the "Self-Serving Store" **filed October 21, 1916**, **granted October 9, 1917**, **U.S. Patent 1,242,872**. Turnstile entrance; a fenced single path past every shelf to the checkout. (Tennessee Encyclopedia, "Clarence Saunders"; *Smithsonian Magazine*, "The Bizarre Story of Piggly Wiggly"; the patent record.) **[VERIFY cleared: 1916 confirmed; note the opening date is September 6, though some sources say September 11 — state September 6 with the discrepancy noted.]**
- **King Kullen**, **August 4, 1930**, Jamaica, Queens, New York; founder **Michael J. Cullen**, a former Kroger employee whose supermarket memo Kroger ignored. **The Smithsonian recognizes King Kullen as the first supermarket** on five criteria: separate departments, self-service, discount pricing, chain marketing, volume dealing. Slogan "Pile it high. Sell it low." (King Kullen corporate history citing the Smithsonian; Encyclopedia.com; Bowery Boys history — journalism, labeled.) **Parked item now used.**
- **Dispute, stated:** Piggly Wiggly (1916) is the self-service pioneer; King Kullen (1930) is the Smithsonian's "first supermarket." They are different claims and both are true as stated. Do not collapse them.
- Frozen-food retailing began **March 6, 1930**, Springfield, Massachusetts (Birdseye's 18-store test). The freezing technology is `technology`'s; the store case is this chapter's; the food is `food-farming`'s. (Mass Moments; NIHF.)

### Clarence Saunders and the 1923 corner
- Saunders listed Piggly Wiggly on the New York Stock Exchange. In early **1923** a group of franchised outlets in New York failed and short sellers raided the stock. Saunders borrowed about **$10 million** from Southern bankers, added his own money, and bought until he held orders for **196,000 of the 200,000 outstanding shares**. The Exchange then suspended trading and extended the short sellers' delivery deadline. Saunders was bankrupt and out of the company within months. (Tennessee Encyclopedia; *Smithsonian Magazine*; Finaeon, "The Piggly Crisis"; financial-history accounts — the corner narrative appears mostly in financial journalism; label it.)

### Installment buying
- By the end of the 1920s roughly **60 percent of automobiles** and about **80 percent of radios** were bought on installments; by 1930 most appliances, radios, and furniture and **more than two-thirds of all automobiles** were bought that way. (Encyclopedia.com, "Installment Buying, Selling, and Financing"; American RadioWorks, "The American Dream and Consumer Credit.") **The two figures come from different measures — give one, with its source.**
- Registered automobiles rose from about **9 million in 1920** to nearly **27 million by 1929**. (Encyclopedia.com.)
- **1924**: the National Association of Finance Companies adopted standards of **one-third down** on a new car, **two-fifths** on a used car, **twelve months maximum** term. (Encyclopedia.com.)

### Advertising enters the home
- **First paid radio commercial: August 28, 1922, WEAF, New York.** The **Queensboro Corporation** paid **$50** for about ten minutes promoting the Hawthorne Court apartments in Jackson Heights, Queens. (Saturday Evening Post, "Making Radio Pay"; NPR; Frequent Business Traveler; broadcast histories.)
- **First legal television commercial: July 1, 1941, WNBT, New York**, before a Brooklyn Dodgers–Philadelphia Phillies game at Ebbets Field. A Bulova watch face over a U.S. map, about ten seconds. Cost **$9** — **$4** air charges plus **$5** station charges. (Television Academy; Slate; Guinness World Records; Adweek.)
- `news-communication` leads radio and television as media.

### Shopping under rationing
- The **Office of Price Administration** ran rationing; every person including infants received a ration book. (National WWII Museum, "Ration Books"; NPS, "Food Rationing on the World War II Home Front.")
- **War Ration Book Two**, from **March 1, 1943**, introduced point rationing of processed foods. **Blue stamps** = processed foods (canned, dried, frozen). **Red stamps** = meat, cheese, fats, oils. Each person received **48 blue points and 64 red points per month**. (National WWII Museum; Oklahoma Historical Society; CSUN library.)
- At the counter a shopper handed over **both** stamps and money, and prices could not exceed OPA ceiling prices. Point values changed often and were published in newspapers, on the radio, and posted at local OPA boards. (National WWII Museum; NPS; Study.com summary of the coupon system.)
- Rationing overall is `food-farming`'s lead; this chapter's slice is the transaction at the counter.

### The catalog under Jim Crow
- Argument: in the segregated South, Black farm families were tied to white-owned country stores, often owned by their landlord, at prices the storekeeper set, and had to wait until white customers were served. Mail order let them pay national prices with no clerk deciding the order of service, and buy on the company's credit rather than the landlord's.
- Claimed also: Southern storekeepers organized bonfires to burn catalogs, and told white customers Sears was a Black-owned company to discourage them.
- **Attribution:** these claims come from **Louis Hyman**, historian at Cornell, in a widely circulated 2018 account, reported by **NPR** (October 16, 2018), the ***Washington Post*** (October 16, 2018), *America's Black Holocaust Museum* (November 10, 2018), and FEE. **This is journalism carrying a historian's argument, not an archival count — label it, and do not turn the burnings into a statistic.**

## 9. 1950 to 2000

### The enclosed mall
- **Southdale Center, Edina, Minnesota, opened October 8, 1956** — the first fully enclosed, climate-controlled shopping mall in the United States. **72 stores**, **two levels**, arranged around a brightly lighted central court, anchored by two department stores. Construction began 1954. (Minnesota Historical Society LibGuide, "Southdale Center: The First Indoor Shopping Mall"; Library of Congress, *This Month in Business History*; MNopedia; Gruen Associates project page.) **[VERIFY cleared: 1956 confirmed.]**
- Commissioned in **1952** by the **Dayton family**, Minneapolis department store merchants, from **Victor Gruen & Associates**. (Minnesota Historical Society; MNopedia.)
- **Victor Gruen**: Austrian Jewish architect, fled Vienna after the 1938 annexation. He intended shopping centers as suburban town centers with civic functions; developers built the retail and dropped the rest. **In a 1978 speech in London he disavowed American malls, saying he refused to pay alimony for those "bastard developments."** (MNopedia; Wikipedia "Gruen transfer" and Victor Gruen; Journal of Applied Research in Economic Development; *The Gruen Effect*.) **For an 8–15 readership, paraphrase the quote without the word — the outline does.** He returned to Vienna and objected to a shopping center built there.
- **Movie:** ***The Gruen Effect: Victor Gruen and the Shopping Mall*** (**2009**), directed by **Anette Baldauf and Katharina Weingartner**. (Austrian Cultural Forum New York; dergrueneffekt.at; Denver Film Society listing.) About Gruen directly.

### Plastic at the counter
- **Diners Club, February 1950** — first multi-merchant charge card, cardboard, bill paid in full monthly, first used at Major's Cabin Grill, New York. The forgotten-wallet founding story was **invented for publicity**, admitted by the company's own first PR man, Matty Simmons. (Smithsonian NMAH object record; Diners Club corporate history; payment-history journalism, labeled.)
- **Fresno Drop, September 1958** — Bank of America mailed about **60,000** unsolicited, ready-to-use BankAmericards to Fresno residents; first true revolving-credit bank card; delinquency about **22 percent** against about **4 percent** projected; merchants paid a **6 percent** fee; cardholders received **$300–$500** of instant credit; licensed nationwide and renamed **Visa in 1976**. (KVPR "Central Valley Roots"; 99% Invisible, "The Fresno Drop"; Joe Nocera's 1994 *Washington Post* history — journalism, labeled.) **Parked item now used.**
- **1970**: Congress banned mailing unsolicited credit cards (Public Law 91-508, amending the Truth in Lending Act). **Parked item now used.**
- Counter-side scale: by the Federal Reserve's payment diaries, credit cards were the single most-used U.S. payment method by the late 2010s, at **35 percent of all payments in 2024** (cash 14 percent). (Federal Reserve, *Diary of Consumer Payment Choice*, 2025 findings.)
- `money` leads the instrument; this chapter leads what it did to the transaction.

### 1962 and the discount store
- **Kmart** — first discount store **March 1, 1962**, Garden City, Michigan (from the S. S. Kresge five-and-dime chain); reported 4,000 customers in line on opening day.
- **Target** — first store **May 1, 1962**, Roseville, Minnesota (from the Dayton Company department stores).
- **Wal-Mart** — first store **July 2, 1962**, Rogers, Arkansas (Sam Walton).
- **Woolco** — also 1962, from F. W. Woolworth.
(Forbes, "From Kmart To Walmart — The Discount Store Class Of 1962"; Britannica; Target corporate 60th-anniversary page; Walmart corporate history; KSTP and Minnesota local history.) **The 1962 coincidence is real and confirmed across four sources.**

### Sam Walton
- Ran Ben Franklin variety stores in Arkansas before 1962; strategy was to put discount stores in small towns the big chains judged too small, buy in volume, and undercut. Walmart became the largest U.S. retailer and the largest private employer. (Britannica Money, "Walmart"; company histories; Independent Institute retrospective.)
- **Both sides, stated:** lower prices for rural households are documented; so are main-street store closures in the same towns and disputes over wages and supplier pressure. **Do not resolve it; give both.**
- **Movie:** ***Wal-Mart: The High Cost of Low Price*** (**2005**), directed by **Robert Greenwald**, Brave New Films, released **November 4, 2005**, 99 minutes, budget about $1.5 million. **It argues one side** — an advocacy documentary about the company, not a biography of Walton. Name it as such. (Brave New Films; Wikipedia; Rotten Tomatoes.)

### Franchising
- **A&W**: Roy Allen bought a root beer formula in 1919 and began franchising the A&W concept in **1924** (some accounts 1925); early franchisees included J. Willard Marriott. Often called the first successful food-franchising operation. (International Franchise Association; A&W history.)
- **Howard Johnson's**: first franchise awarded to **Reginald Sprague in 1935**; **41 restaurants by the end of 1936**; **107 by 1939** with about **$10.5 million** in revenue. (University of New Hampshire Rosenberg International Franchise Center; American Business History Center.)
- **Keep-out:** `food-farming` leads Ray Kroc and McDonald's, with *The Founder* (2016) as its film.

### The barcode
- **June 26, 1974, 8:01 a.m., Marsh Supermarket, Troy, Ohio.** Cashier **Sharon Buchanan** scanned a **ten-pack of Wrigley's Juicy Fruit gum** costing **67 cents** for **Clyde Dawson**, Marsh's head of research and development — the first retail sale using a Universal Product Code. (GS1, "Marsh holds place of honor in history of GS1 barcode"; History.com; *Smithsonian Magazine*, "The History of the Bar Code.")
- **Smithsonian holding:** NMAH holds one of the first ten **Spectra-Physics Model A** scanners. Accounts differ over whether the museum holds the original gum pack or a facsimile — **state the scanner, not the pack**. (Smithsonian NMAH object record "Supermarket Scanner"; *Smithsonian Magazine*.)
- The gum was chosen deliberately to prove a code could be printed legibly on a small package. (History.com; GS1.)

### Green Stamps and television selling
- **S&H Green Stamps**: collected in roughly **80 percent** of American households in the 1960s and 1970s; in the 1960s the company issued more stamps than the U.S. Post Office and distributed about **35 million catalogs a year**. (Britannica; Encyclopedia.com, "Trading Stamps"; Antique Trader.)
- **Home Shopping Network**: began **1982** as the local Home Shopping Club on cable in Pinellas County, Florida (founders **Lowell "Bud" Paxson** and **Roy Speer**); went national **July 1, 1985**. Origin: a **1977** radio advertiser paid Paxson's station in electric can openers, and host **Bob Circosta** sold them on air at $9.95 each. (HSN company history; Wikipedia; Encyclopedia.com — company history and journalism, labeled.)
- **QVC**: founded **1986** by **Joe Segel** (founder of the Franklin Mint); first broadcast **November 1986**, opening product a water-resistant AM/FM shower radio. (Britannica; QVC company histories.)

### The end of the big catalog
- **January 25, 1993**: Sears announced it was closing its catalog division, along with 50,000 job cuts and 113 store closures, as part of a $1.7 billion restructuring. The final general catalog was mailed in **spring 1993**. (*Baltimore Sun*, January 26, 1993; WRTV retrospective; Britannica.)
- **Age dispute:** the announcement was reported as ending a **97-year-old** catalog; other accounts say **101** or **105** years, depending on whether the count starts from 1886, 1888, 1893, or 1896. **State the range; do not pick one silently.**

### Online selling begins (dates belong to this era; the consequences to the next)
- **Amazon**: incorporation papers filed **July 5, 1994** (originally "Cadabra, Inc.") from a rented house and garage in **Bellevue, Washington**; **Amazon.com opened July 16, 1995**; first book sold was **Douglas Hofstadter's *Fluid Concepts & Creative Analogies***; about **$12,000** in book orders in the first week. (Amazon corporate history "first office" page; HistoryLink, "Amazon: The Early Years"; History.com; GeekWire.)
- **eBay**: started by **Pierre Omidyar** over Labor Day weekend, **September 3, 1995**, as AuctionWeb. First sale **$14.83**; eBay's own telling says a **broken laser pointer**, some accounts say a broken printer — **state the dispute**. (eBay corporate history; Academy of Achievement; Wikipedia.)

## 10. 2000 to Today (current through 2026)

### The measured size of online shopping
- **U.S. Census Bureau, Quarterly Retail E-Commerce Sales:** e-commerce was **16.4 percent** of total U.S. retail sales for **2025**, and **16.6 percent** in **Q4 2025**; Q3 2025 was **16.4 percent**. (Census Bureau releases; FRED series ECOMPCTSA.)
- The plain point for a young reader: **more than five dollars in six is still spent in physical stores.**

### Amazon and Prime
- **Amazon Prime launched February 2, 2005**: **$79 a year** for unlimited two-day shipping. Membership passed **200 million** worldwide by 2021; the U.S. price reached **$139 a year**. (Amazon press release, February 2005, via SEC EDGAR; Amazon Prime histories; CNN Business timeline.)
- **Movie:** ***Amazon Empire: The Rise and Reign of Jeff Bezos*** (PBS ***Frontline***), premiered **February 18, 2020**, two hours, by **James Jacoby and Anya Bourg**. It is an investigation and argues a case — say so. (PBS Frontline official site; IMDb.)
- **Keep-out:** `big-business` leads Amazon's market power and antitrust; `work-workers` leads warehouse and delivery labor and already carries Chris Smalls and the April 1, 2022 JFK8 union vote.

### Malls empty out — the honest numbers
- **Counts disagree because definitions disagree.** Green Street Advisors is cited for roughly **1,000** enclosed malls; other compilations give about **700** enclosed malls by 2023; broader counts including open-air centers give about **1,150** for 2024. Green Street's grading gives roughly **200 A malls**, **250 B malls**, and **300-plus at C+ or lower** in its 2017 outlook, with occupancy about 95 percent for A, 89 percent for B, and 72 percent for C and below. (Green Street Advisors as cited in retail trade press; industry statistics compilations — **company research and journalism, labeled; no government count of malls exists.**)
- **Store closures** are counted more consistently: **Coresight Research** recorded **7,325** U.S. store closures in **2024** and forecast about **15,000** for **2025**. (Coresight, reported in retail press — journalism, labeled.)
- Say the mechanism plainly, without personifying buildings: shoppers moved online or to newer open-air centers; anchor department stores closed; the smaller shops that depended on anchor traffic lost customers; owners could not re-let. Reuses include apartments, clinics, colleges, and warehouses.

### Targeted advertising and the data trade
- **FTC, *Data Brokers: A Call for Transparency and Accountability*, May 2014**: data brokers collect and store **"billions of data elements"** on **nearly every U.S. consumer**, from online activity (shopping, social networking) and offline activity (warranty cards, retail purchases, property records), **largely without consumer knowledge**. Three product categories: marketing, risk mitigation, and people search. In "onboarding," offline data is attached to browser cookies so a person can be followed site to site and shown targeted advertisements. The FTC asked Congress to legislate for transparency and consumer access. (FTC report and press materials, May 2014; Senate HSGAC testimony of Maneesha Mithal, May 15, 2014; Covington *Inside Privacy* summary; *Communications of the ACM*.)
- **Both sides, unloaded:** targeting funds services people use without paying money and can surface relevant goods; it also means records of what people read and buy are traded by firms they have never heard of. **No general federal privacy law exists as of 2026; several states have passed their own.** Say only that.

### Secondhand and resale
- **ThredUp, 13th Annual Resale Report (2025)**: U.S. secondhand apparel market grew **14 percent in 2024**, its strongest since 2021 and about 5 times the growth of the broader clothing market; **online resale grew 23 percent**; **58 percent of shoppers bought secondhand in 2024**, up from **52 percent in 2023** — the first time the figure passed half. Projections: U.S. secondhand apparel to **$74 billion by 2029**; online resale to **$40 billion by 2029**.
- **Label it:** ThredUp is a resale company, so this is a company estimate with a commercial interest, not a government statistic; the 2029 numbers are forecasts. (ThredUp investor releases and Resale Report; Forbes, "Online Resale Jumped 23% In 2024"; Fashion Dive — journalism, labeled.)
