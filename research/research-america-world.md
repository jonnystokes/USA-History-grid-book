## ERA 01 — BEFORE 1500

### 1.1 There is no United States and no European claim at the start of the era

No sourcing needed for the negative; it is the definitional fact. The positive content is
below.

### 1.2 Native diplomacy existed and is documented — but `native-nations` leads on it

Councils, alliances, agreed protocol for meeting, and gift exchange as part of an
agreement were how nations on this continent dealt with each other. **This chapter does
not tell that story** — `research/research-native-nations.md` and `outlines/native-nations.md`
carry it. This chapter's only claim is the boundary claim: the diplomacy conducted on this
land before 1500 was Native diplomacy, not American diplomacy.

- **No named Native diplomat from before European contact is documented by name.** Named
  speakers appear in colonial treaty-council minutes from the 1600s onward, not before.
  Therefore **no `hb-story` in this era.** Do not invent a name for the slot.

### 1.3 Polynesian voyagers reached Hawai‘i — territories thread, and the date is disputed

- **Older orthodox estimate:** initial Polynesian discovery of Hawai‘i between about
  **AD 300 and 750** — described as the long-held "orthodox opinion" in *When Did the
  Polynesians Settle Hawai‘i? A Review of 150 Years of Scholarly Inquiry and a Tentative
  Answer* (University of Hawai‘i at Mānoa, eVols repository,
  https://evols.library.manoa.hawaii.edu/items/6efe6b3b-7a0e-4290-82bf-7f2ebf9dbac0).
- **Current "short chronology" from revised radiocarbon dating:** roughly **AD 1000–1200**,
  with some studies narrowing to **AD 1200–1290** (Wikipedia, *Radiocarbon dating in
  Hawaii*, summarizing Rieth/Hunt and Wilmshurst et al.;
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiocarbon_dating_in_Hawaii ; and *The 13th century
  Polynesian colonization of Hawai'i Island*, **Journal of Archaeological Science**,
  https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0305440311002159).
- **The dispute is live.** Some archaeologists hold to greater antiquity for Hawaiian
  settlement (same Wikipedia summary). The only securely dated early habitation site in the
  archipelago is **O18, the Bellows Beach site at Waimānalo, O‘ahu**.
- **How to write it:** give the range and say the experts disagree. Do not pick a date.

### 1.4 The Treaty of Tordesillas, 7 June 1494 — belongs in this era by the boundary rule

- Signed **7 June 1494** at Tordesillas, Castile, between **Ferdinand II of Aragon and
  Isabella I of Castile** on one side and **John II of Portugal** on the other
  (UNESCO Memory of the World, https://www.unesco.org/en/memory-world/treaty-tordesillas ;
  EHNE, https://ehne.fr/en/encyclopedia/themes/international-relations/europe-and-legal-regulation-international-relations/treaty-tordesillas-june-7-1494).
- It drew a **line from pole to pole 370 leagues west of the Cape Verde Islands** — about
  **1,185 miles**, roughly **46°30′ W** (Britannica,
  https://www.britannica.com/event/Treaty-of-Tordesillas). Everything east of the line was
  Portugal's to claim; everything west was Spain's.
- **What to say plainly:** two European kings and a queen divided the Atlantic half of the
  world between their two kingdoms in a Spanish town, six years before this era ends. Nobody
  who lived in the Americas was asked, present, or told. Millions of people were inside a
  line drawn by people who had never seen them.
- Preceded by Pope Alexander VI's bull *Inter caetera* (1493), which Tordesillas revised —
  **not separately verified for this bank; do not state the bull's date without checking it.**

## ERA 02 — THE 1500s

### 2.1 Still no United States; the decisions were made in Europe

Spain, Portugal, France and England made claims to this land in their own capitals. The
people already living here were not parties to any of it. (Follows directly from Tordesillas
above and from the era's colonizing record; no separate citation needed for the negative.)

### 2.2 Fort Caroline and Matanzas, 1565 — a European war settled by killing, on this coast

This is the one concrete 1500s episode worth telling, because it shows exactly whose foreign
relations were being conducted here. **Source: National Park Service, Fort Matanzas National
Monument, "The Massacre"** (https://www.nps.gov/foma/learn/historyculture/the_massacre.htm)
and NPS Fort Matanzas history page
(https://www.nps.gov/foma/learn/historyculture/index.htm).

- France planted a colony, **Fort Caroline**, inside land Spain claimed. Spain sent
  **Pedro Menéndez de Avilés**, who arrived in **August 1565**.
- **Fort Caroline was attacked on 20 September 1565.** Menéndez marched roughly **500 men**
  overland while the French commander **Jean Ribault** was away with the fleet, which a storm
  had scattered. NPS: most of the fort's soldiers were absent and "most men killed in
  battle"; the French commander at the fort, **René de Laudonnière**, escaped into the woods.
  - **DISPUTE ON THE DEATH TOLL.** History.com's "This Day in History" entry for 20 September
    1565 (https://www.history.com/this-day-in-history/september-20/first-european-battle-on-american-soil)
    gives **132 killed out of about 240 people at the fort**. Other popular accounts say
    everyone was killed. NPS does not give a number for Fort Caroline. **Write the range and
    say the count is disputed; do not assert a single figure.**
- **Matanzas Inlet, mid-September 1565:** 127 shipwrecked Frenchmen were stopped at an inlet
  south of St. Augustine. NPS: **"111 Frenchmen were killed."** **Sixteen were spared** —
  Catholics, sailors who had been forced into service, and craftsmen.
- **Matanzas Inlet, 12 October 1565:** more shipwrecked French appeared. NPS: **"134 were
  killed."** **Jean Ribault and his men surrendered and were executed.**
- The inlet is still called **Matanzas** — NPS: the Spanish word for **"slaughters."**
- **Shared with:** `war` (the fighting itself) · `exploration` (the coastal claims) ·
  `religion` (these were French Protestants killed by Catholic Spain). Our angle is only
  this: the question of who would hold Florida was a European quarrel, and it was settled on
  this coast by killing the losers.
- **Hard-subjects note:** say "killed" and "executed" and give the numbers. Do not write
  "clashed," "conflict," "eliminated the French presence," or "Spain secured Florida."

### PATCH 2026-09-27 (T-254): Menéndez's own account of the killings, and whose land it was

- **Primary source:** Pedro Menéndez de Avilés, letter to King Philip II, **15 October

  https://earlyfloridalit.net/pedro-menendez-de-aviles-letter-to-king-philip-ii/).
  - **Fort Caroline, 20 September 1565 (his count):** "without the death of a single

    and, the next day, ten more who were taken prisoner in the woods." So **132 killed at
    the fort plus 10 caught the next day** in Menéndez's own count. This matches
    History.com's 132 and adds the 10. NPS gives no number. Keep the dispute note: other
    accounts differ.
  - **Matanzas Inlet (the first group):** the Frenchmen "surrendered their arms to me. And
    I had their hands tied behind them and put them to the knife." ("Put to the knife"

    craftsmen, "carpenters and caulkers ... people of whom I have need."
  - **His stated reason:** he believed that "to punish them in this way would be serving
    God Our Lord, and Your Majesty," because they were Protestants spreading "their evil
    sect" and he had come "to plant the Gospel in these parts."
  - **Who acted:** Menéndez gave the order and his soldiers did the killing (his letter).
    NPS (The Massacre page, read directly) notes King Philip II was "incensed" at the French

    Menéndez ordered it.
- **Whose land:** the NPS page records that Menéndez "learned from Timucuan Indians that a
  group of white men were on the beach." Fort Caroline and St. Augustine stood in the
  homeland of **Timucua**-speaking peoples (NPS, same page, names them as present). Neither
  France nor Spain asked them.

## ERA 03 — THE 1600s

### 3.1 Whose foreign relations these were

English, Dutch, French and Spanish colonies on this coast were possessions. Their trade
rules, their wars, and their borders were decided in London, Amsterdam, Paris and Madrid.
Colonists had no ministers abroad, made no treaties in their own name, and were told what
they were allowed to buy and sell.

### 3.2 The Navigation Acts — trade rules written in London for people 3,000 miles away

- **Navigation Act of 1651**, aimed chiefly at the Dutch: **all trade between England and the
  colonies had to travel in English or colonial ships** (NCpedia, "Navigation Acts (1651,
  1660)," https://www.ncpedia.org/navigation-acts-1651-1660). It helped bring on the first
  Anglo-Dutch war in **1652**.
- **Navigation Act of 1660** continued the 1651 rules and **"enumerated"** goods that could
  be shipped only to England or an English possession: **sugar, tobacco, cotton, wool, indigo
  and ginger** (NCpedia, same page).
- **Staple Act of 1663** (the third act): **European goods bound for the American colonies
  had to be landed in England first**, inspected by customs, and re-shipped from there
  (Britannica, https://www.britannica.com/event/Navigation-Acts ; EBSCO Research Starters,
  https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/british-navigation-acts/).
- **Plain-language point for a child:** a colonist in Boston could not legally buy a Dutch
  rope or sell his tobacco to a French merchant. Parliament, in a building he would never
  see, decided who he was allowed to trade with.

### 3.3 New Netherland changes owners twice — and is traded away for a nutmeg island

- **1664:** an English fleet arrived; the Dutch surrendered New Netherland without a fight.
  **DATE DISPUTE:** sources give the surrender document variously — the Wikipedia article
  *Articles of Surrender of New Netherland* dates the "Articles of Capitulation on the
  Reduction of New Netherland" to **29 September 1664**
  (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Articles_of_Surrender_of_New_Netherland), while other
  accounts date the capitulation to late August / early September 1664, the gap reflecting
  Old Style vs New Style calendars. **Write "1664"; do not assert a day without further
  checking.**
- **Treaty of Breda, 21 July 1667**, ended the Second Anglo-Dutch War. The Dutch had the
  better of the war, but at Breda **they let England keep New Netherland and took instead
  Suriname, with its sugar plantations, and Run, an island in what is now Indonesia that was
  a source of nutmeg** (Britannica, https://www.britannica.com/event/Treaty-of-Breda ;
  Historical Society of the New York Courts,
  https://history.nycourts.gov/about_period/treaty-of-breda-1667/).
- **Treaty of Westminster, 1674**, ended the Third Anglo-Dutch War and **returned the colony
  — by then New York — to England**, renewing the 1667 Breda terms (Wikipedia, *Treaty of
  Westminster (1674)*, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Westminster_(1674)).
- **Plain-language point:** New York was handed back and forth by two European governments,
  and at one point Dutch negotiators judged sugar plantations and a nutmeg island the better
  end of the bargain. Nobody who lived in New York was consulted.
- **Shared with:** `city-building` (New Amsterdam becoming New York) · `marketplace` /
  `economy` (the sugar and spice trade). Our angle: the town changed countries by treaty.
- **Note on Suriname and Run:** the sugar came from enslaved labor. `slavery-freedom` leads.

### PATCH 2026-09-27 (T-254): whose land New Netherland was

- Pratt Institute Libraries, *Lenape Peoples' History* research guide (university library,
  read directly, https://libguides.pratt.edu/c.php?g=1088684&p=9380209): the **Lenape**
  "originally lived on lands that now encompass New York City, parts of Long Island and the
  Hudson Valley, New Jersey, northeastern Delaware, and eastern Pennsylvania"; their
  homeland is called **Lenapehoking**. The Dutch claimed they "purchased" Mannahatta in
  1626 for 60 guilders' worth of trade goods, and likely misread what the Lenape meant by
  the exchange. "by 1700 the Lenape population was reduced by 85%," from diseases the
  colonists brought.
- So in 1664, 1667 and 1674, when English and Dutch diplomats traded New Netherland back
  and forth, the land was Lenape land (and Mohican and Haudenosaunee land farther up the
  Hudson, which this bank has not separately sourced). No Lenape were party to Breda or
  Westminster (the treaties are between the two crowns; see 3.3 sources).

## ERA 04 — 1700 TO 1750

### 4.1 Still no United States

Colonists in this era were subjects of Britain (or France, or Spain). When their empire went
to war, they were at war. When their empire made peace, they had no say in the terms.

### 4.2 The summer of 1701 — the Haudenosaunee deal with both empires at once

This is the sharpest example of diplomacy conducted on this land by people who were not
Europeans and not Americans.

- **Nanfan Treaty, Albany, New York, 19 July 1701.** Representatives of the Haudenosaunee
  (Five Nations) met **John Nanfan**, acting colonial governor of New York, and made a deed
  to **King William** covering western hunting lands running from what is now western New
  York toward what is now Chicago (Wikipedia, *Nanfan Treaty*,
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanfan_Treaty ; text at Oklahoma State University's treaty
  archive, https://treaties.okstate.edu/treaties/nanfan-treaty-1701 ; Six Nations of the
  Grand River posts the document,
  https://www.sixnations.ca/LandsResources/NanFanTreaty.pdf).
  - **What the deed meant is still contested today.** Six Nations and other Haudenosaunee
    parties read it as preserving hunting rights; other writers dispute that reading. Say
    that it is disputed; do not settle it.
- **Great Peace of Montréal, 4 August 1701.** Signed by **Louis-Hector de Callière**,
  governor of New France, and **about 1,300 representatives of 39 Indigenous nations**. It
  ended decades of war and was intended to keep the Five Nations **neutral** if France and
  the English colonies fought (The Canadian Encyclopedia, *Great Peace of Montreal, 1701*,
  https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/peace-of-montreal-1701 ;
  Pointe-à-Callière museum, https://pacmusee.qc.ca/en/stories-of-montreal/article/the-great-peace-of-montreal/).
- **Two weeks apart, the same confederacy made a deal with the English at Albany and a peace
  with the French at Montréal, and kept itself out of the empires' next war.** That is the
  point of the era.
- **Kondiaronk**, a Tionontati (Petun/Wendat) leader, was central to bringing it about.
  Already ill in an epidemic then spreading through Montréal, he made a decisive speech at
  the council **on 1 August 1701**, was carried to the Hôtel-Dieu hospital, and **died a few
  hours later, on 2 August 1701**. He was given a large funeral in Montréal on **3 August
  1701**, and the treaty was signed **the next day** (Pointe-à-Callière, *Kondiaronk, broker
  of the Great Peace of Montréal*,
  https://pacmusee.qc.ca/en/stories-of-montreal/article/kondiaronk-broker-of-the-great-peace-of-montreal/ ;
  The Canadian Encyclopedia, *Kondiaronk*,
  https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/kondiaronk).
  - **No verified film about Kondiaronk was found.** Leave `movie=""`.
  - **Shared with:** `native-nations` (**lead** — Haudenosaunee and Wendat history in full).
    Our slice is only this: he was a diplomat, and the treaty he argued for was between
    nations.

### 4.3 The Treaty of Utrecht, 11 April 1713 — a war ends and a slave-trading contract is a prize

- The **Treaty of Utrecht** was concluded **11 April 1713** at Utrecht in the Netherlands,
  one of the treaties ending the War of the Spanish Succession — the war the colonists called
  Queen Anne's War (The Canadian Encyclopedia, *Treaty of Utrecht*,
  https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/treaty-of-utrecht).
- France gave Britain **the whole drainage basin of Hudson Bay**, **its claims to
  Newfoundland** (evacuating its base at Plaisance/Placentia, though French fishermen kept
  certain coastal rights), and **Acadia**, whose capital Port-Royal (Annapolis Royal) a New
  England expedition had taken in **1710** (same source).
- **Britain was awarded the *asiento*: the sole right to carry enslaved Black people into
  Spanish America for thirty years** (The Canadian Encyclopedia, same page; New World
  Encyclopedia, *Treaty of Utrecht*,
  https://www.newworldencyclopedia.org/entry/Treaty_of_Utrecht).
- **Hard-subjects note — this is the sentence that must not be softened.** Do not write
  "trading rights," "the asiento," or "commercial concessions" and stop. Write what it was:
  a peace treaty handed Britain a thirty-year contract to ship kidnapped African people
  across the ocean and sell them in Spanish colonies, and Britain counted it among its
  winnings. **Shared with:** `slavery-freedom` (**lead** — the trade itself and the Middle
  Passage) · `war` (the fighting).

### 4.4 Louisbourg, 1745 — colonists take a fortress; Britain trades it for a city in India

- **4 April 1745:** almost **3,000 New England troops** under **Colonel William Pepperrell**
  sailed from Boston; a Royal Navy squadron under **Commodore Peter Warren** joined them
  (Loyalist Collection, University of New Brunswick,
  https://loyalist.lib.unb.ca/record/louisbourg-papers-expedition-1745-1744-1758 ; Wikipedia,
  *Siege of Louisbourg (1745)*, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Louisbourg_(1745)).
- **17 June 1745:** the fortress of **Louisbourg**, on Île-Royale (Cape Breton), surrendered
  after a **49-day siege** (same sources).
- **1748: the Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle gave Louisbourg back to France, in exchange for
  Madras in India**, which France had taken from Britain. Britain did not consult the
  colonists, and New Englanders were furious (American History Central, *Treaty of
  Aix-la-Chapelle*, https://www.americanhistorycentral.com/entries/aix-la-chapelle-treaty-1748/ ;
  *Journal of the American Revolution*, "The Two Sieges of Louisbourg: Harbingers of American
  Discontent," https://allthingsliberty.com/2021/11/the-two-sieges-of-louisbourg-harbingers-of-american-discontent/).
- **Plain-language point:** New England men died taking a fortress, and three years later the
  British government swapped it back for a city in India that most of them had never heard
  of. That is what it meant to have no foreign policy of your own.
- **Shared with:** `war` (**lead** — the siege as fighting). Our angle: the treaty that
  undid it.
- **Caution:** the "harbingers of American discontent" framing is an interpretation, not a
  fact. Do not write that Louisbourg caused the Revolution.

## ERA 05 — 1750 TO 1800 — where this chapter actually begins

### 5.1 The Revolution was a foreign-relations project from the start

- **29 November 1775:** the Second Continental Congress created the **Committee of Secret
  Correspondence** to open and protect secret communication with possible foreign friends.
  Original members: **Benjamin Franklin, Benjamin Harrison, Thomas Johnson, John Dickinson
  and John Jay** (DVIDS, "Congress Creates Committee of Secret Correspondence (29 NOV 1775),"
  https://www.dvidshub.net/news/458492/congress-creates-committee-secret-correspondence-29-nov-1775 ;
  Library of Congress holds the committee's journal extracts among the Jefferson papers,
  https://www.loc.gov/item/mtjbib000136/ ; Office of the Historian,
  https://history.state.gov/milestones/1776-1783/secret-committee).
- **17 April 1777:** Congress renamed it the **Committee for Foreign Affairs** (same
  sources).
- **10 August 1781:** Congress chose **Robert R. Livingston** of New York as the first
  **Secretary for Foreign Affairs** (National Archives, *Records of the Department of State*,
  https://www.archives.gov/research/foreign-policy/state-dept/agency-records).
  - **DATE DISPUTE on the department itself.** One summary dates the creation of the
    Department of Foreign Affairs to **10 January 1780**; most accounts give **10 January
    1781**. **State it as "in 1781, Congress set up a department for foreign affairs" or
    give both dates; do not assert one.**
- **27 July 1789:** the new federal government re-established the **Department of Foreign
  Affairs**; **15 September 1789**, an act renamed it the **Department of State**, because it
  was also given domestic record-keeping duties (National Archives, same page; Office of the
  Historian FAQ, https://history.state.gov/about/faq/why-called-state).

### 5.2 Morocco recognized the United States first — December 1777

- **20 December 1777:** Sultan **Mohammed III (Sidi Muhammad ben Abdallah, r. 1757–1790)**
  of Morocco issued a declaration that vessels sailing under the American flag could enter

### 5.3 France: secret help, then the alliance of 1778

### 5.4 The Treaty of Paris, 1783

- **30 November 1782:** preliminary articles signed. **20 January 1783:** France signed
  preliminary articles. **3 September 1783:** the formal **Treaty of Paris** signed. American
  negotiators: **Benjamin Franklin, John Adams and John Jay** (Office of the Historian,
  https://history.state.gov/milestones/1776-1783/treaty).
- Terms: Britain accepted American independence and the boundaries; fishing rights on the
  Newfoundland banks; pre-war debts to British creditors; compensation promised for property
  taken from Loyalists; British forces to withdraw from the thirteen states (same page).
  Franklin asked Britain to hand over Canada; Britain refused (same page).
- **Ratification:** the Confederation Congress, sitting at **Annapolis, Maryland**, ratified
  on **14 January 1784**; Britain ratified **9 April 1784**; the ratified copies were
  **exchanged in Paris on 12 May 1784** (Library of Congress, *Treaty of Paris: Primary
  Documents in American History*, https://guides.loc.gov/treaty-of-paris ; U.S. House of
  Representatives History, Art & Archives,
  https://history.house.gov/Historical-Highlights/1700s/The-Continental-Congress-s-ratification-of-the-Treaty-of-Paris/ ;
  Avalon Project ratification text,
  https://avalon.law.yale.edu/18th_century/parisrat.asp).
- **The part that must not be left out.** The boundary Britain drew ran west to the
  Mississippi. The land inside it was lived on by Native nations who were **not** at the
  negotiating table, were **not** parties to the treaty, and had **not** surrendered. Britain
  handed over a claim it did not exercise on the ground. **This bank did not separately
  source a specific quotation or figure for that point** — it follows from the treaty's
  boundary terms and the absence of Native signatories. **Verify a specific citation before
  putting a number or quotation on it.** `native-nations` leads on what followed.
- Britain also did not withdraw from the northwestern posts as promised — which is why the
  Jay Treaty had to address it eleven years later (see 5.6).

### 5.6 The Barbary states — American sailors enslaved, and a country too weak to help them

This is the strongest human material in the era and it must be told without euphemism. The
men were **enslaved**; that is the word the sources use.

- **1784:** an American merchant ship was seized by Morocco; a treaty followed in **1786**
  (Office of the Historian, *Barbary Wars*,
  https://history.state.gov/milestones/1801-1829/barbary-wars).
- **1785:** the **Dey of Algiers, Muhammad**, declared war on the United States and captured
  several American ships (same page).
- **25 July 1785:** the schooner ***Maria*** **of Boston** was taken about three miles
  southeast of **Cape St. Vincent, Portugal**, and reached Algiers on **4 August 1785**.
  **James Leander Cathcart**, a young crewman, and **twenty other sailors** were enslaved.
  (Library of Congress finding aid, *James L. Cathcart Papers*,
  https://findingaids.loc.gov/exist_collections/ead3pdf/mss/2009/ms009015.pdf ; LOC catalog
  record, https://www.loc.gov/item/mm79015388/ ; Dartmouth Libraries Archives &
  Manuscripts, agent record for Cathcart, James Leander, 1767–1843,

  - **BIRTH-YEAR DISPUTE:** Dartmouth's archival record gives **1767–1843**; a genealogy page

- Over eleven years in Algiers, Cathcart **rose to be chief Christian clerk (secretary) to
  the Dey**. The Avalon Project's editorial note on the 1795 treaty records that he **gave
  significant assistance to the American negotiator and translated the Turkish text of the
  treaty into English** (https://avalon.law.yale.edu/18th_century/bar1795n.asp).

  Joseph Donaldson and Richard O'Brien** as the diplomats sent to North Africa in 1795, who
  concluded treaties with **Algiers, Tunis and Tripoli**
  (https://history.state.gov/milestones/1801-1829/barbary-wars).

  enslaved"):** the *Maria* had a crew of **six**. Cathcart: "They were twenty-one in number
  and we were only six." The **21** is the total of Americans taken in 1785 from **both**
  ships: 6 from the *Maria* and 15 from the *Dauphin* (his appendix list names all 21).

  and shoes, and on the voyage shut the captives (42 men) every night in the sail room in
  the hold; the water was so foul "we were, literally, obliged to strain through our teeth"
  (*The Captives*, ch. 1).

  chose some captives for his palace and garden; others went to the slave prisons (bagnios)

  clothes worth about a dollar and a half and two coarse blankets meant to last the whole
  captivity. About four months in, the Dey's chamberlains had four men throw Cathcart down,
  tie his arms and legs, and beat the soles of his feet with sticks, **28 blows**, which
  cost him four toenails. **Bastinado** (define it at first use) = beating the soles of the
  bare feet with a stick while the person is held down. He records that all fourteen
  garden slaves got the same, "old men of sixty and children of ten years of age" included,

  to the Bagnio Galera and "put to hardest labor" (ch. 7 heading).

  minister of the marine), then clerk of the marine, then clerk of the Bagnio Galera, and
  at last **chief Christian clerk to the Dey** (preface: "Christian clerk to the Dey of

### 5.7 The 1790s — staying out of Europe's war

- **8 April 1793:** **Edmond-Charles Genêt**, the new French minister, landed at
  **Charleston, South Carolina** (Miller Center, *George Washington — Key Events*,
  https://millercenter.org/president/george-washington/key-events). He commissioned American
  privateers and, when Washington would not do as he wished, threatened to appeal over the
  President's head directly to the American people. Washington demanded France replace him
  (same source).
- **22 April 1793: Washington issued the Proclamation of Neutrality**, declaring the United
  States would stay out of the war spreading in Europe (Miller Center,
  https://millercenter.org/the-presidency/presidential-speeches/april-22-1793-proclamation-neutrality ;
  Mount Vernon, https://www.mountvernon.org/library/digitalhistory/digital-encyclopedia/article/neutrality-proclamation).
- **Jay Treaty:** negotiated by **John Jay**; **signed 19 November 1794**; the **Senate
  ratified it 24 June 1795 by 20 votes to 10** (Office of the Historian, *Jay Treaty*,
  https://history.state.gov/milestones/1784-1800/jay-treaty).
  - Britain agreed to **leave the northwestern posts** it had promised to leave in 1783, and
    granted the United States "most favored nation" trading status **with heavy limits on
    trade with the British West Indies**.
  - Left unsettled and sent to arbitration: the **Canada–Maine boundary**, pre-Revolution
    debts, and **British seizures of American ships**. Britain kept the right to **seize
    American cargo bound for France (with compensation)** and to **take French goods off
    American ships without paying** (same page).
  - It was very unpopular. The Office of the Historian's summary: the treaty "addressed few
    U.S. interests, and ultimately granted Britain additional rights." Washington signed it
    anyway, judging peace with Britain worth it while the country was weak.
  - **NOT VERIFIED in this pass but commonly stated: that the treaty was silent on the
    impressment of American sailors, and that Jay was burned in effigy. Verify before
    writing either.**
- **Pinckney's Treaty (Treaty of San Lorenzo), signed 27 October 1795** with Spain, negotiated
  by **Thomas Pinckney (1750–1828)** for the United States and **Manuel de Godoy** for Spain;
  **ratified by the Senate 7 March 1796** (Britannica,
  https://www.britannica.com/event/Pinckneys-Treaty ; Encyclopedia.com, *Pinckney Treaty*,
  https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/pinckney-treaty ;
  Mississippi Encyclopedia, https://mississippiencyclopedia.org/entries/san-lorenzo-treaty-of-pinckneys-treaty/).
  - It set the boundary with Spanish West Florida at the **31st parallel**, gave Americans
    **free navigation of the Mississippi** through Spanish territory, and **tax-free use of
    the port of New Orleans**.
  - **Why it mattered to ordinary people:** a farmer in Kentucky or Tennessee could not get a
    crop to market over the mountains. He floated it down the rivers to New Orleans. Spain
    controlled that port. This treaty is the reason he could sell his corn.
- **Washington's Farewell Address, published 19 September 1796 in the *American Daily
  Advertiser*** in Philadelphia — announcing he would not seek a third term and arguing
  against permanent foreign attachments (Miller Center, *Key Events*, as above; Owl Eyes
  historical context note, https://www.owleyes.org/text/farewell-address/analysis/historical-context).
  It was **drafted in collaboration with Alexander Hamilton** (Miller Center).

    not Washington's. Do not attribute it to the Farewell Address. **Verify any quotation
    from the Address against the text before using it.**

  commissioners to France: **Elbridge Gerry, Charles Cotesworth Pinckney and John Marshall**.

  https://history.state.gov/milestones/1784-1800/xyz).

## ERA 06 — 1800 to 1850

### The Louisiana Purchase, 1803

- **Treaty signed April 30, 1803.** The United States paid **$15 million** for **828,000 square miles** west of the Mississippi, roughly doubling the size of the country. ([National Archives, Milestone Documents — Louisiana Purchase Treaty](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/louisiana-purchase-treaty); the Archives' own "America's Historical Documents" page uses the same 828,000 figure. Some sources round to "over 827,000 square miles" — the difference is rounding, not a dispute.)
- **Why France sold — say it plainly.** France's attempt to put down the revolution of the enslaved people of Saint-Domingue failed; with that army destroyed and war with Britain coming, Napoleon sold Louisiana to raise money and to keep Britain from taking it. ([National Archives](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/louisiana-purchase-treaty); [Monticello — The Louisiana Purchase](https://www.monticello.org/thomas-jefferson/louisiana-lewis-clark/the-louisiana-purchase/).) The Haitian revolution is the reason the deal existed. **Do not write "France decided to sell."**
- **The American envoys had been sent to buy New Orleans only**, and were offered the whole territory. ([Monticello](https://www.monticello.org/thomas-jefferson/louisiana-lewis-clark/the-louisiana-purchase/).)
- **Territories beat — Article III of the treaty.** The treaty promised that the inhabitants of the ceded territory "shall be incorporated in the Union of the United States, and admitted as soon as possible … to the enjoyment of all the rights, advantages and immunities of citizens of the United States." ([Treaty text, National Archives Milestone Documents](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/louisiana-purchase-treaty).) **This promise is the hinge of the whole chapter:** in 1803 land bought by the United States came with a written promise that its people would become full citizens. A century later the Supreme Court held that no such promise applied to Puerto Rico, Guam, or the Philippines (see era 08, *Downes v. Bidwell*). The Native nations living across those 828,000 square miles were not consulted, were not party to the treaty, and were not included in the promise — the land was sold by one European empire to a new country while they were living on it. (`native-nations` leads on what followed for them.)

### The Barbary Wars

- The United States had been paying **tribute** — protection money — to Algiers, Tunis, Tripoli, and Morocco to keep American ships from being seized and American sailors from being held for ransom. The **pasha of Tripoli declared war on the United States on May 14, 1801** when the payments were not increased. ([Britannica, First Barbary War](https://www.britannica.com/event/First-Barbary-War); [U.S. Department of State, Office of the Historian — Barbary Wars](https://history.state.gov/milestones/1801-1829/barbary-wars).)
- **Peace treaty signed June 10, 1805**: no future tribute, but the United States did pay a ransom for the crew of the captured frigate *Philadelphia*. ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/event/First-Barbary-War).) A **Second Barbary War followed in 1815**. ([Office of the Historian](https://history.state.gov/milestones/1801-1829/barbary-wars).)
- Plain-language framing for a child: the country's first war fought entirely overseas was about money paid to stop ships being taken.

### The Monroe Doctrine, 1823

- **December 2, 1823**, in President James Monroe's annual message to Congress. The words: **"the American continents, by the free and independent condition which they have assumed and maintain, are henceforth not to be considered as subjects for future colonization by any European powers."** ([U.S. Senate — Message of President James Monroe, December 2, 1823](https://www.senate.gov/about/images/documents/annual-message-monroe-doctrine-1823-nara.htm); [National Archives Milestone Documents](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/monroe-doctrine); [Miller Center transcript](https://millercenter.org/the-presidency/presidential-speeches/december-2-1823-seventh-annual-message-monroe-doctrine).)
- **Most historians credit Secretary of State John Quincy Adams with writing it**, and with talking Monroe out of issuing it jointly with Britain. ([Office of the Historian — Monroe Doctrine, 1823](https://history.state.gov/milestones/1801-1829/monroe); [State Department Diplomacy Center — John Quincy Adams](https://diplomacy.state.gov/encyclopedia/john-quincy-adams-secretary-of-state-and-president/).)
- The honest note for a young reader: the United States in 1823 had no navy able to enforce this. What kept European powers out of Latin America in practice was Britain's navy, which had its own reasons.

### Florida, Texas, Oregon, and the war with Mexico

- **Adams–Onís Treaty, 1819** — Spain ceded Florida; John Quincy Adams negotiated it, which is why it carries his name. ([State Department Diplomacy Center — John Quincy Adams](https://diplomacy.state.gov/encyclopedia/john-quincy-adams-secretary-of-state-and-president/).)
- **Oregon Treaty, June 15, 1846** — the boundary with British territory set at the 49th parallel, settling the Oregon dispute by agreement rather than war. ([Office of the Historian — Oregon Territory](https://history.state.gov/milestones/1830-1860/oregon-territory).) *(Date well established; flag as `candidate` if the writer wants a second source.)*
- **Texas annexed 1845; war with Mexico 1846–1848.**
- **Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo, signed February 2, 1848**, at Guadalupe Hidalgo, a city north of Mexico City where the Mexican government had fled. Mexico gave up **more than 525,000 square miles**; the United States paid **$15,000,000**. ([National Archives — The Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo](https://www.archives.gov/education/lessons/guadalupe-hidalgo).)
- **Territories beat — what happened to the people on the land.** **Article IX** promised those who became U.S. citizens would be "maintained and protected in the free enjoyment of their liberty and property, and secured in the free exercise of their religion without restriction"; **the Senate rewrote Article IX** and struck out its last two paragraphs. **The Senate deleted Article X entirely when it ratified the treaty on March 10, 1848** — Article X was the one that guaranteed Spanish and Mexican land grants would stay valid under U.S. law. American negotiators then wrote the **Protocol of Querétaro**, telling Mexico that deleting Article X did not cancel those land titles; the Protocol did not carry the same force in American courts, and Mexican landowners spent decades losing land in them. ([National Archives — The Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo](https://www.archives.gov/education/lessons/guadalupe-hidalgo); [Homestead Museum on Article X](https://homesteadmuseum.blog/2019/05/31/article-ten-of-the-treaty-of-guadalupe-hidalgo-1848/) — museum blog, labeled.) The Court of Private Land Claims (1891–1904) later ruled on these titles. ([Wikipedia — United States Court of Private Land Claims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Court_of_Private_Land_Claims) — encyclopedia, labeled; use only for the court's existence and dates.)
- **Zoom-3 candidate — Nicholas Trist (verified).** Polk's envoy to Mexico. **Polk recalled him in October 1847**; Trist ignored the recall order, kept negotiating, and **signed the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo in February 1848 without the president's authority**. Polk was furious, sent the treaty to the Senate anyway because it got him what he wanted, **fired Trist, and refused to pay him for his expenses or for his last months of work.** ([National Constitution Center — "The man who delivered California to the U.S., and was fired for it"](https://constitutioncenter.org/blog/the-man-who-delivered-california-to-the-u-s-and-was-fired-for-it); [Encyclopedia Virginia — Nicholas Philip Trist](https://encyclopediavirginia.org/entries/trist-nicholas-philip-1800-1874/); [Britannica — Nicholas Trist](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Nicholas-Trist).) No film. **Movie: none — do not invent one.**
- **Zoom-3 candidate — John Quincy Adams (verified as diplomat).** Secretary of State 1817–1825; negotiated the Adams–Onís Treaty (1819) that took Florida from Spain; wrote the Monroe Doctrine. ([Office of the Historian — Biographies of the Secretaries of State: John Quincy Adams](https://history.state.gov/departmenthistory/people/adams-john-quincy); [State Department Diplomacy Center](https://diplomacy.state.gov/encyclopedia/john-quincy-adams-secretary-of-state-and-president/).) `government-politics` owns his presidency; ours is the Secretary of State. **Movie: none verified — leave the line out.**

### PATCH 2026-09-29 (T-312): Article XI of the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo, in its own words

### The first overseas territory was made of bird droppings — the Guano Islands Act, 1856

- **Congress passed the Guano Islands Act on August 18, 1856.** It let any American citizen who found an unclaimed island with guano (seabird droppings, used as fertilizer) take possession of it in the name of the United States. **The United States claimed roughly 100 islands under it** — the first time the country took territory overseas. ([US History Scene — The Guano Islands](https://ushistoryscene.com/article/guano-islands-bird-turds/); [48 U.S.C. Ch. 8 — Guano Islands, still on the books](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?path=%2Fprelim%40title48%2Fchapter8&edition=prelim); [Science News — "The U.S. empire was built on bird dung"](https://www.sciencenews.org/article/us-empire-built-bird-dung) — journalism, labeled.) The Supreme Court upheld the law in *Jones v. United States* (1890).
- Why it belongs in a children's history: it is the plainest possible answer to "when did the United States start taking islands?" — 1856, for fertilizer, forty-two years before 1898.

### The Alaska purchase, 1867

- **Treaty with Russia signed March 30, 1867**; the price was **$7,200,000**, and the formal transfer ceremony at Sitka was **October 18, 1867**. ([National Archives, *Prologue* — "Seward's Bargain: The Alaska Purchase from Russia"](https://www.archives.gov/publications/prologue/1994/winter/alaska-check), which reproduces the $7,200,000 warrant; [Treaty text, Alaska Historical Society](https://alaskahistoricalsociety.org/about-ahs/special-projects/150treaty/150th-resource-library/primary-source-documents/text-of-treaty-with-russia/).)
- **Territories beat — Article III, in the treaty's own words.** Inhabitants could return to Russia within three years, or stay and "with the exception of uncivilized native tribes" be admitted to "the enjoyment of all the rights, advantages, and immunities of citizens of the United States." The excepted people — Alaska Natives — "shall be subject to such laws and regulations as the United States may from time to time adopt in regard to aboriginal tribes." ### Hawaii: the overthrow of a kingdom, 1893–1898

## ERA 08 — 1900 to 1950

### The Insular Cases: part of the country, but not all of the Constitution

- Between 1901 and 1922 the Supreme Court decided a series of cases, called the **Insular Cases**, about what the Constitution required in the islands taken in 1898.
- ***Downes v. Bidwell*, decided May 27, 1901.** The Court held that **Puerto Rico "belongs to, but is not a part of, the United States"** for purposes of the tariff clause — that it was, in a phrase from the opinions, **foreign to the United States in a domestic sense.** ([U.S. Commission on Civil Rights, "The Insular Cases and the Doctrine of the Unincorporated Territory," 2024](https://www.usccr.gov/files/2024-02/final_english_pr-ac_memo-1.pdf) — federal civil-rights commission, the best single source for this section; [Harvard Law School — "Reexamining the Insular Cases. Again."](https://hls.harvard.edu/today/reexamining-the-insular-cases-again/).)
- From these cases comes the **doctrine of the "unincorporated territory"**: a place the United States owns and governs, whose people are **not** guaranteed the whole Constitution, unless Congress chooses to "incorporate" it. ([USCCR memo](https://www.usccr.gov/files/2024-02/final_english_pr-ac_memo-1.pdf).)
- ***Balzac v. Porto Rico*, 1922** — Puerto Ricans, by then U.S. citizens, still had **no constitutional right to a jury trial** in Puerto Rico, because the island was unincorporated. ([USCCR memo](https://www.usccr.gov/files/2024-02/final_english_pr-ac_memo-1.pdf).)
- **The reasoning is on the record and it was racial.** The opinions rested on the view that the people of the islands were unfit for full constitutional government. Say this, because the Court itself has now said it: in **2022, Justice Neil Gorsuch wrote that the Insular Cases "have no foundation in the Constitution and rest instead on racial stereotypes," and that "They deserve no place in our law."** ([*United States v. Vaello Madero*, 596 U.S. ___ (2022), Gorsuch, J., concurring — slip opinion](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/21pdf/20-303_6khn.pdf); [Volokh/Reason summary](https://reason.com/volokh/2022/04/21/justice-gorsuch-calls-for-overruling-the-insular-cases/).)
- **The doctrine is still in force and still contested.** No case has overruled the Insular Cases; they remain binding precedent in 2026, and they are the legal reason people in Puerto Rico, Guam, the U.S. Virgin Islands, American Samoa, and the Northern Marianas do not have the same rights as people in the fifty states. ([USCCR memo](https://www.usccr.gov/files/2024-02/final_english_pr-ac_memo-1.pdf); [Harvard Law School](https://hls.harvard.edu/today/reexamining-the-insular-cases-again/).) **The outline's [VERIFY] on the Insular Cases and "unincorporated territory" is cleared.**
- **Child-level wording that does not soften:** "The Supreme Court decided that these places belonged to the United States but were not part of it, and that the people who lived there did not get the whole Constitution. That decision has never been overturned. It is still the law today, and people are still arguing about it in court."

### Who is a citizen — island by island

- **Puerto Rico.** The **Foraker Act, signed April 12, 1900**, set up a civil government and declared the island's people **"citizens of Porto Rico … entitled to the protection of the United States"** — deliberately *not* United States citizens. ([Library of Congress — Foraker Act (Organic Act of 1900)](https://guides.loc.gov/world-of-1898/foraker-act); [Foraker Act text](https://loveman.sdsu.edu/docs/1900ForakerAct.pdf).)
- **The Jones–Shafroth Act, signed by President Wilson on March 2, 1917**, made Puerto Ricans **U.S. citizens** — **one month before the United States entered the First World War.** Two months after that, Wilson signed the conscription act. **Sources disagree on how many Puerto Ricans were drafted: about 18,000 is commonly given, and History.com states 20,000.** ([Library of Congress — 1917: Jones-Shafroth Act](https://guides.loc.gov/latinx-civil-rights/jones-shafroth-act); [Britannica — Jones-Shafroth Act](https://www.britannica.com/event/Jones-Shafroth-Act); [History.com](https://www.history.com/this-day-in-history/march-2/puerto-ricans-become-u-s-citizens-are-recruited-for-war-effort) — journalism, labeled.) **State the range; do not pick one.** The citizenship was statutory — granted by Congress, not by the Constitution — and it did not come with a vote for president. **This clears the outline's [VERIFY] on 1917.**
- **The U.S. Virgin Islands.** The United States **bought the Danish West Indies from Denmark**; treaty signed **August 4, 1916**, transfer completed **March 31, 1917**, price **$25 million in gold coin**. **The people who came with the islands were not made U.S. citizens until 1927.** ([Library of Congress — Today in History, March 31](https://www.loc.gov/item/today-in-history/march-31/); [Department of the Interior, Office of Insular Affairs — U.S. Virgin Islands](https://www.doi.gov/oia/islands/virgin-islands); [History.com](https://www.history.com/articles/us-virgin-islands-denmark-purchase) — journalism, labeled.) Ten years as people of the United States with no citizenship at all.
- **Guam.** Governed by U.S. Navy officers from 1899. Its people did not become U.S. citizens until the **Organic Act of Guam, signed by President Truman on August 1, 1950** — fifty-two years after the Navy took the island. The Act set up a civilian government and fixed Guam's status as an **unincorporated territory**. ([Guampedia — Organic Act of Guam](https://www.guampedia.com/organic-act-of-guam/); [Britannica — Organic Act of Guam](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Organic-Act-of-Guam); [Organic Act text](https://moglen.law.columbia.edu/twiki/pub/AmLegalHist/AndrewKerrProject/organic_act.pdf).)
- **American Samoa.** Navy rule until 1951. **Its people were made U.S. *nationals*, not citizens — and that is still true today.** American Samoa is the only permanently inhabited U.S. territory whose people do not get citizenship at birth. ([Department of the Interior, Office of Insular Affairs](https://www.doi.gov/oia/islands/american-samoa); [ACLU — "Nationals but not Citizens"](https://www.aclu.org/news/voting-rights/nationals-but-not-citizens-how-the-u-s-denies-citizenship-to-american-samoans) — advocacy organization, labeled; [8 FAM 308.2, U.S. State Department Foreign Affairs Manual](https://fam.state.gov/FAM/08FAM/08FAM030802.html) — the operative federal rule.)
- **The Philippines.** The Philippine Organic Act (1902) and the Jones Law (1916) set up American colonial government with a promise of eventual independence. **The Tydings–McDuffie Act, signed March 24, 1934, promised independence on July 4, 1946, after a ten-year Commonwealth**, and at the same time reclassified Filipinos for U.S. immigration purposes so that far fewer could enter. **Independence came on July 4, 1946.** ([Britannica — Tydings-McDuffie Act](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Tydings-McDuffie-Act); [Immigration History — Tydings-McDuffie Act of 1934](https://immigrationhistory.org/item/tydings-mcduffie-act/).)

### Finishing the Philippine war, 1901–1902

- **Balangiga, Samar, September 28, 1901.** Townspeople and guerrillas attacked a U.S. Army company at breakfast and **killed about 48 American soldiers** — the Army's worst single loss since the Little Bighorn in 1876.
