## 01 · Before 1500 (T-263a, 2026-09-27)

Sources opened this pass:
- **Martin & Goodman 2002:** Debra L. Martin and Alan H. Goodman, "Health conditions before Columbus: paleopathology of native North Americans," *Western Journal of Medicine* 176(1), January 2002, 65-68, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC1071659/ (full text opened by curl 2026-09-27).
- **Vågene 2022:** Å. J. Vågene and others, "Geographically dispersed zoonotic tuberculosis in pre-contact South American human populations," *Nature Communications* 2022, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8901693/ (opened).
- **Barquera 2025:** Rodrigo Barquera and others, "Ancient genomes reveal a deep history of Treponema pallidum in the Americas," *Nature*, published April 2025, https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-08515-5 (abstract and metadata opened by curl).
- **Newson 1993:** Linda A. Newson, "The Demographic Collapse of Native Peoples of the Americas, 1492-1650," *Proceedings of the British Academy* 81 (1993), 247-288, https://www.thebritishacademy.ac.uk/documents/4002/81p247.pdf (PDF downloaded and read).

How the record works: no written records of illness exist from before 1500 in this land. Martin & Goodman: "data on health in ancient societies are inferred from the analysis of a wide range of archaeologic materials, with human bones and teeth forming the largest body of evidence."

**Diseases present before contact.**
- Martin & Goodman: "Diseases such as treponemiasis and tuberculosis were already present in the New World, along with diseases such as tularemia, giardia, rabies, amebic dysentery, hepatitis, herpes, pertussis, and poliomyelitis, although the prevalence of almost all of these was probably low in any given group." And: "Osteologic data demonstrate that native groups were most definitely not living in a pristine, disease-free environment before contact. Although New World indigenous disease was mostly of the chronic and episodic kind, Old World diseases were largely acute and epidemic."
- Tuberculosis: Vågene 2022: "cases of pre-contact TB in North America begin to appear in the archaeological record after 900 CE, and the majority of affected individuals are located at inland sites in the midcontinent and southwestern USA." The paper's ancient DNA genomes (a seal-related strain, *M. pinnipedii*) all came from Peru and Colombia. It proposes the disease reached North America from South America's west coast along trade routes, and says this still needs testing on North American remains. Do not write that North American TB came from seals as a fact.
- Plain definition for the prose: tuberculosis is a disease caused by bacteria (*Mycobacterium tuberculosis*). It most often infects the lungs and can spread to bone, including the spine (standard medical definition, as in CDC "About Tuberculosis": not opened this pass, general knowledge).
- Treponemal diseases: Barquera 2025 abstract: human treponemal infections "give rise to the diseases yaws, bejel, pinta and, most notably, syphilis." Five pre- and peri-contact genomes from the Americas "support an American origin for all T. pallidum characterized at the genomic level, both modern and ancient." Newson 1993 (p. 260) lists "treponemal infections such as yaws, pinta and non-venereal syphilis" among the chronic infections that can persist in small communities. Martin & Goodman's own (2002) wording is "venereal and nonvenereal syphilis." Yaws and bejel spread mainly by skin and mouth contact, not by sex (WHO yaws fact sheet: general knowledge, not opened this pass).
- Diet and anemia: Martin & Goodman: "Iron deficiency anemia appears to have been widespread and ubiquitous in most ancient populations in the New World. The general distribution of the lesion corresponds with increasing reliance on agricultural products such as maize, which are low in bioavailable iron." Porotic hyperostosis is "lesions primarily found on the parietal and orbital bones of the cranium, produced by bone marrow proliferation diagnostic of anemia ... a raised, porous area." Lallo and co-workers studied "ancient Mississippians in Illinois living in the 12th century" (Dickson Mounds: reference 10 is Lallo, Armelagos and Rose, "Paleoepidemiology of infectious disease in the Dickson Mounds population," 1977) and found porotic hyperostosis "increased dramatically in the transition from hunting and gathering to agriculture." "The severity of periosteal reactions among Mississippian burials increased nearly fourfold during the period spanning the hunter-gatherer phase through intensive agriculture." (Periosteal reaction: new bone laid down on the outer surface of a bone in response to infection or injury.) Adult women had higher infection rates than men, and people with infections died younger.

**Diseases absent before contact.** Martin & Goodman: "Old World diseases that were not present in the Americas until contact include bubonic plague, measles, smallpox, mumps, chickenpox, influenza, cholera, diphtheria, typhus, malaria, leprosy, and yellow fever. Indians in the Americas had no acquired immunity to these infectious diseases, and these diseases caused what Crosby referred to as 'virgin soil epidemics,' in which all members of a population would be infected simultaneously." (Alfred W. Crosby Jr., "Virgin soil epidemics as a factor in the aboriginal depopulation in America," William and Mary Quarterly 33, 1976.)

**Why (the crowd-disease mechanism).** Newson 1993, p. 259-260: acute infections "have a short period of communicability, generally less than two weeks." A large population supplies new susceptible children, so the disease becomes endemic. "Bartlett (1957) has estimated that for measles to become endemic a population of 7,000 susceptibles out of a total population of between 200,000 and 300,000 is required, though Black (1975) would raise the latter figure to 500,000. Where settlements are small and dispersed, as among tribal groups, the spread of disease is slow and 'fade outs' are common." Newson also reports genetics research suggesting low diversity in the immune system of New World populations "may adversely affect disease susceptibility and provide a partial explanation" (Black 1990, 1992). It is a partial explanation and must be stated as one.

**Population in 1492 north of Mexico (disputed: give the range).** Newson 1993 Table 1, "North America": Steward 1949, 1,000,880. Rosenblat 1954, 1,000,000. Dobyns 1966, 9,800,000 to 12,250,000. Denevan 1992, 3,790,000. The parked native-nations material further down this file adds Ubelaker 1976, about 1.85 million, and Dobyns 1983, about 18 million. Newson: Dobyns (1966) estimated declines from contact to the low point, reached by many groups in the 1600s, "by a ratio of 20:1 or 25:1," and later research found much more variation by region.

**Healers and treatments before 1500.** No written record exists. What Native healers did is described in European accounts from the 1500s on (era 02: Cabeza de Vaca, Harriot) and in Native nations' own later teaching. The outline does not describe pre-1500 healing practices in detail for that reason. Do not project later practices back without saying where the account comes from.

**Cabeza de Vaca on the Texas coast, 1528 and 1529 (Hodge 1907, Smith translation).**
- About 80 men of the Narváez expedition reached the island (Malhado, "Isle of Misfortune", on or near Galveston Island) in November 1528 in two boats (outline date from `exploration`'s bank; Hodge text: "of eighty men who arrived in the two instances, fifteen only remained alive").
- Cannibalism among the Spaniards, stated in the text: "Five Christians, of a mess [quartered] on the coast, came to such extremity that they ate their dead; the body of the last one only was found unconsumed. Their names were Sierra, Diego Lopez, Corral, Palacios and Gonçalo Ruiz. This produced great commotion among the Indians."
- The epidemic, verbatim: "After this, the natives were visited by a disease of the bowels, of which half their number died. They conceived that we had destroyed them, and believing it firmly, they concerted among themselves to dispatch those of us who survived. When they were about to execute their purpose, an Indian who had charge of me, told them not to believe we were the cause of those deaths, since if we had such power we should also have averted the fatality from so many of our people, whom they had seen die without our being able to minister relief." No disease name, no count beyond "half their number."
- The island's peoples: "They speak two languages; those of one are called Capoques, those of the other, Han" (Lumen excerpt).
- Forced to heal: "they wished to make us physicians without examination or inquiring for diplomas. They cure by blowing upon the sick, and with that breath and the imposing of hands they cast out infirmity. ... In consequence, they withheld food from us until we should practice what they required." Native method: "The practitioner scarifies over the seat of pain, and then sucks about the wound. They make cauteries with fire, a remedy among them in high repute, which I have tried on myself and found benefit from it. They afterwards blow on the spot." The Spaniards' method: "to bless the sick, breathing upon them, and recite a Paternoster and an Ave-Maria." (Lumen excerpt.) Plain words for the prose: scarify = make small cuts in the skin. Cautery = burning the skin on purpose.
- The arrowhead operation, verbatim: a man "had been wounded by an arrow in the right shoulder, and that the point of the shaft was lodged above his heart ... With a knife I carried, I opened the breast to the place ... at last with great difficulty I drew the head forth. It was very large. With the bone of a deer ... I made two stitches that threw the blood over me, and with hair from a skin I stanched the flow. ... The next day I cut the two stitches and the Indian was well." The text does not give the year or the exact place. The walk west ran 1534 to 1536 (exploration's story). Hodge's footnotes around the passage (notes 168 to 171) place the travellers in central or west Texas (the Llano or Colorado River, the trans-Pecos). Route disputed: say so.

**The Gentleman of Elvas at Cofitachequi, spring 1540 (Hodge 1907).** Verbatim: "About the place, from half a league to a league off, were large vacant towns, grown up in grass, that appeared as if no people had lived in them for a long time. The Indians said that, two years before, there had been a pest in the land, and the inhabitants had moved away to other towns." Hodge's note 255 spells the town Cutifachiqui and, from Ranjel's narrative, puts it on the west side of the Savannah River, "not at Silver Bluff, South Carolina, as commonly thought, but further down the river." Later scholars place it elsewhere (a location near Camden, South Carolina, is often cited: not opened this pass). The outline says "South Carolina or Georgia" and notes the disagreement. The disease is unnamed and uncounted. "Two years before" puts it about 1538, before de Soto arrived.

**Hariot at Roanoke, 1585 and 1586 (Hariot 1588 and EV).**
- Who: born about 1560, died 1621, Oxford graduate 1580, lecturer on open-ocean navigation at Raleigh's Durham House. Joined the expedition led by Sir Richard Grenville, which arrived at Roanoke Island on 26 June 1585. He and John White "explored and mapped the area." The 1588 edition was small, without pictures. Theodor de Bry's 1590 illustrated edition "went through seventeen editions between 1590 and 1620" (EV entry).
- The deaths, verbatim (Hariot 1588): "within a few dayes after our departure from euerie such towne, the people began to die very fast, and many in short space; in some townes about twentie, in some fourtie, in some sixtie, & in one sixe score, which in trueth was very manie in respect of their numbers. This happened in no place that wee could learne but where wee had bene ... The disease also so strange, that they neither knew what it was, nor how to cure it; the like by the report of the oldest men in the countrey neuer happened before, time out of minde." (Six score = 120.)
- The English: "all the space of their sicknesse, there was no man of ours knowne to die, or that was specially sicke."
- Wingina: "the Wiroans Wingina had obserued such effects in foure or fiue towns"; he and other friends of the English "were preswaded that it was the worke of our God through our meanes, and that wee by him might kil and slai whom we would without weapons." They asked the English to pray that their enemies would die. Hariot answered that such requests were "vngodlie." When the enemies died, they came "to giue vs thankes." Wingina himself: "Twise this Wiroans was so greiuously sicke that he was like to die," and sent for the English to pray for him.
- Algonquian explanations: some thought the English "were not borne of women, and therefore not mortall"; some said beings "inuisible & without bodies ... did make the people to die ... by shooting inuisible bullets into them." "Their phisitions ... [said] that the strings of blood that they sucked out of the sicke bodies, were the strings wherewithal the inuisible bullets were tied and cast." Hariot calls the healers ignorant in the same sentence. The outline does not repeat his judgment. EV: Hariot thought it "the speciall worke of God for our sakes."
- Medicine: uppowoc (tobacco): "they vse to take the fume or smoke thereof by sucking it through pipes made of claie into their stomacke and heade; from whence it purgeth superfluous fleame ... wherby their bodies are notably preserued in health, & know not many greeuous diseases wherewithall wee in England are oftentimes afflicted." Also wapeih, a clay earth the inhabitants "vse ... very much for the cure of sores and woundes."
- What the disease was: Mires 1994 abstract: comparing Hariot and John Smith "reveals that the Roanoke colonists were the vectors of epidemic disease. The disease was probably influenza"; Smith "heard stories of a devastating epidemic that had ravaged the Accomac, one of the groups visited by the Roanoke colonists during the winter of 1585/86." A search-result summary adds Mires's reasons (spread person to person, only villages the English visited, fast killing, no rash or pox) (unconfirmed: search summary only).
- Not used: Wingina's killing by Lane's men in June 1586 (a `native-nations` / `war` subject, not opened this pass).

**The "Great Dying" framing.** Newson 1993 (era 01 sources): "they were afflicted not once but repeatedly," and falling birth rates also cut populations. The 1500s record in this land is scattered eyewitness accounts. The whole-continent collapse is told through the population range (era 01) and native-nations' span.

## Parked from Ch27 Elements research (2026-07-23) — radiation and health; elements in medicine
Ch27 tells the elements' stories; what radiation does to bodies, and how elements heal or harm, belongs here. Facts source-checked in `research/research-elements.md`.

### 8. 1900 to 1950
- **Radium poisoning — the science of what happened to the Radium Girls.** Radium behaves chemically like calcium, so swallowed radium (from lip-pointing paint brushes, from 1917 at the dial factories) settles into bone, where its radiation kills bone tissue and causes anemia, "radium jaw" (necrosis of the jaw), fractures, and cancers. The dial painters' deaths in the 1920s–30s produced some of the first hard knowledge of internal radiation poisoning, and their bodies were studied for decades to set radiation-safety limits for later workers (including Manhattan Project workers). The court fight → Ch17; the element craze → Ch27; the medicine and dosimetry → here. (Sources: History.com; Library of Congress "Radium Girls: Living Dead Women"; NJ historic-register documentation.)
- **Radioactive patent medicine: Radithor.** Radium dissolved in water, sold as a cure-all; industrialist Eben Byers drank ~1,400 bottles (1927–30) and died of radium poisoning in 1932 — his death helped push federal action against radioactive quack cures. (Caution: the famous "jaw came off" headline is a 1990 Wall Street Journal retrospective, not from 1932.) (Sources: Wikipedia Eben Byers/Radithor cross-checked; FDA/AMA history references to VERIFY when Ch18 is worked.)
- **The radium craze as health fad:** radium tonics, cosmetics, and glow products marketed as vitality itself in the 1910s–20s — a case study in medicine before drug regulation (ties to the 1938 Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act — VERIFY the regulatory line when Ch18 is worked).

### 9. 1950 to 2000
- **Technetium-99m — the artificial element that scans hearts.** Technetium, the first man-made element (1937, Ch27), gave medicine its workhorse imaging isotope: Tc-99m, used in tens of millions of nuclear-medicine scans worldwide each year (bone, heart, kidney imaging). A strong "element made by people, healing people" story; get current usage numbers from IAEA/SNMMI sources when Ch18 is worked. (Sources: LANL periodic table on Tc medical use.)
- **Radioisotopes from the labs:** Oak Ridge shipped medical radioisotopes from the late 1940s onward — the reactor-to-hospital pipeline (institutional side → Ch6). VERIFY specifics when Ch18 is worked.
