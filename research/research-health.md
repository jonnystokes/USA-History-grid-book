- **Newson 1993:** Linda A. Newson, "The Demographic Collapse of Native Peoples of the Americas, 1492-1650," *Proceedings of the British Academy* 81 (1993), 247-288, https://www.thebritishacademy.ac.uk/documents/4002/81p247.pdf (PDF downloaded and read).

How the record works: no written records of illness exist from before 1500 in this land. Martin & Goodman: "data on health in ancient societies are inferred from the analysis of a wide range of archaeologic materials, with human bones and teeth forming the largest body of evidence."

**Diseases present before contact.**
- Martin & Goodman: "Diseases such as treponemiasis and tuberculosis were already present in the New World, along with diseases such as tularemia, giardia, rabies, amebic dysentery, hepatitis, herpes, pertussis, and poliomyelitis, although the prevalence of almost all of these was probably low in any given group." And: "Osteologic data demonstrate that native groups were most definitely not living in a pristine, disease-free environment before contact. Although New World indigenous disease was mostly of the chronic and episodic kind, Old World diseases were largely acute and epidemic."
- Tuberculosis: Vågene 2022: "cases of pre-contact TB in North America begin to appear in the archaeological record after 900 CE, and the majority of affected individuals are located at inland sites in the midcontinent and southwestern USA." The paper's ancient DNA genomes (a seal-related strain, *M. pinnipedii*) all came from Peru and Colombia. It proposes the disease reached North America from South America's west coast along trade routes, and says this still needs testing on North American remains. Do not write that North American TB came from seals as a fact.
- Plain definition for the prose: tuberculosis is a disease caused by bacteria (*Mycobacterium tuberculosis*). It most often infects the lungs and can spread to bone, including the spine (standard medical definition, as in CDC "About Tuberculosis": not opened this pass, general knowledge).
- Treponemal diseases: Barquera 2025 abstract: human treponemal infections "give rise to the diseases yaws, bejel, pinta and, most notably, syphilis." Five pre- and peri-contact genomes from the Americas "support an American origin for all T. pallidum characterized at the genomic level, both modern and ancient." Newson 1993 (p. 260) lists "treponemal infections such as yaws, pinta and non-venereal syphilis" among the chronic infections that can persist in small communities. Martin & Goodman's own (2002) wording is "venereal and nonvenereal syphilis." Yaws and bejel spread mainly by skin and mouth contact, not by sex (WHO yaws fact sheet: general knowledge, not opened this pass).
- Diet and anemia: Martin & Goodman: "Iron deficiency anemia appears to have been widespread and ubiquitous in most ancient populations in the New World. The general distribution of the lesion corresponds with increasing reliance on agricultural products such as maize, which are low in bioavailable iron." Porotic hyperostosis is "lesions primarily found on the parietal and orbital bones of the cranium, produced by bone marrow proliferation diagnostic of anemia ... a raised, porous area." Lallo and co-workers studied "ancient Mississippians in Illinois living in the 12th century" (Dickson Mounds: reference 10 is Lallo, Armelagos and Rose, "Paleoepidemiology of infectious disease in the Dickson Mounds population," 1977) and found porotic hyperostosis "increased dramatically in the transition from hunting and gathering to agriculture." "The severity of periosteal reactions among Mississippian burials increased nearly fourfold during the period spanning the hunter-gatherer phase through intensive agriculture." (Periosteal reaction: new bone laid down on the outer surface of a bone in response to infection or injury.) Adult women had higher infection rates than men, and people with infections died younger.

**Diseases absent before contact.** Martin & Goodman: "Old World diseases that were not present in the Americas until contact include bubonic plague, measles, smallpox, mumps, chickenpox, influenza, cholera, diphtheria, typhus, malaria, leprosy, and yellow fever. Indians in the Americas had no acquired immunity to these infectious diseases, and these diseases caused what Crosby referred to as 'virgin soil epidemics,' in which all members of a population would be infected simultaneously." (Alfred W. Crosby Jr., "Virgin soil epidemics as a factor in the aboriginal depopulation in America," William and Mary Quarterly 33, 1976.)

**Why (the crowd-disease mechanism).** Newson 1993, p. 259-260: acute infections "have a short period of communicability, generally less than two weeks." A large population supplies new susceptible children, so the disease becomes endemic. "Bartlett (1957) has estimated that for measles to become endemic a population of 7,000 susceptibles out of a total population of between 200,000 and 300,000 is required, though Black (1975) would raise the latter figure to 500,000. Where settlements are small and dispersed, as among tribal groups, the spread of disease is slow and 'fade outs' are common." Newson also reports genetics research suggesting low diversity in the immune system of New World populations "may adversely affect disease susceptibility and provide a partial explanation" (Black 1990, 1992). It is a partial explanation and must be stated as one.

**Population in 1492 north of Mexico (disputed: give the range).** Newson 1993 Table 1, "North America": Steward 1949, 1,000,880. Rosenblat 1954, 1,000,000. Dobyns 1966, 9,800,000 to 12,250,000. Denevan 1992, 3,790,000. The parked native-nations material further down this file adds Ubelaker 1976, about 1.85 million, and Dobyns 1983, about 18 million. Newson: Dobyns (1966) estimated declines from contact to the low point, reached by many groups in the 1600s, "by a ratio of 20:1 or 25:1," and later research found much more variation by region.

**Healers and treatments before 1500.** No written record exists. What Native healers did is described in European accounts from the 1500s on (era 02: Cabeza de Vaca, Harriot) and in Native nations' own later teaching. The outline does not describe pre-1500 healing practices in detail for that reason. Do not project later practices back without saying where the account comes from.

**Cabeza de Vaca on the Texas coast, 1528 and 1529 (Hodge 1907, Smith translation).**
- About 80 men of the Narváez expedition reached the island (Malhado, "Isle of Misfortune", on or near Galveston Island) in November 1528 in two boats (outline date from `exploration`'s bank; Hodge text: "of eighty men who arrived in the two instances, fifteen only remained alive").
- Cannibalism among the Spaniards, stated in the text: "Five Christians, of a mess [quartered] on the coast, came to such extremity that they ate their dead; the body of the last one only was found unconsumed. Their names were Sierra, Diego Lopez, Corral, Palacios and Gonçalo Ruiz. This produced great commotion among the Indians."
- The epidemic, verbatim: "After this, the natives were visited by a disease of the bowels, of which half their number died. They conceived that we had destroyed them, and believing it firmly, they concerted among themselves to dispatch those of us who survived. When they were about to execute their purpose, an Indian who had charge of me, told them not to believe we were the cause of those deaths, since if we had such power we should also have averted the fatality from so many of our people, whom they had seen die without our being able to minister relief." No disease name, no count beyond "half their number."
- The island's peoples: "They speak two languages; those of one are called Capoques, those of the other, Han" (Lumen excerpt).
- Forced to heal: "they wished to make us physicians without examination or inquiring for diplomas. They cure by blowing upon the sick, and with that breath and the imposing of hands they cast out infirmity. ... In consequence, they withheld food from us until we should practice what they required." Native method: "The practitioner scarifies over the seat of pain, and then sucks about the wound. They make cauteries with fire, a remedy among them in high repute, which I have tried on myself and found benefit from it. They afterwards blow on the spot." The Spaniards' method: "to bless the sick, breathing upon them, and recite a Paternoster and an Ave-Maria." (Lumen excerpt.) Plain words for the prose: scarify = make small cuts in the skin. Cautery = burning the skin on purpose.
- The arrowhead operation, verbatim: a man "had been wounded by an arrow in the right shoulder, and that the point of the shaft was lodged above his heart ... With a knife I carried, I opened the breast to the place ... at last with great difficulty I drew the head forth. It was very large. With the bone of a deer ... I made two stitches that threw the blood over me, and with hair from a skin I stanched the flow. ... The next day I cut the two stitches and the Indian was well." The text does not give the year or the exact place. The walk west ran 1534 to 1536 (exploration's story). Hodge's footnotes around the passage (notes 168 to 171) place the travellers in central or west Texas (the Llano or Colorado River, the trans-Pecos). Route disputed: say so.

**Hariot at Roanoke, 1585 and 1586 (Hariot 1588 and EV).**
- Who: born about 1560, died 1621, Oxford graduate 1580, lecturer on open-ocean navigation at Raleigh's Durham House. Joined the expedition led by Sir Richard Grenville, which arrived at Roanoke Island on 26 June 1585. He and John White "explored and mapped the area." The 1588 edition was small, without pictures. Theodor de Bry's 1590 illustrated edition "went through seventeen editions between 1590 and 1620" (EV entry).
- The deaths, verbatim (Hariot 1588): "within a few dayes after our departure from euerie such towne, the people began to die very fast, and many in short space; in some townes about twentie, in some fourtie, in some sixtie, & in one sixe score, which in trueth was very manie in respect of their numbers. This happened in no place that wee could learne but where wee had bene ... The disease also so strange, that they neither knew what it was, nor how to cure it; the like by the report of the oldest men in the countrey neuer happened before, time out of minde." (Six score = 120.)
- The English: "all the space of their sicknesse, there was no man of ours knowne to die, or that was specially sicke."
- Wingina: "the Wiroans Wingina had obserued such effects in foure or fiue towns"; he and other friends of the English "were preswaded that it was the worke of our God through our meanes, and that wee by him might kil and slai whom we would without weapons." They asked the English to pray that their enemies would die. Hariot answered that such requests were "vngodlie." When the enemies died, they came "to giue vs thankes." Wingina himself: "Twise this Wiroans was so greiuously sicke that he was like to die," and sent for the English to pray for him.
- Algonquian explanations: some thought the English "were not borne of women, and therefore not mortall"; some said beings "inuisible & without bodies ... did make the people to die ... by shooting inuisible bullets into them." "Their phisitions ... [said] that the strings of blood that they sucked out of the sicke bodies, were the strings wherewithal the inuisible bullets were tied and cast." Hariot calls the healers ignorant in the same sentence. The outline does not repeat his judgment. EV: Hariot thought it "the speciall worke of God for our sakes."
- Medicine: uppowoc (tobacco): "they vse to take the fume or smoke thereof by sucking it through pipes made of claie into their stomacke and heade; from whence it purgeth superfluous fleame ... wherby their bodies are notably preserued in health, & know not many greeuous diseases wherewithall wee in England are oftentimes afflicted." Also wapeih, a clay earth the inhabitants "vse ... very much for the cure of sores and woundes."
- What the disease was: Mires 1994 abstract: comparing Hariot and John Smith "reveals that the Roanoke colonists were the vectors of epidemic disease. The disease was probably influenza"; Smith "heard stories of a devastating epidemic that had ravaged the Accomac, one of the groups visited by the Roanoke colonists during the winter of 1585/86." A search-result summary adds Mires's reasons (spread person to person, only villages the English visited, fast killing, no rash or pox) (unconfirmed: search summary only).
- Not used: Wingina's killing by Lane's men in June 1586 (a `native-nations` / `war` subject, not opened this pass).

**New England coast, 1616 to 1619 (Marr & Cathey 2010; Bradford).**
- Marr & Cathey: "During 1616-1619, many persons died of a disease that presumably spared nearby European fishermen and traders." It reached "two hundred miles along the coast" of southern New England to Cape Cod and about 15 miles inland. "The Patuxet (Plimouth) Native American village was severely depopulated."
- Counts, as the article reports them (all estimates): the Wampanoag Nation before the epidemic, 21,000 to 24,000 (Salisbury), and the Patuxet about 2,000 (Salisbury). "Estimated death rates (which lack reliable numerator and denominator data) range from one third of the local population to as high as 90%" (Cook). No count of the dead exists.
- Symptoms: Daniel Gookin, writing in 1674, recorded what Native people told him: "the bodies all over were exceedingly yellow, describing it by a yellow garment they showed me, both before they died and afterwards." Marr & Cathey add "headache and fever with visible signs of epistaxis [nosebleed] and jaundice [yellow skin]."
- What the disease was: disputed. Diseases other scholars have proposed, listed by Marr & Cathey: yellow fever, smallpox, plague, influenza, chickenpox, typhus, typhoid fever, trichinosis, cerebrospinal meningitis, and a combined hepatitis B and D infection. Their own proposal is leptospirosis with Weil syndrome, a bacterial infection carried in the urine of rats. They propose that rats from European ships infected local animals and contaminated land and fresh water. They state: "Our hypothesis is not meant to be a definite answer but a heuristic for others to criticize and explore." The outline must present it as one proposal among several.
- Bradford (1621, on Massasoit's country): "his people had died in great numbers during the recent plague throughout these parts, about three years before the coming of the English. Thousands of them died, until the living were not able to bury the dead, and their skulls and bones were found in many places lying still above ground, where their houses and dwelling places had been." The Narragansetts across the bay "had not been attacked by this wasting plague."
- Bradford on Squanto (Tisquantum): "a native of these parts, and had been one of the few survivors of the plague hereabouts." He had been carried off by Captain Thomas Hunt, who meant to sell him and others as slaves in Spain. Bradford also wrote that Squanto made other Native people "believe the English kept the plague buried in the ground, and could send it among them whenever they wished." **Jamestown, 1607 (EV Jamestown; Percy).** The fort stood in Paspahegh territory (Wikipedia, "Paspahegh", opened: the Paspahegh were a member nation of the Powhatan paramount chiefdom from about 1596 or 1597).
- 104 men and boys arrived in 1607. By the end of September 1607, "half of Jamestown's 104 men and boys are dead, mostly from sickness" (EV Jamestown).
- Percy's death list names each man and a cause: "The sixt of August there died John Asbie of the bloudie Flixe. The ninth day died George Flowre of the swelling. The tenth day died William Bruster Gentleman, of a wound given by the Savages ..." Captain Bartholomew Gosnold, a member of the council, died on 22 August 1607.
- Percy's summary: "Our men were destroyed with cruell diseases, as Swellings, Flixes, Burning Fevers, and by warres, and some departed suddenly, but for the most part they died of meere famine." Food: "a small Can of Barlie sod in water, to five men a day." Water: "cold water taken out of the River, which was at a floud verie salt, at a low tide full of slime and filth, which was the destruction of many of our men." "Not having five able men to man our Bulwarkes." "Many times three or foure in a night; in the morning, their bodies trailed out of their Cabines like Dogges to be buried."
- Plain words: a flux was the old word for diarrhea. A "bloody flux" was diarrhea with blood in it, the illness now called dysentery (standard definition, not opened this pass). Percy's "swelling" is not identified in the text. Do not guess a modern disease for it.
- Relief: Percy: "those people which were our mortall enemies" brought "Bread, Corne, Fish, and Flesh in great plentie," and "divers Kings in the Countrie" brought more. Percy does not name the leaders.
- The Starving Time, winter 1609 to 1610: "only 60 of about 240 colonists in the fort had survived" (EV Jamestown). `disasters` and `food-farming` lead this. Health mentions it only as a count.

**Plymouth, winter 1620 to 1621 (Bradford).** Plymouth stood on the site of the Patuxet town emptied by the 1616-1619 epidemic (Marr & Cathey: "The Patuxet (Plimouth) Native American village").
- "In two or three months' time half of their company died, partly owing to the severity of the winter, especially during January and February, and the want of houses and other comforts; partly to scurvy and other diseases, which their long voyage and their incommodious quarters had brought upon them. Of all the hundred odd persons, scarcely fifty remained, and sometimes two or three persons died in a day."
- "In the time of worst distress, there were but six or seven sound persons," who "fetched wood, made fires, prepared food for the sick, made their beds, washed their infected clothes, dressed and undressed them." Two of them were William Brewster and Myles Standish.
- Plain words: scurvy is the disease caused by too little vitamin C over weeks or months. Gums swell and bleed, teeth loosen, bruises appear, and the person grows too weak to work (standard medical definition, as in NIH Office of Dietary Supplements "Vitamin C" fact sheet: not opened this pass, general knowledge).

**Samuel Fuller, Plymouth's physician (Wikipedia; Mayflower Society; American Ancestors; Bradford).**
- Baptized 20 January 1580 at Redenhall, Norfolk, England. In Leiden he was recorded as "a serge worker of London" (a weaver of wool cloth) (Wikipedia). He came on the Mayflower in 1620. His brother Edward and Edward's wife died in the first winter (Wikipedia; Bradford's passenger list: "Mr. and Mrs. Fuller died soon after they came ashore").
- Training: the Mayflower Society calls him "a self-taught physician." Wikipedia says he "may have tried to learn the rudiments of medical knowledge," knowing the ship would carry no doctor (hedged in the source: say "no record shows he trained as a doctor").
- Salem, 1629: settlers who came with John Endicott fell sick of scurvy and "an infectious fever" brought on the ships. Endicott, "understanding that one of them at New Plymouth had some skill as a physician, and had cured several of scurvy, and others of various diseases, by letting blood and other means," asked Bradford to send him (Bradford). Endicott's letter of thanks is dated "Naumkeag, May 11th, 1629."
- Charlestown, 1630: he treated the newly arrived Massachusetts Bay colonists, "letting blood ... for some 20 persons" (Wikipedia; American Ancestors cites Bradford's letter book, pp. 56-59).
- Plain words: letting blood (bloodletting) meant cutting a vein, most often in the arm, and letting blood run out into a bowl. Doctors believed it balanced the body's fluids. It does not cure scurvy or fever and weakens the patient (standard medical history, not opened this pass: state the method and that it did not cure).
- 1633: "an infectious fever of which many fell very ill, and upwards of twenty persons died, men, women and children." "In the end, after he had helped others much, Samuel Fuller, their physician" died of it. "This disease also swept away many of the Indians from the adjoining parts" (Bradford). **Smallpox on the Connecticut River, 1634 (Bradford, chapter XV, "Smallpox among the Indians: 1634").** Plymouth's trading house stood on the Connecticut River (its site at Windsor, Connecticut, is general knowledge, not opened this pass: the outline says only "on the Connecticut River").

Bradford does not name the nation or the disease for this fort. The count is his.

The body: "As they lie on their hard mats, the pox breaks and matters and runs, their skin sticking to the mats they lie on, so that when they turn a whole side will flay off at once, and they will be all one gore of blood." Survivors often had "permanent scars over large areas of their body, especially their faces." CDC's rate describes the disease in general. **New Netherland: midwives, surgeons and a hospital (Amerman 1957; NYHM 1644).** New Amsterdam stood on Manhattan, in the homeland of the Munsee Lenape. Dutch officials claimed to buy the island from Lenape people in 1626 (Smithsonian Magazine, "The True Native New Yorkers Can Never Truly Reclaim Their Homeland," 2018, unconfirmed: search summary only).

In 1638 Lysbert Dircksen held the town midwife post and "lived in a house built for her at public expense." In 1644 Tryntje Jonas was the official midwife with "her own house on Pearl Street." After Tryntje Jonas died, her daughter Anneke Jans "collected arrears of pay from the Company for services rendered." Hellegond (Hillegont) Joris later held a midwife appointment and "served many years in that capacity" (Amerman 1957).

An ordinance of 1652 ruled that ship barbers "shall not be allowed to dress any wounds" on shore without consent. An ordinance of 1657 required surgeons to "ascertain and report" how a wounded person was hurt. In 1658 Jacob Hendrichsen Varvanger and Kierstede performed what Amerman calls "the first recorded coroner's inquest in America" (unverified "first": attribute it to Amerman).
- Hospital, 1658 to 1659: Varvanger, the Company surgeon, asked for "a proper place for the reception of such patients." The Council agreed. Hilletje Wilbruch became matron at "a regular yearly stipend of 100 florins" (Amerman 1957). Amerman calls it the first hospital. Treat "first" as his claim.

**Smallpox in Boston and Thacher's broadside, 1677 and 1678 (Countway; NLM).**

**Boston smallpox and inoculation, 1721.**
- Arrival: the merchant ship *Seahorse* arrived from Barbados on 22 April 1721 with sick sailors (Wikipedia 1721).
- Population and counts, with the disagreement: Boston had about 10,600 people (Wikipedia 1721). Cases: "almost 5800" (MHS 2021), 5,759 (Wikipedia 1721). Deaths: 844 (both). Paul Revere House gives a 14 percent death rate among people who caught it the natural way. Wikipedia 1721 gives 15 percent.
- Inoculation, also called variolation: MHS: "transferring active smallpox virus from an infected person to a healthy person." Wikipedia 1721: "drying pus from a smallpox patient and rubbing or scraping it into a healthy person's skin." Mass Moments: Boylston put smallpox matter "into scratches on their skin, following African and Turkish methods." The inoculated person "usually got a mild case of the disease and soon recovered" (History.com, unconfirmed: search summary only). Mather's letter (below) records Onesimus's claim that it would "forever preserve him from it." That an inoculated person could still spread smallpox to others is general knowledge, not opened this pass: do not use without a source.
- Boylston's first three, 26 June 1721, in his own words: "my Son Thomas, of about six, my Negro Man, Jack, thrity six, and Jackey, two an half Years old" (Mass Moments quoting Boylston 1726). MHS: "his 6 year old son, his 36 year old enslaved person Jack, and Jack's son." Symptoms came on day 6 or 7. Boylston gave a "vomit" on day 9 (a medicine to make them throw up). Thomas and Jack each had about "an hundred" pustules (blisters of pus). All three recovered (Mass Moments). Jack and Jackey were enslaved by Boylston. No source opened says whether Jack agreed. Write that the record does not say.
- Totals, with the disagreement: Boylston inoculated 242 (Paul Revere House), 247 (Wikipedia 1721) or 248 (Mass Moments). Six of them died (all). About 287 people in all were inoculated in the epidemic, six of whom died (MHS 2021). One in 40 died after inoculation, against about one in seven who caught it naturally (search summary; the ratio follows from 6 of 242 and 14 percent).
- Opposition: William Douglass, a physician, led the opposition and called inoculation a "poisonous humor" (Wikipedia 1721, MHS 2021). James Franklin's *New-England Courant*, founded August 1721, printed attacks on it (Wikipedia 1721). Boston's selectmen condemned inoculation in August and ordered Boylston to stop. He started again two days later (Wikipedia 1721). "Zabdiel Boylston was unable to leave his farm lest he be assaulted" (Mass Moments).
- The bomb: someone threw a crude bomb through a window of Cotton Mather's house (MHS 2021, Paul Revere House). The note, as Wikipedia quotes it: "Cotton Mather, I was once of your meeting, but the cursed lye you told of - you know who, made me leave you, you dog, and damn you, I will inoculate you with this, with a pox on you!" Date and thrower: not in an opened source. Do not name a thrower.
- After: Boylston went to London, was made a Fellow of the Royal Society, and published *An Historical Account of the Small-Pox Inoculated in New England* (London, 1726) (Mass Moments). Born 1679, died 1766 (MHS 2021).

**Onesimus (Wikipedia Onesimus; Paul Revere House; MHS 2021).**
- Members of Mather's North Church congregation gave Onesimus to Cotton Mather in December 1706 (Wikipedia). Paul Revere House says 1707. Mather renamed him after an enslaved man in the Bible. The name means "useful" or "profitable" (Wikipedia). His birth name is not known.
- Mather's 1716 letter to the Royal Society of London, verbatim as quoted in search results and consistent with Paul Revere House and Wikipedia: "Enquiring of my Negro-man Onesimus, who is a pretty Intelligent Fellow, Whether he ever had the Small-Pox; he answered, both, Yes, and No; and then told me, that he had undergone an Operation, which had given him something of the Small-Pox, and would forever preserve him from it, adding that it was often used among the Guramantese, & whoever had the Courage to use it, was forever free from the Fear of the Contagion." Onesimus showed Mather the scar on his arm. Onesimus's description, as Mather wrote it: "People take Juice of Small-Pox; and Cut the Skin, and put in a drop" (Wikipedia).
- "Guramantese": probably the Coromantee, Akan people of the Gold Coast (modern Ghana) (Wikipedia: "likely").
- Family: he married and had two children who died before age ten, a son, Onesimulus (died 1714), and a daughter, Katy (died of consumption, the old name for tuberculosis) (Wikipedia).
- Freedom, 1716: Onesimus paid money toward the purchase of another enslaved man, Obadiah, to take his place. Mather's conditions: Onesimus had to keep doing work for the household when called (Paul Revere House names shoveling snow and chopping wood) and repay five pounds Mather said he had stolen (Wikipedia; Paul Revere House). After 1716 the records of his life are scarce (Paul Revere House).
- Mather's own route to inoculation: MHS 2021 says Mather also read accounts of inoculation in Turkey in the Royal Society's *Philosophical Transactions* in 1716. Tell both: Onesimus's account came first, by Mather's own statement ("Many months before I met with any intimations ... in Europe; I had from a servant of my own an account of its being practised in Africa," Paul Revere House).

**The throat distemper, 1735 to 1740 (NEHS; Historic Ipswich).**
- It began in Kingston, New Hampshire, in the spring (May) of 1735 and spread along the coast into Maine and Massachusetts. Modern physicians identify it as diphtheria. People at the time sometimes confused it with scarlet fever (NEHS).
- What it did to the body (NEHS): "a very sore throat," then loss of appetite and fever. The bacteria "attacked the throat, nose and lungs, poisoning the tissue and killing it off. The coating of dead tissue eventually interfered with breathing and killed the sufferer." (Plain: a thick grey layer of dead tissue grew across the throat and choked the child.)
- Counts (Caulfield, via NEHS and Historic Ipswich): about 5,000 dead in New England, 1735 to 1740, more than three quarters of them children. Hampton Falls, New Hampshire, by 16 July 1736: 210 dead, 160 of them under ten (Historic Ipswich).
- Treatments printed in the *Boston Gazette*: opening veins (bloodletting), borax or honey on the throat, borax plasters, herbal drinks (NEHS).
- The How (Howe) family of Linebrook parish, Ipswich, Massachusetts: Mark and Hephzibah Howe lost eight children in November 1736: Lucy (9), 5 November. Mary (7), 15 November. Hannah (13) and Aaron (5), 18 November. Abijah (1) and Mark (2), 21 November. Moses (11), 25 November. Love (12), November. The cause recorded was "cancre quinsy" (a blocked throat). They are buried in the Leslie Road Burying Ground (Historic Ipswich). Date disagreement: NEHS says November 1735. Historic Ipswich gives the day-by-day dates in 1736. Use 1736 with the Ipswich list.
- Other Ipswich families: Michael Farley lost five children in April 1736, four in one week. John Abbott lost eight. Nathaniel Cross lost seven in about a month (Historic Ipswich).

**Smallpox among the Cherokee and Catawba, 1738 to 1739 (NCpedia; Wikipedia 1738).**

Wikipedia: 900 Cherokees joined the British against the Spanish in Florida in 1739. Historians have criticized Adair's account (Wikipedia). State all three routes as proposals.

NCpedia states the suicides without naming a source. Attribute them to Adair.

**Doctor Caesar, 1749 and 1750 (Butler 2021).**
- Caesar was enslaved by "Mr. John Norman of Beach Hill" in St. Paul's Parish, near the Edisto River, in what is now Dorchester County, South Carolina. He treated people for poisoning and rattlesnake bite.
- Why the colony paid: white South Carolinians feared that the enslaved people who cooked their food would poison them. A 1740 law made poisoning "a felony that was punishable by death."
- November 1749: the Commons House of Assembly voted to buy his freedom for his cure. The act appropriated "£500 ... to Mr. John Norman for the freedom of a negro man named Caesar," ratified 31 May 1750. Caesar was also promised £100 a year for life and received "the first instalment" in 1751.
- The cure (printed in the *South Carolina Gazette*, May 1750): "Plantain roots, horehound, goldenrod, rum, sugar, tobacco, and wood ash lye," boiled together in set amounts, for poisoning and snakebite (Butler). The exact recipe for each use is not copied here. The cure was reprinted in London's *Gentleman's Magazine* (August 1750) and in later medical books (search summary: CCPL, unconfirmed beyond the search result).
- The same assembly then restricted Black healers. The 1751 law: "no negroes or other slaves (commonly called doctors,) shall hereafter be suffered or permitted to administer any medicine" without white permission (Butler quoting the statute).
- Death: early 1754, about age 77. His estate was worth £62 and included one enslaved woman. His wife Lilly and daughter Hannah remained enslaved by John Norman (Butler). The record does not say why the assembly freed Caesar and not his wife and daughter.

**Pennsylvania Hospital, 1751 (Wikipedia PH; Mütter 2021).**
- Founded (chartered) 11 May 1751 by Dr. Thomas Bond and Benjamin Franklin. Bond had studied medicine in Europe (Mütter 2021). A search summary gives 11 March 1751 for the charter (unconfirmed). Use 11 May 1751, the date both opened sources give.
- Purpose: to care for "the poor, sick and insane." Franklin's petition described people "deprived of their rational Faculties" who were "a Terror to their Neighbors" and could be "contained and confined" there (Mütter 2021).

The sources disagree: give 1752 and note the other date. Permanent building at 8th and Pine: cornerstone 1755, patients moved 17 December 1756 (Wikipedia PH). Wikipedia PH calls it "the second public hospital in the United States (after only Bellevue Hospital)." **The first medical school, 1765 (Mütter 2021).** The medical school of the College of Philadelphia (later the University of Pennsylvania), founded 1765 by Dr. John Morgan, is "the first medical college in the colonies." Pennsylvania Hospital's patients were used for bedside teaching.

**Smallpox as a weapon at Fort Pitt, 1763 (Trent; d'Errico; Wikipedia Fort Pitt).**
- Context: in 1763 Delaware (Lenape), Shawnee and Mingo warriors, in the war led by the Ottawa leader Pontiac, besieged Fort Pitt, where the Allegheny and Monongahela rivers meet (now Pittsburgh). Smallpox had broken out among the British garrison.
- The act: at a parley on 24 June 1763, Captain Simeon Ecuyer, the fort's commander, and the trader William Trent gave two Delaware men, Turtle's Heart and Mamaltee, items from the fort's smallpox hospital. Trent's journal: "Out of our regard to them we gave them two Blankets and an Handkerchief out of the Small Pox Hospital. I hope it will have the desired effect." Date note: d'Errico's transcription dates the entry 24 May 1763. Wikipedia and historians date the parley 24 June 1763. Use June and note the other.
- The bill: Levy, Trent and Company billed the army for "2 Blankets @ 20/ ... 1 Silk Handkerchef ... & 1 linnen do:", an account dated 13 August 1763 and signed by Ecuyer (Wikipedia Fort Pitt). Its wording, "to Replace in kind those which were taken from people in the Hospital to Convey the Smallpox to the Indians" (HistoryNet via search result: unconfirmed: search summary only).
- The generals: General Jeffery Amherst, British commander in North America, wrote to Colonel Henry Bouquet: "Could it not be contrived to send the Small Pox among those disaffected tribes of Indians?" Bouquet answered on 13 July 1763: "I will try to inocculate the Indians by means of Blankets." Amherst also wrote of trying "to extirpate this Execrable Race" (d'Errico). Letter dates: d'Errico gives 16 July for Amherst's question. Wikipedia gives 8 July. Record both. The fort's blanket gift in June came before these letters and was "carried out independently" (Wikipedia).
- Did it work? A month later Trent met the same two men, who had not visibly caught smallpox (Wikipedia). Gershom Hicks reported in spring 1764 that smallpox "had been raging for some time" among the Native nations (Parkman, via d'Errico), and testified that about 100 people died of it between spring 1763 and April 1764 (Wikipedia). Elizabeth Fenn: "the actual effectiveness of an attempt to spread smallpox remains impossible to ascertain" (Wikipedia). **The Doctors' Riot, New York, 1788 (Lovejoy 2014).**
- Medical students took corpses for dissection (cutting a body open to study it) from the Negroes Burial Ground and the potter's field (the graveyard for the poor).
- February 1788: free and enslaved Black New Yorkers petitioned the Common Council that "young gentlemen in this city who call themselves students of the physic" were digging up "bodies of our deceased friends and relatives" without "respect for age or sex." **Yellow fever in Philadelphia, 1793.**
- Dates and counts: August into November 1793. Deaths from 1 August to 9 November: 4,041 (Mathew Carey's count) or 4,044 (Benjamin Rush's) (O'Malley 2020). "About 5,000" (PBS, era 03 source, and `city-building`'s bank). Mütter 2025: "about 10% of the population." About 20,000 people left the city (O'Malley 2020). Give Carey's count and the range.
- Cause: the virus spread by the Aedes aegypti mosquito. Nobody in 1793 knew this. It was established in the early 1900s (O'Malley 2020).
- Government: Mayor Matthew Clarkson issued a proclamation on 10 September 1793 and led a citizens' committee of 26 members from 14 September (O'Malley 2020).
- Treatments: Benjamin Rush treated with heavy bloodletting and purges made with mercury (Mütter 2025: he "settled on the use of mercury ... and bloodletting"). A search summary gives his purge as 10 grains of calomel (a mercury compound) and 15 grains of jalap (a plant root that empties the bowels) (unconfirmed: search summary only). Mütter 2025: removing too much blood could send patients into shock ("hypovolemic shock," from too little blood in the body: confusion, low blood pressure, fast heartbeat). Jean Devèze, a French doctor from Saint-Domingue, used milder treatment at Bush Hill hospital, run by Stephen Girard and Peter Helm (O'Malley 2020).
- The false immunity claim: Rush believed and printed in the Philadelphia papers that Black people could not catch yellow fever (O'Malley 2020). Jones & Allen 1794: "Early in September, a Solicitation appeared in the public papers, to the people of colour to come forward and assist the distressed, perishing, and neglected sick; with a kind of assurance, that people of our colour were not liable to take the infection."
- What the Black volunteers did (Jones & Allen 1794): members of the Free African Society, led by Jones and Allen, nursed the sick and carried and buried the dead. They hired five men to help bury the dead ("Two of whom were Richard Allen's brothers"). Rush "called us more immediately to attend upon the sick, knowing we could both bleed" and told them what medicine to give and when to bleed. "We have bled upwards of eight hundred people." "We have buried several hundreds of poor persons and strangers, for which service we have never received, nor never asked any compensation."
- Black deaths (Jones & Allen, citing the city's bill of mortality): "In 1792, there were 67 of our colour buried, and in 1793 it amounted to 305." Nurses who fell sick "have been turned out of the house, and wandering and destitute ... we know of one who even died in a stable."
- Carey's charge: Mathew Carey's *A Short Account of the Malignant Fever* accused Black nurses of charging high prices and stealing. Jones and Allen answered in print in 1794 (Jones & Allen 1794, "a Refutation of Some Censures"). They named Mary Scott, a Black nurse who refused more than her fee from Mrs. Mason, who then settled on her six pounds a year for life.
- Bush Hill: two-thirds of the prisoners released to nurse at the Bush Hill hospital were Black, freed on condition of nursing, after the elders of the African church asked (Jones & Allen 1794).

**Vaccination reaches the United States, 1800 and 1802 (Countway Waterhouse).**
- Vaccination here means scratching matter from cowpox, a mild disease of cows, into the skin. It protects the person against smallpox without the risk of the older inoculation, which used matter from a smallpox sore (era 04).
- Dr. Benjamin Waterhouse of Cambridge, Massachusetts, received "specimens of thread impregnated with the vaccine matter" from Edward Jenner's circle in England. On 8 July 1800 he vaccinated his young son Daniel Oliver and a servant, Samuel Carter. He then vaccinated "three more Waterhouse children and another servant, Kesiah Flag." A search summary gives Daniel's age as 5 and calls it the first recorded vaccination in the United States (unconfirmed: search summary only).
- He sent vaccine matter to other doctors and to President Thomas Jefferson (Countway). A search summary says Jefferson vaccinated his family, the enslaved people at Monticello and his neighbours (unconfirmed: search summary only).
- 16 August 1802: Waterhouse and six other physicians vaccinated nineteen boys. That fall the boys were exposed to smallpox. The Boston Board of Health reported, "The Cow-pox is a complete preventive against all the effects of the Small-pox upon the human system" (Countway). Whose sons the boys were, and whether their parents agreed, is not in the opened page.

**The Indian Vaccination Act, 1832, and the smallpox of 1837 (Wikipedia IVA; ND Studies 1837).**
- President Andrew Jackson signed the act on 5 May 1832. Congress gave $12,000 to buy vaccine and pay for vaccinating Native people. Secretary of War Lewis Cass designed it and ran it through Indian agents. "By February 1, 1833, more than 17,000 Indians had been vaccinated." Some Army surgeons refused to take part because the pay was too low (Wikipedia IVA).
- Excluded: the Mandan, Hidatsa and Arikara on the upper Missouri River (Wikipedia IVA, after Pearson 2003). ND Studies: the upper-Missouri nations "had been denied access to the 1832 federal vaccination program." Why Cass left them out: a search summary says he judged the upper-Missouri fur trade no longer economically important (unconfirmed: search summary only). The opened pages do not quote his reason.
- The epidemic: the American Fur Company's steamboat *St. Peters* reached Fort Clark (North Dakota, beside the Mandan villages) on 18 June 1837. The fort's head trader, Francis Chardon, learned from the captain that people aboard had smallpox. The crew unloaded goods and loaded furs in less than 24 hours, and the boat went on upriver to Fort Union (ND Studies 1837).
- Chardon's journal, 14 July 1837: "A young Mandan died to day of the Small Pox." Later: "they die so fast that it is impossible" to keep count. By August about 500 had died (ND Studies 1837).
- Counts (ND Studies 1837): about 2,000 Mandan lived at the Knife River villages in spring 1837, and 138 were alive in October, a loss of about 93 percent. The Arikara lost about two-thirds. The Hidatsa also lost many (no number in the opened page). Wikipedia IVA: smallpox "killed 90% of the Mandan." A search summary gives more than 60 percent of the Blackfeet (unconfirmed: search summary only).
- The Mandan leader Four Bears caught the disease. In his death speech, as recorded by Chardon: "I have loved the Whites ... and how have they repaid it! With ingratitude!" (ND Studies 1837).
- Carriers: the passengers and crew of the *St. Peters*. The opened sources do not say that anyone spread it on purpose. Who decided to keep unloading at Fort Clark: the captain and Chardon's post, per ND Studies. The captain's name is not in the opened page.

**Cholera, 1832 and 1849 (SLCL 2017).**
- Cholera is "a bacterial infection in the small intestine, caused by exposure to contaminated food or water." Its main sign is diarrhea, which can begin "within as little as two hours after exposure." The body loses so much water and salt that the person can die (dehydration). It spread from India after 1817 and first reached the United States in 1832.
- 1832: `city-building` tells New York (about 3,500 dead of about 250,000). St. Louis: about 300 dead from 1832 to 1834 (estimate, no death records kept). `work-workers` tells Duffy's Cut (57 Irish railroad workers dead in 1832).
- 1849, St. Louis: two steamboats from New Orleans arrived on 2 January 1849 with 66 passengers sick with cholera. Deaths rose in the spring as boats and California gold seekers came through. 65 died on 13 June and 85 on 21 June. By early July more than 100 a day.
- City conditions: population 4,977 in 1830 and 77,000 in 1850. No sewers. About 75 percent of people drank from shallow wells, often near outhouses. Pigs rooted in the garbage in the streets.
- What doctors thought: nobody knew the cause. Doctors blamed "bad diet, night air, and alcoholic beverages," banned vegetables, then meat, burned fires at night against "miasma" (bad air), and tried bloodletting. Two researchers identified the bacterium and dirty water as its route in 1854 (SLCL does not name them).
- What officials did: on 27 June 1849 a city law created a Committee of Public Health with $50,000 and "nearly unlimited powers." Its members set up a quarantine station for steamboats on Arsenal Island in the Mississippi. The city engineer drained standing water, hogs were banned in the city, and the council ordered streets paved. Deaths fell to 10 by 27 July. The committee's last meeting was 1 August. Its final report asked for sewers to every house.
- Count: reports of the time give 4,557 cholera deaths out of 8,603 deaths in 1849. Deaths went unreported, so both numbers were higher. About 70 percent of the dead were Irish and German immigrants (SLCL 2017). Other counts: at least 4,317, about 6 percent of the city (search summary: St. Louis Public Radio, unconfirmed), and 4,285 in *The Western Journal* (search summary, unconfirmed). Give "about 4,500, and the real number was higher."
- New cemeteries: Bellefontaine (opened 1850) and later Calvary were laid out because cholera burials filled the city graveyards.

**Ether, 1842 and 1846 (MHS 2016; Thomas 2019).**
- Anesthesia means using a drug to keep a patient from feeling pain during an operation. Before it, surgeons "had to operate quickly to prevent shock" (MHS 2016), so they could attempt only short operations.
- 30 March 1842, Jefferson, Georgia: Dr. Crawford Long removed a tumor from the neck of James Venable while Venable breathed ether. Both men had taken part in "ether frolics," parties where people breathed ether for fun, and Long had noticed that people hurt in them felt little pain. Long did not publish until 1849, in the *Southern Medical and Surgical Journal* (Thomas 2019).
- 16 October 1846, Massachusetts General Hospital, Boston: the dentist William T. G. Morton gave ether through an inhaler he had made, and Dr. John Collins Warren removed a tumor from the neck of Edward Gilbert Abbott (spelled "Abbot" by MHS), a young housepainter. Abbott said he had not been completely unconscious but "had felt no sensation of pain." Warren's journal: "the Substance employed was Sulphuric Ether" (MHS 2016). Warren's words "Gentlemen, this is no humbug" are a later report (search summary: "reportedly"). Do not quote them as certain.
- What ether did: the patient breathed its vapour from a soaked sponge in the inhaler and lost feeling (MHS; Linda Hall search summary for the sponge).
- `war` tells chloroform and ether in Civil War amputations.

**J. Marion Sims and the enslaved women of Montgomery, 1845 to 1849.**
- What a vesicovaginal fistula is: a hole torn through the wall between the bladder and the vagina, usually by a long, blocked childbirth that crushes the tissue (Andrei 2013, Wall 2006). Urine then leaks out all the time. Wall quotes that it "trickles constantly down her thighs, irritates the integument" (the skin). Kaye Museum: the injury brought "pain, incontinence, smell, infection and, ultimately, isolation." Some women, Anarcha among them, also had a hole into the rectum, so stool leaked too (Kaye Museum, Wall: "vesicovaginal and rectovaginal").
- Where and who: Montgomery, Alabama, 1845 to 1849. Sims wrote in 1855 of "three young healthy colored girls given to me by their owners in Alabama, I agreeing to perform no operation without the full consent of the patients" (Wall 2006, quoting Sims). The enslaved women could not refuse their owners. Their consent is recorded only in Sims's own words.
- The arrangement: the owners provided clothing and paid taxes, and Sims housed and fed the women in a small hospital he built behind his office (Wikipedia Sims, Holland 2017: an eight-bed hospital).
- Names: Sims named Anarcha, Betsey and Lucy. Wikipedia Sims counts twelve enslaved women and girls in all. His colleague Nathan Bozeman later named four more: Ann McRee (16), Lavinia Boudurant (13), Delia (23) and Julia McDuffie (20) (Wikipedia Sims). Kaye Museum says six or seven women whose names Sims did not record. Record both counts.
- No pain relief during surgery: Sims used no anesthesia on these women in any of the operations, from 1845 to 1849 (Wikipedia Sims, Kaye Museum). Ether was shown in public in Boston in October 1846, partway through these years. Sims wrote that fistula operations were "not painful enough to justify the trouble, and risk attending their administration" (Wall 2006). Holland 2017 and Wikipedia Sims tie this to the false belief, held by many white doctors, that Black people felt less pain. After the surgeries he often gave the women opium, a drug that dulls pain and causes sleep (Wikipedia Sims).
- Lucy: about 18 (Holland 2017). She was operated on for about an hour while about a dozen doctors watched. Sims put a sponge in to draw off urine, left it in her urethra and bladder, and she nearly died of blood poisoning (sepsis, an infection spread through the blood) (Wikipedia Sims, Holland 2017). Sims's own words: "Lucy's agony was extreme. She was much prostrated, and I thought that she was going to die" (quoted in Holland 2017).
- Anarcha: 17 when she went into labour (Kaye Museum, Holland 2017). Wikipedia Sims: "estimated to have been 13" when first treated. Record both. Sims operated on her about 30 times over about four years (Wall 2006: 30 operations; Kaye Museum: 29 failed operations before the one that worked). In 1849, on the thirtieth operation, he closed her fistula (Andrei 2013).
- The method (Andrei 2013, Kaye Museum): each woman was placed naked on her knees and elbows so that he could see inside. He bent a pewter spoon into a tool to hold the vagina open (the Sims speculum). He sewed the hole shut with silver wire, which caused fewer infections than silk. A sponge (later a catheter, a tube) drained urine from the bladder while the wound healed. A search summary adds that he cut away the edges of the hole before sewing (unconfirmed: search summary only).
- He published the method in 1852 (Wikipedia Sims). White women later came to him for the same operation, and most "were apparently unable to endure the pain" (Wikipedia Sims). He operated on them with anesthesia later in his career (not stated in an opened source: do not use).
- Sims called these years the most "memorable time" of his life and wrote, "There was never a time that I could not, at any day, have had a subject for operation" (Holland 2017).
- Enslaved infants: Wikipedia Sims reports that he also operated on enslaved babies with neonatal tetanus (lockjaw in newborns), using "a shoemaker's awl to pry the skull bones ... into alignment," and blamed their deaths on "the sloth and ignorance of their mothers." Only Wikipedia opened for this. Use only with that attribution, or leave to round 2.
- Later: New York City took his statue down from Central Park in April 2018 after a unanimous vote of the Public Design Commission (Wikipedia Sims, NPR 17 April 2018 search result).

**Smallpox among freedpeople, 1862 to 1868 (Downs 2017).**
- The epidemic began in Washington, D.C., and "slowly crept into Alexandria." A search summary (Oxford chapter abstract) adds that it reached the Lower South in 1863 and the Mississippi Valley in 1864-65 (unconfirmed: search summary only).
- Count: "Over 60,000 newly freed slaves died from smallpox" (Downs 2017). **Clara Barton (NPS Antietam Barton; NPS Barton chronology).**

**Hannah Ropes (Wikipedia Ropes; Alcott diary).**

**Cleaning up New York, 1866 (CUNY 1866).**
- February 1866: the Metropolitan Board of Health was founded (CUNY says by "the Common Council." **Yellow fever, 1878 (PBS 1878).**
- It began in New Orleans after the ship *Emily B. Souder* arrived in late May 1878. A sick sailor was taken off, diagnosed with malaria. After the ship was cleared, crew members fell ill and died. The first official yellow fever death in New Orleans that year was a 4-year-old girl, in July.
- The fever spread up the river to Vicksburg and Grenada, Mississippi, by August, then Memphis. Mississippi Valley: 120,000 cases and 20,000 deaths.
- Memphis: about 25,000 to 27,000 of 47,000 people fled. More than 5,000 died. Losses of $15 million "bankrupted the city." Annie Cook, who ran a brothel, turned it into a hospital, nursed the sick, and died of the fever.
- 1879: Congress set up the National Board of Health (PBS: "established the National Board of Health in 1879").
- What caused it: the Aedes aegypti mosquito, proved in 1900 (era 08).

**Garfield and the doctors' unwashed hands, 1881 (Wikipedia Garfield; UTMB).**
- Charles J. Guiteau shot President James A. Garfield on 2 July 1881 at the Baltimore and Potomac Railroad Station in Washington.
- Doctors pushed "unwashed fingers into the wound to probe for the bullet" and used unsterilized instruments. Dr. Willard Bliss, who took charge, probed the wound "several times a day ... never disinfecting his hands first" (UTMB). Joseph Lister, an English surgeon, had urged doctors to clean hands and instruments to stop infection. "American doctors resisted this 'as too much trouble'" (UTMB). Wikipedia: most American doctors then "did not believe in anti-sepsis measures."
- Garfield died on 19 September 1881. The autopsy found "pneumonia in both lungs and a body that was filled with pus due to uncontrolled septic shock" (Wikipedia). A 2013 medical paper argues a burst artery near the spleen (Wikipedia). Historians "now believe Garfield probably would have survived his wound had the doctors been more capable" (Wikipedia).
- Germ theory in plain words: tiny living things too small to see, germs, cause many diseases. Antisepsis: killing germs on hands, tools and wounds.

**Proving the mosquito, 1900 and 1901 (PBS Reed; NMHM Maass).**
- In 1900 an army board led by Major Walter Reed, with James Carroll, Jesse Lazear and Aristides Agramonte, began work outside Havana, Cuba (NMHM: from June 1900. A search summary: appointed May 1900 by Surgeon General George Miller Sternberg, unconfirmed).
- Lazear raised mosquitoes and let them feed on yellow fever patients. Mosquitoes that had fed on a patient at least 12 days before passed the disease on. Carroll and a soldier volunteer caught yellow fever after bites on 27 to 31 August 1900. Lazear, "likely exposed to infected mosquitoes himself," died on 25 September 1900 (PBS Reed).
- November 1900, Camp Lazear: volunteers slept in one building with bedding soiled by yellow fever patients and stayed well. In a second, clean building, infected mosquitoes bit volunteers, and several caught yellow fever. All of those recovered (PBS Reed). Reed presented the results on 6 February 1901. After workers killed mosquitoes in Havana, yellow fever there nearly stopped (PBS Reed).
- Volunteers signed written contracts that named the risk. Pay: NMHM gives "$100 to volunteers and $200 to those who became successfully infected." A search summary gives $100 in gold plus $100 more if they fell ill, and says some contracts were in Spanish for Spanish immigrant volunteers (unconfirmed: search summary only).
- Clara Maass: born to a poor German immigrant family in East Orange, New Jersey, trained at the Newark German Hospital school from 17, head nurse by 21 (1898). Contract nurse in the 1898 war and in the Philippines. In Havana she let infected mosquitoes bite her in March 1901, had a mild case, and recovered. She volunteered again in August 1901 and died of yellow fever at 25 (NMHM; Wikipedia via search summary: 24 August 1901). The commission "was ordered to disband shortly after her death" (NMHM).

**Mary Mallon, "Typhoid Mary" (NOVA Mallon).**

- Who held her: the New York City Board of Health (Dr. William H. Park is named by NOVA). George Soper, the investigator usually credited with tracing her, is not named on the opened page.

**The Flexner Report, 1910 (JSTOR Flexner).**
- Abraham Flexner, hired by the Carnegie Foundation, published his survey of medical schools in 1910. The country had 148 medical schools. After the reforms that followed, it had 66.
- Of seven medical schools for Black students, two survived: Howard University (Washington, D.C.) and Meharry Medical College (Nashville). The AMA, whose members were mostly white men, used the report to close schools (JSTOR Daily's reading). A search summary (NCBI 2006) names four that closed: Shaw University, Louisville National Medical College, the University of West Tennessee College of Physicians and Surgeons, and Medico-Chirurgical and Theological College of Christ's Institution (unconfirmed: search summary only). **The 1918 influenza (CDC 1918; Davis 2018; Brown 1986; Rozell 2020).**
- CDC: about 500 million people, one-third of the world, infected. "At least 50 million" dead in the world, "about 675,000" in the United States. First found in the United States among military personnel in spring 1918. Many of the dead were healthy people aged 20 to 40. No vaccine and no antibiotics. Officials used "isolation, quarantine, good personal hygiene, use of disinfectants, and limitations of public gatherings, which were applied unevenly."
- Philadelphia (Davis 2018): the Fourth Liberty Loan parade on 28 September 1918 drew about 200,000 people onto Broad Street. Dr. Wilmer Krusen, the city's director of public health, let it go ahead and "publicly denied that influenza was a threat," while nearby army camps were full of the flu. Within 72 hours every bed in the city's 31 hospitals was full. About 2,600 died in the week ending 5 October and more than 4,500 the next week. On 3 October officials closed schools, churches, theaters and pool halls. "More than 12,000 Philadelphians would die in a matter of weeks."
- What the flu did to the body (Brown 1986, a nurse's eyewitness words): "terrific nosebleeds ... Sometimes the blood would just shoot across the room." "Some were delirious and some had their lungs punctured. Then their bodies would fill with air ... You would see them with bubbles all through their arms." `war` outline: most soldiers who died had pneumonia, lungs filling with fluid.
- Great Lakes Naval Training Station, Illinois (Brown 1986): "There were 173,000 men at Great Lakes at the time, and 6,000 were in the hospitals at the height of the epidemic. I suppose no one knows how many died."
- Brevig Mission, Alaska (Rozell 2020): within five days of the flu's arrival, "72 of the 80 villagers in Brevig Mission were dead." A search summary gives 15 to 20 November 1918 and says the 80 were adults (unconfirmed: search summary only). Miners from Nome thawed the frozen ground with steam and dug a mass grave 12 feet wide, 25 feet long and six feet deep. In 1951 and again in August 1997 Johan Hultin dug there, with the village elders' permission in 1997 (search summary), and recovered lung tissue from a woman buried in the frozen ground. In 2005 Jeffery Taubenberger's team rebuilt the virus from it.
- Native continuity: Brevig Mission was an Inupiaq village (general knowledge, not in the opened page: say "Alaska Native village" only).

**Josie Mabel Brown (Brown 1986).**

**Radium in the body, 1917 to 1932 (PMC10046820 via rights-movements bank; Wikipedia Byers; FDA 1938).**
- Mechanism: "Radium, which is in the same alkaline earth family as calcium, would be taken up in the hydroxy apatite of growing bones and teeth." The body builds radium into bone as if it were calcium, and it gives off radiation from inside. Results named: "aplastic anemia, necrosis of the jaw, and osteosarcomas." In plain words: the bone marrow stops making blood cells, the jaw bone dies and breaks apart, and bone cancers grow. Autopsies found 99 percent of the radioactivity in the skeleton.
- Dial painters at U.S. Radium in Orange, New Jersey, from 1917, shaped their brushes with their lips (physicist Robley Evans's account). In 1924 the dentist Theodor Blum found Grace Fryer's jaw bone wasting away, which he called "radium jaw." `rights-movements` and `work-workers` tell the lawsuit and the workers.
- Radithor: radium salts dissolved in water, sold by William J. A. Bailey, who falsely claimed medical credentials and paid doctors to prescribe it. Eben Byers, a Pittsburgh industrialist, drank about 1,400 bottles from 1927 to October 1930. An FTC investigator in 1931: "All the remaining bone tissue of his body was disintegrating, and holes were actually forming in his skull." He died on 31 March 1932 of jaw bone cancer from radium poisoning (Wikipedia Byers). The FTC ordered Bailey to stop. FDA 1938: the 1906 law could not stop the sale of Radithor. The "Jaw Came Off" headline is from a 1990 Wall Street Journal article, not 1932.

**The Tuskegee study, 1932 to 1972 (Brandt 1978; CDC Tuskegee; McGill OSS; Wikipedia Tuskegee; Wooten 1972; APP 1997).** DECISIONS #3.
- Who: the United States Public Health Service (PHS). Dr. Taliaferro Clark, chief of its Venereal Disease Division, started it in 1932. Dr. Raymond Vonderlehr ran it in the field from September 1932 and later succeeded Clark. Dr. Oliver Wenger ran the regional clinic. Dr. John R. Heller gathered the control group and later ran the division. Eunice Rivers, a Black nurse, kept contact with the men for all 40 years (Brandt, Wikipedia). The Tuskegee Institute's hospital (Dr. Eugene Dibble) cooperated (Brandt).
- Where and whom: Macon County, Alabama, around the town of Tuskegee, which a 1929 PHS survey had found had the highest syphilis rate of six Southern counties surveyed. Vonderlehr looked among "the largely illiterate, poverty-stricken population of sharecroppers and tenant farmers" (Brandt). 600 Black men: 399 with syphilis and 201 without, who served as controls (CDC). Brandt: 400 and 200.
- The purpose: "to determine the natural course of untreated, latent syphilis in black males" (Brandt). They "had no intention of providing any treatment for the infected men" (Brandt). Planned for six months, it ran 40 years.
- What the men were told: that they had "bad blood," and that they were being treated for it (CDC). "Participants' informed consent was not collected" (CDC). Charles Pollard: "they told me I had bad blood ... And that's what they've been telling me ever since" (Wooten 1972).
- What the men got: free medical exams, meals and burial insurance (CDC). Rivers gave them "noneffective medicines, 'spring tonic' and aspirin" and rides and hot meals on exam days (Brandt). From 1935 the Milbank Memorial Fund paid about $50 per man for burial, in exchange for autopsy (Brandt). Wenger: "we have no further interest in these patients until they die" (Brandt).
- The spinal tap: a doctor pushes a needle between the bones of the lower back into the spinal canal and draws out fluid, to see whether syphilis has reached the brain and nerves. It "often entails considerable pain and complications" (Brandt). The PHS called it treatment. Their letter to the men: "Remember This Is Your Last Chance For Special Free Treatment. Be Sure To Meet The Nurse" (Brandt).
- Keeping treatment away: PHS officers gave local doctors lists of the men and asked them not to treat them. They warned the Alabama health department's mobile VD unit off them in the early 1940s. In 1941 the Army drafted some of the men and told them to start treatment. The PHS gave the draft board a list of 256 names to exclude from treatment, "and the board complied" (Brandt). Penicillin became the standard cure for syphilis (CDC: 1943 in the timeline. Wikipedia: "by 1947." Brandt: widely available "by the early 1950s"). Record all three. None of the men was offered it by the study. By 1952 almost 30 percent had got some penicillin on their own, 7.5 percent enough of it (Brandt). Vonderlehr: "I hope that the availability of antibiotics has not interfered too much with this project" (Brandt).
- **The documented bodily course of untreated syphilis** (DECISIONS #3). Syphilis is an infection caused by a bacterium, passed during sex or from a mother to her unborn baby (CDC syphilis). Primary stage: a painless sore where it entered, lasting 3 to 6 weeks. Secondary: a rash, fever, swollen glands, sore throat. Latent: no signs for years while the infection stays in the body. Tertiary, 10 to 30 years after infection: it damages "the heart and blood vessels, and the brain and nervous system." In the brain (neurosyphilis) it causes severe headache and dementia, the loss of memory and reason. In the eyes it can cause blindness. Babies born infected "can die" (CDC syphilis).
- What the study's own doctors recorded in the men (Brandt, quoting the published reports): 1936, "syphilis in this period [latency] tends to greatly increase the frequency of manifestations of cardiovascular disease" (disease of the heart and blood vessels). Only 16 percent of the infected men showed no sign of illness, against 61 percent of the controls. About 1946: "nearly twice as large a proportion of the syphilitic individuals as of the control group has died." Life expectancy was cut by about 20 percent. 1955: slightly more than 30 percent of the infected men autopsied had "died directly from advanced syphilitic lesions of either the cardiovascular or the central nervous system" (the heart and great blood vessels, or the brain and spinal cord). The journal's introduction to Brandt: "scores of people died painful death, others became permanently blind or insane, and the children of several were born with congenital syphilis."
- Counts at the end (1972): 28 men died directly of syphilis, 100 of related complications, 40 wives were infected, and 19 children were born with syphilis (McGill OSS, Wikipedia Tuskegee). 74 of the men were alive in 1972 (McGill). Brandt: "at least twenty-eight, but perhaps more than 100, had died directly from advanced syphilitic lesions."
- End and after (era 09, T-263c): exposed by an Associated Press story in July 1972 (CDC. Wooten's NYT piece is dated 27 July 1972, the day after. McGill's "New York Times ... November 16, 1972" conflicts: T-263c to check). Ended November 1972 (CDC). $10 million settlement, 1974 (CDC). Wikipedia names the case Pollard v. United States, 1973, with the lawyer Fred D. Gray. Clinton's apology, 16 May 1997: "What the United States Government did was shameful, and I am sorry." The men "were betrayed ... even once a cure was discovered, they were denied help, and they were lied to by their Government" (APP 1997). Last participant died January 2004 (CDC).
- Heller in 1972: "I don't see why they should be shocked or horrified. There was no racial side to this. It just happened to be in a black community" (Brandt).

**Elixir Sulfanilamide and the 1938 law (FDA Sulfanilamide; FDA 1938).**
- September and October 1937: the S. E. Massengill Company of Bristol, Tennessee, sold "Elixir Sulfanilamide," a liquid sulfa drug for strep infections, especially children's sore throats. Its chemist, Harold Cole Watkins, dissolved the drug in diethylene glycol, "a chemical normally used as an antifreeze," which "is a deadly poison." The company did not test it for safety (FDA: no law required it).
- It killed "more than 100 people in 15 states," "many of whom were children." Victims had "stoppage of urine, severe abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, stupor, and convulsions," the signs of kidney failure. A mother wrote to President Roosevelt about her daughter Joan: "we can see her little body tossing to and fro and hear that little voice screaming with pain."
- The owner, Dr. Samuel Evans Massengill: "My chemists and I deeply regret the fatal results, but there was no error in the manufacture of the product ... I do not feel that there was any responsibility on our part." Watkins killed himself. FDA's 239 inspectors tracked down the bottles. The only legal charge available was misbranding: "elixir" meant a drug dissolved in alcohol.
- On 25 June 1938 President Franklin D. Roosevelt signed the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act, passed by Congress. Under it "a manufacturer would have to prove to FDA that a drug were safe before it could be sold" (FDA 1938). [VERIFY 1938] cleared.

**Penicillin reaches a patient, 1942 (Yale 2026; Time 2016).**
- Anne S. Miller of New Haven, Connecticut, had a bacterial infection of the blood (septicemia) after a miscarriage and a fever of at least 103 for weeks (Time). Search summary: she was 33 (unconfirmed).
- Her doctors, John H. Bumstead and Orvan W. Hess, asked Dr. John F. Fulton of Yale to get penicillin from Norman Heatley of Oxford. - Death: Yale 2026 says "at the age of 90 in 2000." A search summary says 27 May 1999 in Salisbury, Connecticut. Record both. Penicillin in plain words: a drug made from a mold that kills many kinds of bacteria. It was the first antibiotic made in large amounts.

**The Guatemala experiments, 1946 to 1948 (NARA 2011; Dixon 2016).**
- PHS doctors of the Venereal Disease Research Laboratory, under Dr. John C. Cutler in Guatemala City, deliberately infected people with syphilis, gonorrhea and chancroid (all diseases passed by sex) (NARA). Subjects of experiments: 696 for syphilis, 772 for gonorrhea, 142 for chancroid (NARA; people could be in more than one). Dixon: "around 1,500 people." Search summary: about 1,300 infected (unconfirmed).

The U.S. National Institutes of Health and the Pan American Sanitary Bureau paid for it, and Guatemalan officials took part (Dixon). Nothing was published (NARA). It became public in 2010 (Dixon). Deaths: not in the opened pages. The 2011 Presidential Commission count (83 deaths, often cited) is not opened: do not use without a source.

- Paralysis: "Less than 1% of all polio infections in children result in flaccid paralysis" (CDC Pinkbook). Flaccid paralysis: the muscles go limp and cannot move. When the breathing muscles are paralysed, the person cannot breathe without a machine. An iron lung is "seven-foot-long cylinders that use negative pressure and bellows to draw air into their lungs" (NPR 2024).
- Salk: Jonas Salk, born 28 October 1914 in New York City, died 23 June 1995 in La Jolla, California. At the University of Pittsburgh School of Medicine from 1947 (Wikipedia Salk). His vaccine used killed virus ("killed-virus" vaccine, 1952, funded by the National Foundation for Infantile Paralysis, forerunner of the March of Dimes) (CBS/HealthDay 2014).
- The 1954 field trial: designed and run by Dr. Thomas Francis Jr. of the University of Michigan (Poliomyelitis Vaccine Evaluation Center). About 1.8 million children in the United States, Canada and Finland (Wikipedia Francis. CBS/HealthDay: "more than 1.8 million children"). Shots given between 26 April and 10 July 1954: "more than 443,000 children received at least one polio inoculation, while more than 210,000 received a placebo" (March of Dimes figures, CBS/HealthDay). Wikipedia Salk gives "approximately one million children": counts differ by whether observed children are included. Double-blind: neither child nor nurse knew who got vaccine or placebo. A placebo is a dummy shot with no medicine in it. Children were called "Polio Pioneers." Salk "initially resisted the idea of a 'placebo-control' trial, arguing that doctors shouldn't be giving kids something that deliberately would not protect them against polio," and gave way because only that design could prove the vaccine worked (Peter Salk, his son, via CBS/HealthDay). Parents signed consent forms (Oshinsky, via CBS). The National Foundation spent $7.5 million on the trial (CBS). The trial cost is given as $17.5 million by Wikipedia Francis. Figures differ.
- 12 April 1955: results announced at Rackham Auditorium, University of Michigan: the vaccine was "80 to 90 percent effective in preventing polio" (CBS/HealthDay. Wikipedia Francis for the place). IPV (Salk) licensed 1955 (CDC Pinkbook).
- Patent: asked by Edward R. Murrow who owned the patent, Salk said, "Could you patent the sun?" The National Foundation and the University of Pittsburgh had looked into a patent and judged the methods "likely not novel enough to patent" (Wikipedia Salk). NPR 2024: Salk "never sought a patent."
- The Cutter incident, April 1955: Cutter Laboratories of Berkeley, California made 120,000 doses that "contained live polio virus." Of children given it, "40,000 developed abortive poliomyelitis" (a mild form), "56 developed paralytic poliomyelitis" and "five children died." Spread to families and neighbours caused "a further 113 people paralyzed and 5 deaths." Cutter withdrew the vaccine on 27 April 1955. A June 1955 congressional hearing blamed mainly "the lack of scrutiny from the NIH Laboratory of Biologics Control" (Wikipedia Cutter).
- Sabin's oral vaccine: types 1 and 2 licensed 1961, type 3 in 1962, three-type OPV in 1963 (CDC Pinkbook).
- "The last case of wild poliovirus acquired in the United States was in 1979" (CDC Pinkbook). A 2022 case in an unvaccinated traveller in Rockland County, New York (NPR 2024).
- Post-polio syndrome: "After an interval of 15 to 40 years, 25% to 40% of persons who contracted paralytic poliomyelitis in childhood experience new muscle pain and exacerbation of existing weakness or develop new weakness or paralysis" (CDC Pinkbook).

**Paul Alexander (NPR 2024; Wikipedia Alexander).**
- Born 30 January 1946 in Dallas, Texas. Got polio in 1952 at age 6, during an outbreak in Dallas. Within days he was paralysed from the neck down and could not breathe on his own. A tracheotomy saved him (NPR). A tracheotomy is an operation that cuts an opening in the front of the neck into the windpipe so air can get in.
- He lived in an iron lung for more than 70 years. Guinness World Records named him the longest-surviving iron lung patient (NPR). From 1954 he learned glossopharyngeal breathing, swallowing air into his lungs ("intentionally swallow air," NPR), which let him leave the machine for hours at a time. He went back into it every night (NPR, speaking of him and Martha Lillard).
- He held a rod in his mouth to turn pages and paint, and later typed his autobiography with it (NPR). He studied at Southern Methodist University and the University of Texas at Austin (bachelor's 1978, law degree 1984), was admitted to the bar in 1986 and worked as a lawyer in Dallas (Wikipedia Alexander). Book: *Three Minutes for a Dog: My Life in an Iron Lung* (2020) (Wikipedia).
- Quote (Gizmodo video 2017, via NPR): "I've never thought of myself as a cripple." ... "I'm Paul Alexander, human being."
- Died 11 March 2024 in Dallas, at 78 (NPR: "Monday," per Grove Hill Funeral Home). Cause not stated (Wikipedia: unclear, after a COVID-19 hospital stay in February).

**Henrietta Lacks, 1951 (Wikipedia Lacks).**
- Born Loretta Pleasant, 1 August 1920, Roanoke, Virginia. Grew up in Clover, Virginia, in a log cabin that had been slave quarters on the plantation her white great-grandfather and great-uncle had owned.
- 29 January 1951: went to Johns Hopkins Hospital, Baltimore, about a "knot" in her womb. Dr. Howard W. Jones took a biopsy of a mass on her cervix: cancer of the cervix. Treated with radium tubes placed inside her body, then X-rays. "During her treatments, two samples were taken from Lacks's cervix without her permission or knowledge." The samples went to Dr. George Otto Gey. "As was then the practice, no consent was required."
- Died 4 October 1951, age 31, after the cancer spread through her body. Buried in an unmarked grave in Lackstown, Clover.
- Her cells kept dividing in the laboratory, the first human cells to do so: the HeLa cell line (first two letters of her first and last names). By 1954 Salk used HeLa cells in polio vaccine work. Chester M. Southam injected HeLa cells into cancer patients, prisoners and healthy people to see whether cancer could be passed on.
- Family not told of the cell line until 1975. Family medical records published without consent in the 1980s. 2013 agreement with NIH gave the family a say over the genome data. Estate sued Thermo Fisher Scientific in October 2021, settled 31 July 2023. Settled with Novartis February 2026, terms not public (Wikipedia, recent: journalism behind it not opened).
- Book: Rebecca Skloot, *The Immortal Life of Henrietta Lacks* (2010). Film: HBO, 22 April 2017, same title (drama, about her and her daughter Deborah).

**Kidney transplant 1954 and technetium-99m (Wikipedia Murray; Wikipedia Tc-99m).**
- 23 December 1954, Peter Bent Brigham Hospital, Boston: Dr. Joseph Murray moved a healthy kidney from Ronald Herrick into his identical twin Richard, "who was dying of chronic nephritis" (kidney disease). The operation took five and a half hours. Richard lived eight more years, married and had two children. Ronald lived more than 50 years after. Murray shared the 1990 Nobel Prize. Identical twins were used because the body's defences attack an organ from anyone else, called rejection (general medical knowledge, consistent with Wikipedia Murray: "the problem of immune rejection").
- Technetium-99m: first generator 1958 at Brookhaven National Laboratory (Walter Tucker and Margaret Greene). Powell Richards proposed it as a medical tracer in 1960. First US report of scanning with it, August 1963. Now "used in tens of millions of medical diagnostic procedures annually, making it the most commonly used medical radioisotope in the world." A doctor injects a tiny amount. It gives off gamma rays, which a camera outside the body picks up to make pictures of bones, the heart or other organs.

**Thalidomide, 1960 to 1962 (FDA Kelsey; Wikipedia Kelsey; Wikipedia thalidomide).**
- Frances Oldham Kelsey (born Canada 1914, died 7 August 2015) joined the FDA in 1960, one of about seven full-time physicians reviewing drugs. One of her first assignments: Richardson-Merrell's application to sell thalidomide (brand name Kevadon) as a sleeping pill and for morning sickness in pregnant women. Already sold in more than 20 countries. "Despite constant pressure from the company, [she] refused to approve the application because of its inadequate evidence" (FDA). She asked for more data every 60 days for more than a year (Wikipedia).
- November 1961: births of babies with deformed limbs in Europe linked to thalidomide. Worldwide more than 10,000 babies, possibly up to 20,000, about 40 percent died at or soon after birth. The most common damage was very short or missing arms and legs (phocomelia), and harm to eyes, heart and other organs (Wikipedia thalidomide).
- United States: Richardson-Merrell gave out more than 2.5 million tablets to more than 1,000 doctors as "testing": nearly 20,000 patients, several hundred pregnant. At least 17 US babies born with thalidomide damage. The company withdrew its application in March 1962 (Wikipedia thalidomide and Kelsey).
- Morton Mintz's Washington Post front page, 15 July 1962. President Kennedy gave Kelsey the President's Award for Distinguished Federal Civilian Service, 7 August 1962. Congress passed the Kefauver-Harris Amendments unanimously in October 1962: drug makers had to prove a drug worked, report side effects, and get patients' consent in drug trials (Wikipedia Kelsey).

**Smoking, asbestos and lung disease, 1954 to 1998 (NLM 1964; CDC tobacco; MedlinePlus; NCI asbestos; big-business bank).**
- 1957: Surgeon General Leroy Burney declared on 12 June 1957 that the evidence pointed to a causal link between smoking and lung cancer (NLM 1964).
- 11 January 1964: Surgeon General Luther Terry released *Smoking and Health*, from a committee of ten that reviewed more than 7,000 articles. Findings: smoking caused a 70 percent higher death rate for smokers than non-smokers. Average smokers had 9 to 10 times the risk of lung cancer, heavy smokers at least 20 times. Smoking was the main cause of chronic bronchitis. Links to emphysema and heart disease. Smoking in pregnancy lowered babies' birth weight. The committee called smoking "an habituation rather than an addiction" (NLM 1964).
- After: 1965 warning labels on packs required by Congress. 1969 ban on TV and radio cigarette ads, in force September 1970 (NLM 1964). Share of Americans who believed smoking caused cancer: 44 percent in 1958, 78 percent in 1968 (Gallup, via NLM).
- Company side (big-business bank, not reopened): the Frank Statement, 4 January 1954, in more than 400 newspapers, designed by Hill & Knowlton. Addison Yeaman memo, 17 July 1963. Seven tobacco company heads told Congress on 14 April 1994 that nicotine is not addictive (William Campbell of Philip Morris: "I believe nicotine is not addictive, yes"). Master Settlement Agreement, 23 November 1998, 46 states, at least $206 billion over 25 years.
- The body: "Lung cancer is cancer that forms in tissues of the lung, usually in the cells that line the air passages." Smoking "causes about 9 out of 10 cases of lung cancer in men and about 8 out of 10 cases of lung cancer in women." Signs: a cough that will not go away, trouble breathing, coughing up blood (MedlinePlus). Emphysema: "the walls between many of the air sacs in the lungs are damaged. This causes the air sacs to lose their shape and become floppy." Cigarette smoke is its main cause in the United States. Signs: coughing, wheezing, shortness of breath (MedlinePlus).
- Count: "Cigarette smoking and secondhand smoke exposure cause more than 480,000 deaths each year in the United States. This is nearly one in five deaths" (CDC tobacco, updated 17 September 2024). This is a current yearly estimate, not a 1950-2000 figure.
- Asbestos: "the name given to six minerals that occur naturally in the environment as bundles of fibers." Breathed-in fibres "may get trapped in the lungs and remain there for a long time" and cause scarring and inflammation. Diseases: mesothelioma, "a relatively rare cancer of the thin membranes that line the chest and abdomen", lung cancer, and asbestosis, which "can cause shortness of breath, coughing, and permanent lung damage." "It can take from 10 to 40 years or more for symptoms." Workers exposed: shipbuilding, asbestos mining and milling, textiles, insulation, demolition, firefighting, car repair. EPA banned new asbestos uses in 1989 (NCI asbestos). Johns-Manville, the largest maker, filed for bankruptcy in August 1982 facing thousands of lawsuits (big-business bank, Manville Trust). What the company's managers knew in the 1930s: SEARCHED, NOT FOUND in big-business's bank (search summaries only).

**Willowbrook, 1956 to 1987 (Wikipedia Willowbrook).**
- Willowbrook State School, Staten Island, New York City, 1947 to 1987, a state institution for children with intellectual disabilities. Built for 4,000, it held 6,000 by 1965. Hepatitis A outbreaks were common.

Paul Offit's account: Krugman found hepatitis developed in 90 percent of children soon after arrival. Yellow skin and eyes (jaundice) show the liver is not clearing the blood.

**AIDS, 1981 to 2000 (MMWR 2001a/b; HIV.gov; Wikipedia White).**
- 5 June 1981: CDC's MMWR reported "five cases of Pneumocystis carinii pneumonia (PCP) among previously healthy young men in Los Angeles," of whom "two had died" (MMWR 2001a). This was the first report of what was named AIDS.
- Plain definitions: HIV (human immunodeficiency virus) destroys white blood cells that fight infection (T cells). AIDS (acquired immune deficiency syndrome) is the late stage, when the body's defences are so weak that other infections and cancers kill the person (general medical definition, consistent with Wikipedia White on T-cell counts: "below 200 is AIDS-defining in the U.S."). HIV passes through blood, semen and other body fluids, not through touch (Wikipedia White on transmission).
- 1985 (by position on HIV.gov): CDC reported more people diagnosed with AIDS in 1985 than in all earlier years combined. "Of all AIDS cases to date, 51% of adults and 59% of children have died." Patients died "about 15 months after the disease is diagnosed" (HIV.gov, 16 January 1986 entry).
- 1987: Larry Kramer founded ACT UP (AIDS Coalition to Unleash Power) in New York on 12 March. FDA approved the first AIDS drug, AZT (zidovudine), on 19 March. 11 October 1988: more than 1,000 ACT UP members shut down FDA offices in Rockville, Maryland. 176 arrests. Eight days later the FDA announced rules to speed drug approval (HIV.gov).
- 1994 (by position on HIV.gov): "AIDS becomes the leading cause of death for all Americans ages 25 to 44."
- 1996-97: combination drug treatment (HAART, several drugs taken together). CDC reported AIDS deaths fell 47 percent from the year before (HIV.gov, 1997 entry by position). AIDS was no longer the leading cause of death for Americans 25 to 44, but still was for African Americans in that age group (HIV.gov, 1996 by position).
- Count to 2000: "As of December 31, 2000, 774,467 persons had been reported with AIDS in the United States; 448,060 of these had died" (MMWR 2001b). 322,865 living with AIDS. Deaths: 565,000 by 2007 (HIV.gov, year by position).
- Ricky Ray Hemophilia Relief Fund Act, 12 November 1998 (year by position): payments to people with hemophilia infected by unscreened clotting products between 1982 and 1987 (HIV.gov).

### 8. 1900 to 1950
- **Radium poisoning — the science of what happened to the Radium Girls.** Radium behaves chemically like calcium, so swallowed radium (from lip-pointing paint brushes, from 1917 at the dial factories) settles into bone, where its radiation kills bone tissue and causes anemia, "radium jaw" (necrosis of the jaw), fractures, and cancers. The dial painters' deaths in the 1920s–30s produced some of the first hard knowledge of internal radiation poisoning, and their bodies were studied for decades to set radiation-safety limits for later workers (including Manhattan Project workers). The court fight → Ch17; the element craze → Ch27; the medicine and dosimetry → here. (Sources: History.com; Library of Congress "Radium Girls: Living Dead Women"; NJ historic-register documentation.)
- **Radioactive patent medicine: Radithor.** Radium dissolved in water, sold as a cure-all; industrialist Eben Byers drank ~1,400 bottles (1927–30) and died of radium poisoning in 1932 — his death helped push federal action against radioactive quack cures. (Caution: the famous "jaw came off" headline is a 1990 Wall Street Journal retrospective, not from 1932.) (Sources: Wikipedia Eben Byers/Radithor cross-checked; FDA/AMA history references to VERIFY when Ch18 is worked.)
- **The radium craze as health fad:** radium tonics, cosmetics, and glow products marketed as vitality itself in the 1910s–20s — a case study in medicine before drug regulation (ties to the 1938 Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act — VERIFY the regulatory line when Ch18 is worked).

A strong "element made by people, healing people" story; get current usage numbers from IAEA/SNMMI sources when Ch18 is worked. (Sources: LANL periodic table on Tc medical use.)
