## 1. Before 1500

- **Knowledge framing.** Careful, tested knowledge existed here long before laboratories: astronomy built into structures, plant knowledge, fire ecology, weather and tide knowledge — held by nations and taught on. The respectful, accurate frame is systematic observation over generations. Traditional Ecological Knowledge (TEK) is the term working scientists use; the Ecological Society of America has a TEK section co-founded by Robin Wall Kimmerer (SUNY ESF; MacArthur Foundation 2022 citation).
- **Cahokia's Woodhenge** (Illinois): five successive circles of upright posts, about A.D. 900–1100, 24 to 72 posts each, up to 145 meters across, marking sunrise positions through the year including the solstices and equinoxes. (Cahokia Mounds State Historic Site — verified for the technology bank 2026-08-08; technology tells the built calendar, we tell the sky-knowledge it encodes.)
- **The Sun Dagger, Fajada Butte, Chaco Canyon** (New Mexico): three sandstone slabs lean against a cliff near the butte's summit and channel sunlight into "daggers" of light on two spiral petroglyphs — marking the sun at solstices and equinoxes, and, researchers argue, the moon's 18.6-year standstill cycle. Recorded at summer solstice 1977 by Anna Sofaer, a volunteer documenting rock art. **Honest status: by summer solstice 1989 two of the three slabs had shifted** — erosion accelerated by human foot traffic despite restricted access — and the centered daggers no longer form as first recorded. (NPS Chaco Culture brochure "Fajada Butte Solstice Marker"; Solstice Project; UCAR High Altitude Observatory.) The lunar-standstill claims are argued by the Solstice Project and debated among archaeoastronomers — say "researchers argue," not "it is known."
- **Story candidate → verified:** Robin Wall Kimmerer — Citizen Potawatomi Nation botanist, SUNY Distinguished Teaching Professor at the College of Environmental Science and Forestry, founder of the Center for Native Peoples and the Environment, MacArthur Fellow 2022; *Braiding Sweetgrass* (2013) a New York Times bestseller since February 2020. PhD in botany, University of Wisconsin. She works at exactly this chapter's join: Native knowledge and laboratory science as two ways of knowing used together. (ESF news 2022; MacArthur Foundation fellow page; robinwallkimmerer.com.) No documentary film about her — omit Movie line.

## 2. The 1500s (thin)

- European science barely exists yet (this is still the age of alchemy), and no newcomer is doing sustained study here. What crosses the ocean is collecting and describing.
- **Thomas Hariot, 1585–86 at Roanoke** — English mathematician sent on Raleigh's Roanoke expedition; his *A Briefe and True Report of the New Found Land of Virginia* (1588) was the first book in English about North America by someone who had been there: plants, animals, foods, and the Algonquian people, published with John White's drawings in the 1590 de Bry edition. (Encyclopedia Virginia; UNC Documenting the American South; EBSCO.) He observed here; the knowledge was carried back to Europe — that is the honest 1500s shape.

## 3. The 1600s (thin)

- **Harvard, 1636** — oldest college in the English colonies, founded by vote of the Massachusetts Bay General Court; renamed 1639 for John Harvard's bequest. (harvard.edu history timeline; Britannica.)
- **The first almanac printed in the colonies:** *An Almanack for New England for the Year 1639*, calculated by mariner William Pierce, printed by Stephen Daye at Cambridge — one of the first two things off the first press. Almanacs carried astronomy into ordinary houses for the next two centuries. (Britannica; EBSCO "North America's First Printing Press.")
- **The first telescope:** John Winthrop Jr. — Connecticut governor, elected a fellow of the Royal Society during his 1661–63 London visit (among the first colonial members) — gave his 3.5-foot telescope to Harvard in 1671–72, the college's first scientific instrument. (Linda Hall Library; Museum of Connecticut History; EBSCO. Sources give the gift as 1671 or 1672 — state "about 1672.")
- **The comet of 1680 and the argument about what comets are.** Thomas Brattle (Harvard 1676) tracked the Great Comet of 1680–81 with a telescope from Boston and reasoned the November and December appearances were one comet rounding the sun; his measurements reached Newton, who cited the New England observations in the *Principia*. (American Antiquarian Society proceedings; Colonial Williamsburg journal; Salem Witch Museum historical note.) Increase Mather's *Kometographia* (1683) catalogued comets as divine warnings — yet used parallax to show they were distant bodies, not vapors. He also founded the short-lived Boston Philosophical Society (1683, modeled on the Royal Society; lasted about three years). (Marco Institute, University of Tennessee; Smithsonian Libraries catalog.) The era's honest shape: observation was real, and the observers still read the sky as sermon.
- **Story (ordinary, verified): Rev. John Campanius Holm** — chaplain to the New Sweden colony; recorded the weather twice a day through 1644–45 near present Wilmington, Delaware, without instruments — the first known systematic weather record in what is now the U.S. NOAA's John Campanius Holm Award for volunteer weather observers (up to 25 a year) is named for him. (NWS award pages, weather.gov.)

## 4. 1700 to 1750

- **The American Philosophical Society, 1743, Philadelphia** — oldest learned society in the U.S. The idea came from botanist John Bartram in 1739; Franklin organized it with his 1743 pamphlet *A Proposal for Promoting Useful Knowledge among the British Plantations in America*; grown out of his Junto club, answering London's Royal Society. (amphilsoc.org history; Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia; Britannica.) **KEEP IN THIS ERA — v1 had it under 1750–1800; the 1743 date controls.**
- **John Bartram** (1699–1777): Quaker farmer-botanist; bought a 102-acre farm on the Schuylkill at Kingsessing in 1728 and built America's oldest surviving botanic garden; collecting expeditions from New York to Florida, 1730–1765; named the King's Botanist for North America by George III in 1765; he and his sons introduced over 200 American plants to science. Linnaeus is *reported* to have called him "the greatest natural botanist in the world" — the line is quoted everywhere but is an attribution, label it "Linnaeus reportedly called him." (Bartram's Garden; Encyclopedia.com; Monticello encyclopedia.)
- **Franklin's electrical science, 1747–1751:** experiments in Philadelphia reported in letters to Peter Collinson of the Royal Society; the single-fluid theory with **positive and negative charge** (his terms, still ours), conservation of charge, the proposal that lightning is electrical, and the grounded-rod idea. The letters were published in London, April 1751, as *Experiments and Observations on Electricity* — and in 1753 the Royal Society gave Franklin the Copley Medal, its highest honor, the first to someone outside Britain. First American with an international scientific reputation. (Library of Congress exhibitions; IEEE Milestone; Smithsonian NMAH "Electric Dr. Franklin.") The rod on the roof → `technology`; the almanac → `news-communication`.

## 5. 1750 to 1800

- **The kite, told honestly.** Franklin published the design for the lightning experiment; it was **first performed in France** — at Marly-la-Ville on May 10, 1752, with a tall iron rod, arranged by Thomas-François Dalibard following Franklin's printed proposal (report to the French Academy May 13; Founders Archives holds Dalibard's report). Franklin's own kite flight in Philadelphia is traditionally dated June 1752; the exact date and place were never recorded, he first described it in print that October (Pennsylvania Gazette, Oct 19, 1752), and Joseph Priestley's 1767 account supplies the June date. Some historians question details of the kite story; the documented core is: Franklin designed the test, France ran it first, the kite followed, and lightning was shown to be electricity. (Founders Archives; APS News "May 10, 1752"; Franklin Institute; Uman, *Why did Benjamin Franklin fly the kite?*) The KNOWLEDGE (lightning = electricity) is ours; the rod → `technology` (already told there).
- **The transit of Venus, June 3, 1769** — the colonies' first big organized science project. The APS appointed observing committees; at Norriton, David Rittenhouse, William Smith, John Lukens, and John Sellers timed Venus crossing the sun; results filled the first volume of the APS *Transactions* (published 1773) and were reprinted by the Royal Society — the work that put American science on the international map. (APS museum exhibition pages; Royal Society Phil. Trans. 1769; JSTOR record.)
- **David Rittenhouse** (1732–1796): self-taught Pennsylvania instrument maker and astronomer; built celebrated orreries; APS president after Franklin (1791); later first director of the U.S. Mint (that job → `money`). (Penn Today; SciHi; APS records.)
- **Benjamin Banneker** (born Nov 9, 1731, Baltimore County, Maryland; died 1806): free Black tobacco farmer, self-taught in mathematics and astronomy from borrowed books and instruments. Correctly predicted the solar eclipse of April 14, 1789, against the calculations of better-known almanac makers; computed the complete ephemerides (sun, moon, planet positions) for published almanacs for **1792 through 1797**. On **August 19, 1791** he sent Jefferson his manuscript almanac with a letter confronting him on slavery ("...however variable we may be in society or religion, however diversified in situation or colour, we are all of the same family"); Jefferson replied August 30, 1791, complimentary and condescending, and forwarded the almanac to Condorcet in Paris. (Britannica; Encyclopedia.com; Founders Archives for Jefferson's reply; history.com for the letter date.) **His 1791 survey work on the federal district belongs to `city-building` (its existing story) — our story is the astronomy and the almanacs.** Slug must be suffixed: `benjamin-banneker-science` (city-building holds `benjamin-banneker`).
- **Jefferson as natural historian:** *Notes on the State of Virginia* argued with evidence against Buffon's theory that American animals and people were "degenerate" — smaller and weaker in the New World; on March 10, 1797 he presented the fossil "Megalonyx" ("giant claw") paper to the APS (published 1799, the first named fossil genus from the U.S.); Caspar Wistar's companion paper begins American paleontology. (Monticello encyclopedia; American Scientist "Jefferson's Old Bones"; Orton Geological Museum, Ohio State.) Keep short — he is `landmarks`' story elsewhere; here he is one span, not a story block.

## 6. 1800 to 1850

- **Benjamin Silliman:** appointed professor of chemistry and natural history at Yale in 1802 (first lectures 1804); founded the **American Journal of Science, 1818** — the country's first enduring scientific journal, known for decades as "Silliman's Journal." With James Kingsley he documented and chemically analyzed the **Weston, Connecticut meteorite fall of December 14, 1807** — the first scientifically documented meteorite fall in the New World. (Britannica; Connecticut History/CTHumanities; Linda Hall Library.) The often-told Jefferson sneer about "Yankee professors" lying about stones from heaven is apocryphal — do not use it.
- **Joseph Henry:** Albany Academy teacher whose best research years were 1826–1832; built the strongest electromagnets of his day; **discovered self-induction, published July 1832 in Silliman's journal**. On mutual induction the honest line: Faraday in London published electromagnetic induction first (1831); Henry had seen it independently but published late — **Faraday keeps discovery credit; Henry is credited with self-induction, and the unit of inductance is the henry.** Princeton professor 1832; elected **first Secretary of the Smithsonian, December 3, 1846.** From 1849 he ran the Smithsonian Meteorological Project: about **150 volunteer weather observers** reporting by mail and free telegraph transmission, building the first national weather maps; about 600 observers a decade later; the project (1849–1870) led to the National Weather Service. (Britannica; Smithsonian Institution Archives; Princeton physics department history; Smithsonian Magazine "The National Weather Service began as a crowdsourcing experiment"; NWS anniversary pages.)
- **Matthew Fontaine Maury:** superintendent of the Navy's Depot of Charts and Instruments from 1842; mined thousands of archived ships' logs for winds and currents and issued the first **Wind and Current Chart of the North Atlantic in 1847**, then collected standardized logs from captains worldwide — crowdsourced ocean science that cut sailing times; his *The Physical Geography of the Sea* (1855) falls in the next era. Honest note for the writer: in 1861 Maury resigned and joined the Confederacy. (Navy History and Heritage Command biography; LOC Maury Papers; Google Arts & Culture/whoi divediscover.)
- **The Smithsonian and Smithson's strange gift:** James Smithson, English chemist, died June 27, 1829 in Genoa, **never having set foot in the United States**; his will left his estate — if his nephew died without heirs, which happened in 1835 — "to the United States of America, to found at Washington, under the name of the Smithsonian Institution, an establishment for the increase & diffusion of knowledge." More than 100,000 gold sovereigns arrived at the Philadelphia mint in September 1838 — over $500,000 recoined. Congress argued about it for eight years; Polk signed the founding act **August 10, 1846**. Nobody knows exactly why Smithson did it — he left no explanation; say that plainly. (Smithsonian Institution Archives "James Smithson, Founding Donor"; si.edu history; House of Representatives history office.)
- **Maria Mitchell:** on **October 1, 1847**, from the roof of the Pacific National Bank building on Nantucket where her father worked, sweeping the sky with a small telescope, she found a comet no chart showed (C/1847 T1, "Miss Mitchell's Comet"). The King of Denmark's standing gold medal for telescopic comet discoveries went to her — first American and first woman to win it (the Italian observer de Vico had seen it two days later). First woman elected to the American Academy of Arts and Sciences (1848). Her Vassar professorship (1865, first American woman professor of astronomy) and the 1878 eclipse expedition belong to the NEXT era by the boundary rule — tell them there or as a forward note in her story. (Maria Mitchell Association; APS News October 2006; Smithsonian American Women's History Museum; Vassar Encyclopedia.)

### PATCH 2026-09-26 (T-240a): skull collecting and "race science" — Samuel George Morton
- **Who:** Samuel George Morton (1799–1851), Philadelphia physician and naturalist, member of the Academy of Natural Sciences of Philadelphia. He began collecting human skulls in the early 1830s (the trigger was a lecture on skull differences he prepared in 1830). At his death in 1851 he held **867 human skulls** (Penn Museum *Expedition* 50:3, Nov 2008, Renschler and Monge, https://www.penn.museum/sites/expedition/the-samuel-george-morton-cranial-collection/). NPR/Penn Museum coverage (April 2021) says "nearly 900."
- **How he got them:** through **138 contacts** — scientific colleagues, merchants, military men and missionaries. Army surgeons at remote posts were his most useful suppliers, and the collectors "were not above robbing graves" (Penn *Expedition*, as above). Penn Museum's own 2021 account: about **a dozen** skulls came from a Philadelphia potter's field (a burial ground for the poor) where poor Black Philadelphians were buried, and **more than 50** were dug up from a burial ground of enslaved Africans in Cuba; others were taken from battlefields and gallows (NPR/GBH, April 27–28, 2021, https://www.wgbh.org/news/national/2021-04-28/penn-museum-apologizes-for-unethical-possession-of-human-remains). The collection includes many Native American skulls (44 Native skulls were loaned to Spain in 1892 for a Columbus celebration — Penn *Expedition*).
- **What he did with them:** he measured the space inside each skull by filling it with seed (peppercorns; later lead shot), pouring it out and measuring the volume (NPR/GBH 2021). He held that a bigger skull meant more intelligence and ranked "races" by average size, white Europeans at the top. His *Crania Americana* (1839) made him internationally famous; his work was used to argue for a racial hierarchy and to defend slavery (Penn *Expedition*; NPR 2021). Penn Museum describes him as sometimes called the "father of scientific racism" (NPR 2021).
- **The measurement dispute — record both sides:** Stephen Jay Gould (Harvard; *Science* 1978 and *The Mismeasure of Man*, 1981) charged that Morton's numbers were biased. In June 2011 six scholars led by Jason E. Lewis re-measured **308 of the 670** skulls Morton had published and found his measuring errors small and random (*PLoS Biology*, June 7, 2011, https://journals.plos.org/plosbiology/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pbio.1001071); a 2016 *PLoS Biology* comment disputes parts of that. **What is not disputed:** the idea that skull size ranks the intelligence or worth of human groups is false; the argument is only over whether Morton measured carelessly.
- Owner: `science` (the scientist's conduct and the false knowledge). The people buried in the potter's field → `slavery-freedom` may share. Repatriation and apology → era 10 patch below.

## 7. 1850 to 1900

- **Darwin in America — the argument over evidence.** *On the Origin of Species* (London, November 1859) reached Harvard fast. Botanist **Asa Gray** — who negotiated Darwin's American publishing — defended the theory; zoologist **Louis Agassiz**, the most famous scientist in America, attacked it, insisting every species was separately created. ### PATCH 2026-09-26 (T-240a): the Manhattan Project's experiments on people, and Trinity's fallout

## 9. 1950 to 2000

- **DNA, 1953 — the honest version.** James Watson (American, 24) and Francis Crick (British) at Cambridge, England, published the double-helix structure in *Nature*, April 25, 1953. (Nature 2023 "What Rosalind Franklin truly contributed"; history.com; PBS; UKRI archive; CSHL statements.) - **The Big Bang's afterglow, 1964–65:** Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson at Bell Labs' Holmdel, New Jersey horn antenna could not remove a faint microwave hiss — they even cleared out nesting pigeons and their droppings — until Princeton physicists identified it as the cosmic microwave background, the cooled light of the early universe. Nobel 1978. (APS historic site "Holmdel Horn Antenna"; EBSCO; Nokia Bell Labs.)
- **Quarks:** Gell-Mann proposed and named quarks in 1964 (Nobel 1969); in 1968 the MIT–SLAC deep-inelastic scattering experiments (Friedman, Kendall, Taylor — Nobel 1990) showed point-like pieces inside the proton. (NobelPrize.org lectures; APS Physics Landmarks.)
- **Plate tectonics and Marie Tharp.** From 1948 at Columbia's Lamont Geological Observatory, Tharp turned shipboard soundings into seafloor profiles — women were barred from the research ships (she first sailed in 1968) — and in 1952–53 recognized a continuous rift valley splitting the Mid-Atlantic Ridge; her partner Bruce Heezen first dismissed it as "girl talk." Their *Physiographic Diagram of the Atlantic Ocean* appeared in 1957 and the painted World Ocean Floor map in 1977. Harry Hess's 1962 "History of Ocean Basins" proposed seafloor spreading; by about 1970 plate tectonics was the accepted frame — continents move, and the evidence was on Tharp's maps. The Library of Congress later named her among the great cartographers of the 20th century. (LOC blogs; Lamont-Doherty; Science News; Columbia oral history.)
- **What space flight taught (journeys → `exploration`; the LEARNING is ours):**
  - Six Apollo landings (1969–72) returned **382 kilograms (842 pounds)** of Moon rock and soil — 2,200 samples curated at NASA Johnson, still being opened and studied; the rocks anchored the Moon's history and ages across the solar system. (NASA JSC curation; LPI; National Air and Space Museum.)

(Morabito's own historical review, arXiv 1211.2554; Guinness/NASA; JPL.)

### PATCH 2026-09-26 (T-240a): the Lacks family, the genome, and the 2023 settlement (dated)

- **2021–2023:** her estate, represented by Ben Crump and Chris Seeger, sued Thermo Fisher Scientific in 2021 over a dozen products using HeLa cells, alleging the company "made staggering profits by using the HeLa cell line—all while Ms. Lacks' Estate and family haven't seen a dime"; the settlement was announced on **August 1, 2023** — what would have been her 103rd birthday — terms confidential (Smithsonian Magazine Aug 2023; STAT Aug 1, 2023; Boston Globe Aug 1, 2023 — journalism).
