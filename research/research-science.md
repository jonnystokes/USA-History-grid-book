## 1. Before 1500

- **Knowledge framing.** Careful, tested knowledge existed here long before laboratories: astronomy built into structures, plant knowledge, fire ecology, weather and tide knowledge — held by nations and taught on. The respectful, accurate frame is systematic observation over generations. Traditional Ecological Knowledge (TEK) is the term working scientists use; the Ecological Society of America has a TEK section co-founded by Robin Wall Kimmerer (SUNY ESF; MacArthur Foundation 2022 citation).
- **Cahokia's Woodhenge** (Illinois): five successive circles of upright posts, about A.D. 900–1100, 24 to 72 posts each, up to 145 meters across, marking sunrise positions through the year including the solstices and equinoxes. (Cahokia Mounds State Historic Site — verified for the technology bank 2026-08-08; technology tells the built calendar, we tell the sky-knowledge it encodes.)
- **The Sun Dagger, Fajada Butte, Chaco Canyon** (New Mexico): three sandstone slabs lean against a cliff near the butte's summit and channel sunlight into "daggers" of light on two spiral petroglyphs — marking the sun at solstices and equinoxes, and, researchers argue, the moon's 18.6-year standstill cycle. Recorded at summer solstice 1977 by Anna Sofaer, a volunteer documenting rock art. **Honest status: by summer solstice 1989 two of the three slabs had shifted** — erosion accelerated by human foot traffic despite restricted access — and the centered daggers no longer form as first recorded. (NPS Chaco Culture brochure "Fajada Butte Solstice Marker"; Solstice Project; UCAR High Altitude Observatory.) The lunar-standstill claims are argued by the Solstice Project and debated among archaeoastronomers — say "researchers argue," not "it is known."
- **Story candidate → verified:** Robin Wall Kimmerer — Citizen Potawatomi Nation botanist, SUNY Distinguished Teaching Professor at the College of Environmental Science and Forestry, founder of the Center for Native Peoples and the Environment, MacArthur Fellow 2022; *Braiding Sweetgrass* (2013) a New York Times bestseller since February 2020. PhD in botany, University of Wisconsin. She works at exactly this chapter's join: Native knowledge and laboratory science as two ways of knowing used together. (ESF news 2022; MacArthur Foundation fellow page; robinwallkimmerer.com.) No documentary film about her — omit Movie line.

## 2. The 1500s (thin)

- European science barely exists yet (this is still the age of alchemy), and no newcomer is doing sustained study here. What crosses the ocean is collecting and describing.
- **Thomas Hariot, 1585–86 at Roanoke** — English mathematician sent on Raleigh's Roanoke expedition; his *A Briefe and True Report of the New Found Land of Virginia* (1588) was the first book in English about North America by someone who had been there: plants, animals, foods, and the Algonquian people, published with John White's drawings in the 1590 de Bry edition. (Encyclopedia Virginia; UNC Documenting the American South; EBSCO.) He observed here; the knowledge was carried back to Europe — that is the honest 1500s shape.

## 3. The 1600s (thin)

- **Harvard, 1636** — oldest college in the English colonies, founded by vote of the Massachusetts Bay General Court; renamed 1639 for John Harvard's bequest. (harvard.edu history timeline; Britannica.)
- **The first almanac printed in the colonies:** *An Almanack for New England for the Year 1639*, calculated by mariner William Pierce, printed by Stephen Daye at Cambridge — one of the first two things off the first press. Almanacs carried astronomy into ordinary houses for the next two centuries. (Britannica; EBSCO "North America's First Printing Press.")
- **The first telescope:** John Winthrop Jr. — Connecticut governor, elected a fellow of the Royal Society during his 1661–63 London visit (among the first colonial members) — gave his 3.5-foot telescope to Harvard in 1671–72, the college's first scientific instrument. (Linda Hall Library; Museum of Connecticut History; EBSCO. Sources give the gift as 1671 or 1672 — state "about 1672.")
- **The comet of 1680 and the argument about what comets are.** Thomas Brattle (Harvard 1676) tracked the Great Comet of 1680–81 with a telescope from Boston and reasoned the November and December appearances were one comet rounding the sun; his measurements reached Newton, who cited the New England observations in the *Principia*. (American Antiquarian Society proceedings; Colonial Williamsburg journal; Salem Witch Museum historical note.) Increase Mather's *Kometographia* (1683) catalogued comets as divine warnings — yet used parallax to show they were distant bodies, not vapors. He also founded the short-lived Boston Philosophical Society (1683, modeled on the Royal Society; lasted about three years). (Marco Institute, University of Tennessee; Smithsonian Libraries catalog.) The era's honest shape: observation was real, and the observers still read the sky as sermon.
- **Story (ordinary, verified): Rev. John Campanius Holm** — chaplain to the New Sweden colony; recorded the weather twice a day through 1644–45 near present Wilmington, Delaware, without instruments — the first known systematic weather record in what is now the U.S. NOAA's John Campanius Holm Award for volunteer weather observers (up to 25 a year) is named for him. (NWS award pages, weather.gov.)

## 4. 1700 to 1750

- **The American Philosophical Society, 1743, Philadelphia** — oldest learned society in the U.S. The idea came from botanist John Bartram in 1739; Franklin organized it with his 1743 pamphlet *A Proposal for Promoting Useful Knowledge among the British Plantations in America*; grown out of his Junto club, answering London's Royal Society. (amphilsoc.org history; Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia; Britannica.) **KEEP IN THIS ERA — v1 had it under 1750–1800; the 1743 date controls.**
- **John Bartram** (1699–1777): Quaker farmer-botanist; bought a 102-acre farm on the Schuylkill at Kingsessing in 1728 and built America's oldest surviving botanic garden; collecting expeditions from New York to Florida, 1730–1765; named the King's Botanist for North America by George III in 1765; he and his sons introduced over 200 American plants to science. Linnaeus is *reported* to have called him "the greatest natural botanist in the world" — the line is quoted everywhere but is an attribution, label it "Linnaeus reportedly called him." (Bartram's Garden; Encyclopedia.com; Monticello encyclopedia.)
- **Franklin's electrical science, 1747–1751:** experiments in Philadelphia reported in letters to Peter Collinson of the Royal Society; the single-fluid theory with **positive and negative charge** (his terms, still ours), conservation of charge, the proposal that lightning is electrical, and the grounded-rod idea. The letters were published in London, April 1751, as *Experiments and Observations on Electricity* — and in 1753 the Royal Society gave Franklin the Copley Medal, its highest honor, the first to someone outside Britain. First American with an international scientific reputation. (Library of Congress exhibitions; IEEE Milestone; Smithsonian NMAH "Electric Dr. Franklin.") The rod on the roof → `technology`; the almanac → `news-communication`.

## 5. 1750 to 1800

- **The kite, told honestly.** Franklin published the design for the lightning experiment; it was **first performed in France** — at Marly-la-Ville on May 10, 1752, with a tall iron rod, arranged by Thomas-François Dalibard following Franklin's printed proposal (report to the French Academy May 13; Founders Archives holds Dalibard's report). Franklin's own kite flight in Philadelphia is traditionally dated June 1752; the exact date and place were never recorded, he first described it in print that October (Pennsylvania Gazette, Oct 19, 1752), and Joseph Priestley's 1767 account supplies the June date. Some historians question details of the kite story; the documented core is: Franklin designed the test, France ran it first, the kite followed, and lightning was shown to be electricity. (Founders Archives; APS News "May 10, 1752"; Franklin Institute; Uman, *Why did Benjamin Franklin fly the kite?*) The KNOWLEDGE (lightning = electricity) is ours; the rod → `technology` (already told there).
- **The transit of Venus, June 3, 1769** — the colonies' first big organized science project. The APS appointed observing committees; at Norriton, David Rittenhouse, William Smith, John Lukens, and John Sellers timed Venus crossing the sun; results filled the first volume of the APS *Transactions* (published 1773) and were reprinted by the Royal Society — the work that put American science on the international map. (APS museum exhibition pages; Royal Society Phil. Trans. 1769; JSTOR record.)
- **David Rittenhouse** (1732–1796): self-taught Pennsylvania instrument maker and astronomer; built celebrated orreries; APS president after Franklin (1791); later first director of the U.S. Mint (that job → `money`). (Penn Today; SciHi; APS records.)
- **Benjamin Banneker** (born Nov 9, 1731, Baltimore County, Maryland; died 1806): free Black tobacco farmer, self-taught in mathematics and astronomy from borrowed books and instruments. Correctly predicted the solar eclipse of April 14, 1789, against the calculations of better-known almanac makers; computed the complete ephemerides (sun, moon, planet positions) for published almanacs for **1792 through 1797**. On **August 19, 1791** he sent Jefferson his manuscript almanac with a letter confronting him on slavery ("...however variable we may be in society or religion, however diversified in situation or colour, we are all of the same family"); Jefferson replied August 30, 1791, complimentary and condescending, and forwarded the almanac to Condorcet in Paris. (Britannica; Encyclopedia.com; Founders Archives for Jefferson's reply; history.com for the letter date.) **His 1791 survey work on the federal district belongs to `city-building` (its existing story) — our story is the astronomy and the almanacs.** Slug must be suffixed: `benjamin-banneker-science` (city-building holds `benjamin-banneker`).
- **Jefferson as natural historian:** *Notes on the State of Virginia* argued with evidence against Buffon's theory that American animals and people were "degenerate" — smaller and weaker in the New World; on March 10, 1797 he presented the fossil "Megalonyx" ("giant claw") paper to the APS (published 1799, the first named fossil genus from the U.S.); Caspar Wistar's companion paper begins American paleontology. (Monticello encyclopedia; American Scientist "Jefferson's Old Bones"; Orton Geological Museum, Ohio State.) Keep short — he is `landmarks`' story elsewhere; here he is one span, not a story block.

## 6. 1800 to 1850

- **Benjamin Silliman:** appointed professor of chemistry and natural history at Yale in 1802 (first lectures 1804); founded the **American Journal of Science, 1818** — the country's first enduring scientific journal, known for decades as "Silliman's Journal." With James Kingsley he documented and chemically analyzed the **Weston, Connecticut meteorite fall of December 14, 1807** — the first scientifically documented meteorite fall in the New World. (Britannica; Connecticut History/CTHumanities; Linda Hall Library.) The often-told Jefferson sneer about "Yankee professors" lying about stones from heaven is apocryphal — do not use it.
- **Joseph Henry:** Albany Academy teacher whose best research years were 1826–1832; built the strongest electromagnets of his day; **discovered self-induction, published July 1832 in Silliman's journal**. On mutual induction the honest line: Faraday in London published electromagnetic induction first (1831); Henry had seen it independently but published late — **Faraday keeps discovery credit; Henry is credited with self-induction, and the unit of inductance is the henry.** Princeton professor 1832; elected **first Secretary of the Smithsonian, December 3, 1846.** From 1849 he ran the Smithsonian Meteorological Project: about **150 volunteer weather observers** reporting by mail and free telegraph transmission, building the first national weather maps; about 600 observers a decade later; the project (1849–1870) led to the National Weather Service. (Britannica; Smithsonian Institution Archives; Princeton physics department history; Smithsonian Magazine "The National Weather Service began as a crowdsourcing experiment"; NWS anniversary pages.)
- **Matthew Fontaine Maury:** superintendent of the Navy's Depot of Charts and Instruments from 1842; mined thousands of archived ships' logs for winds and currents and issued the first **Wind and Current Chart of the North Atlantic in 1847**, then collected standardized logs from captains worldwide — crowdsourced ocean science that cut sailing times; his *The Physical Geography of the Sea* (1855) falls in the next era. Honest note for the writer: in 1861 Maury resigned and joined the Confederacy. (Navy History and Heritage Command biography; LOC Maury Papers; Google Arts & Culture/whoi divediscover.)
- **The Smithsonian and Smithson's strange gift:** James Smithson, English chemist, died June 27, 1829 in Genoa, **never having set foot in the United States**; his will left his estate — if his nephew died without heirs, which happened in 1835 — "to the United States of America, to found at Washington, under the name of the Smithsonian Institution, an establishment for the increase & diffusion of knowledge." More than 100,000 gold sovereigns arrived at the Philadelphia mint in September 1838 — over $500,000 recoined. Congress argued about it for eight years; Polk signed the founding act **August 10, 1846**. Nobody knows exactly why Smithson did it — he left no explanation; say that plainly. (Smithsonian Institution Archives "James Smithson, Founding Donor"; si.edu history; House of Representatives history office.)
- **Maria Mitchell:** on **October 1, 1847**, from the roof of the Pacific National Bank building on Nantucket where her father worked, sweeping the sky with a small telescope, she found a comet no chart showed (C/1847 T1, "Miss Mitchell's Comet"). The King of Denmark's standing gold medal for telescopic comet discoveries went to her — first American and first woman to win it (the Italian observer de Vico had seen it two days later). First woman elected to the American Academy of Arts and Sciences (1848). Her Vassar professorship (1865, first American woman professor of astronomy) and the 1878 eclipse expedition belong to the NEXT era by the boundary rule — tell them there or as a forward note in her story. (Maria Mitchell Association; APS News October 2006; Smithsonian American Women's History Museum; Vassar Encyclopedia.)

### PATCH 2026-09-26 (T-240a): skull collecting and "race science" — Samuel George Morton
- **Who:** Samuel George Morton (1799–1851), Philadelphia physician and naturalist, member of the Academy of Natural Sciences of Philadelphia. He began collecting human skulls in the early 1830s (the trigger was a lecture on skull differences he prepared in 1830). At his death in 1851 he held **867 human skulls** (Penn Museum *Expedition* 50:3, Nov 2008, Renschler and Monge, https://www.penn.museum/sites/expedition/the-samuel-george-morton-cranial-collection/). NPR/Penn Museum coverage (April 2021) says "nearly 900."
- **How he got them:** through **138 contacts** — scientific colleagues, merchants, military men and missionaries. Army surgeons at remote posts were his most useful suppliers, and the collectors "were not above robbing graves" (Penn *Expedition*, as above). Penn Museum's own 2021 account: about **a dozen** skulls came from a Philadelphia potter's field (a burial ground for the poor) where poor Black Philadelphians were buried, and **more than 50** were dug up from a burial ground of enslaved Africans in Cuba; others were taken from battlefields and gallows (NPR/GBH, April 27–28, 2021, https://www.wgbh.org/news/national/2021-04-28/penn-museum-apologizes-for-unethical-possession-of-human-remains). The collection includes many Native American skulls (44 Native skulls were loaned to Spain in 1892 for a Columbus celebration — Penn *Expedition*).
- **What he did with them:** he measured the space inside each skull by filling it with seed (peppercorns; later lead shot), pouring it out and measuring the volume (NPR/GBH 2021). He held that a bigger skull meant more intelligence and ranked "races" by average size, white Europeans at the top. His *Crania Americana* (1839) made him internationally famous; his work was used to argue for a racial hierarchy and to defend slavery (Penn *Expedition*; NPR 2021). Penn Museum describes him as sometimes called the "father of scientific racism" (NPR 2021).
- **The measurement dispute — record both sides:** Stephen Jay Gould (Harvard; *Science* 1978 and *The Mismeasure of Man*, 1981) charged that Morton's numbers were biased. In June 2011 six scholars led by Jason E. Lewis re-measured **308 of the 670** skulls Morton had published and found his measuring errors small and random (*PLoS Biology*, June 7, 2011, https://journals.plos.org/plosbiology/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pbio.1001071); a 2016 *PLoS Biology* comment disputes parts of that. **What is not disputed:** the idea that skull size ranks the intelligence or worth of human groups is false; the argument is only over whether Morton measured carelessly.
- Owner: `science` (the scientist's conduct and the false knowledge). The people buried in the potter's field → `slavery-freedom` may share. Repatriation and apology → era 10 patch below.

## 7. 1850 to 1900

- **Darwin in America — the argument over evidence.** *On the Origin of Species* (London, November 1859) reached Harvard fast. Botanist **Asa Gray** — who negotiated Darwin's American publishing — defended the theory; zoologist **Louis Agassiz**, the most famous scientist in America, attacked it, insisting every species was separately created. They argued it out at the American Academy of Arts and Sciences in early 1860 (Agassiz opened the attack January 10, 1860); Gray also defended Darwin in Atlantic Monthly essays and later collected them in *Darwiniana* (1876), arguing science and faith could stand together. (American Academy of Arts and Sciences archives; EBSCO; Harvard Magazine "A Wrangle over Darwin.")
- **Agassiz, the whole truth:** he promoted polygenism — the racist doctrine that races were separately created — and in 1850 commissioned daguerreotypes of enslaved South Carolina people (Renty, Delia, and five others), stripped and posed as "evidence." The images, rediscovered at Harvard's Peabody Museum in 1976, are the earliest known photographs of enslaved Americans; the descendants' lawsuit over them was settled in the 2020s. State it plainly: the era's most celebrated scientist used science's authority for racism. (Harvard Magazine; Peabody Museum coverage; Cultural Property News on Lanier v. Harvard.) **Parked for `slavery-freedom`** (the people photographed and the descendants' fight).
- **Land-grant colleges:** Morrill Act, July 2, 1862 → `education` leads the institution story. Our slice, lightly: the colleges made science teaching national, and the **Hatch Act of 1887** funded an agricultural experiment station in every state — research as a public service in every state, not just old-college towns. (LOC/Congress.gov CRS R45897; state experiment station histories.) **Hatch details parked for `food-farming`.**
- **Josiah Willard Gibbs:** Yale; received the **first American doctorate in engineering (Yale, 1863)**; professor of mathematical physics from 1871 — **unpaid for his first nine years** (he lived on inheritance) until an 1880 Johns Hopkins offer forced Yale to pay him. His *On the Equilibrium of Heterogeneous Substances* (Transactions of the Connecticut Academy, in two parts 1876 and 1878) founded chemical thermodynamics — published so obscurely and written so densely that Europe found it before America did; Maxwell built a plaster model of Gibbs's thermodynamic surface with his own hands and mailed it to New Haven. **Copley Medal 1901**, then the top international science honor. (Britannica; Yale physics department; Encyclopedia.com; ACS landmark note.)
- **Albert Michelson:** born 1852 in Strelno, Prussia (now Poland), raised in California and Nevada mining towns; Annapolis graduate. His 1879 speed-of-light measurement at Annapolis came within about 0.05 percent of today's value (299,864±51 km/s in air; 299,940 km/s vacuum estimate — some references print 299,910; note the variance). The **Michelson–Morley experiment (Cleveland, July 1887)** found no trace of the "ether" light was supposed to wave through — a null result that pointed toward relativity. **Nobel Prize in Physics 1907 — the first American science Nobel** (that event sits in the next era by the boundary rule). (US Naval Academy news; Britannica; NobelPrize.org; APS News.)
- **Williamina Fleming and the Harvard computers:** Scottish immigrant, hired around 1879 as a maid in Observatory director Edward Pickering's house; moved onto the observatory staff in 1881; classified more than 10,000 stars for the **Draper Catalogue (1890)**; spotted the Horsehead Nebula on an 1888 plate; discovered 59 nebulae, over 310 variable stars, 10 novae; named Curator of Astronomical Photographs in 1899. Her entry in Harvard's "Chest of 1900" time capsule records that she earned $1,500 a year while men in comparable jobs got $2,500: "Does he ever think that I have a home to keep and a family to take care of as well as the men?" (Harvard Magazine; Linda Hall Library; Harvard Plate Stacks project; New England Historical Society.)
- **The 1878 eclipse:** Maria Mitchell led a team of Vassar students and graduates about 2,000 miles by rail to Denver to observe the total solar eclipse of July 29, 1878 — women running their own expedition, national press watching. (Smithsonian NMAH "Total eclipse of the sun, partial eclipse of inequality"; Vassar Encyclopedia; sources also print July 28 for departure/setup dates — the eclipse was July 29.)

### PATCH 2026-09-26 (T-240a): the Army Medical Museum's skull collecting from Native dead
- **The order:** the Surgeon General's Office issued **Circular No. 2 in 1867**, asking Army medical officers to collect skulls and specimens of Native weapons, dress, tools, food and medicine; from **1868** the U.S. Army Medical Museum in Washington specifically asked Army doctors in "Indian country" to send Native American skulls (Smithsonian National Anthropological Archives, finding aid "United States Army Medical Museum records concerning skeletal material transferred to the Smithsonian Institution," NAA.XXXX.0330, https://sova.si.edu/record/NAA.XXXX.0330 — read from the search summary; direct fetch blocked). The purpose was **craniometry** (skull-measuring), "a branch of racial science commonly used to delineate the different varieties of mankind and to rank them according to their perceived intellectual attributes" (Elise Juzda, "Skulls, science, and the spoils of war: craniological studies at the United States Army Medical Museum, 1868–1900," *Studies in History and Philosophy of Biological and Biomedical Sciences* 40:3, 2009, pp. 156–167 — abstract via search summary).
- **Who:** George A. Otis, the museum's curator, collected and measured hundreds of these skulls. **A named case:** in May 1864 a Dakota man, about 25–35 years old, was killed near an Army outpost in rural Minnesota; acting assistant surgeon **Alfred Muller** examined his remains and sent them to the Army Medical Museum; they ended at the Smithsonian. The man's name is not recorded — tell him unnamed (Samuel J. Redman, "When Museums Rushed to Fill Their Rooms With Bones," *Smithsonian Magazine*, 2016, adapted from his book *Bone Rooms*, https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/when-museums-rushed-fill-their-rooms-bones-180958424/).
- **How remains were taken (Redman, as above):** dug from graves, sometimes months after burial; taken from battlefields after killings; removed from whole cemeteries; sent in unasked.
- **Count:** the cranial collection held **about 3,000** specimens by around 1900; Native remains (except those of surgical interest) were transferred to the Smithsonian's national museum in **1898 and again in 1904** (NAA finding aid, as above; a Candid "Investing in Native Communities" timeline entry gives "2,000 crania" for the donation — record both figures).
- Owner: `science` (the scientists and the ranking). The wars the soldiers were fighting → `war`; the nations' fight to recover their dead → `native-nations` (lead on NAGPRA).

## 8. 1900 to 1950

- **First American science Nobel:** Michelson, Physics, 1907 (NobelPrize.org).
- **Millikan:** oil-drop experiments 1909–1913 measured the electric charge of a single electron — charge comes in exact chunks; Nobel 1923. (APS Physics "Landmarks"; Britannica.)
- **Henrietta Swan Leavitt:** Harvard computer at 30 cents an hour; deaf after an illness in her twenties. Her 1912 paper on 25 Cepheid variable stars in the Small Magellanic Cloud showed the period–luminosity law — a star's blink rate tells its true brightness, turning Cepheids into distance markers for the universe. Died December 12, 1921; in 1925 a Swedish mathematician wrote to begin a Nobel nomination, not knowing she had died. (Harvard Plate Stacks; APS News December 2017; Britannica.)
- **Edwin Hubble:** at Mount Wilson's 100-inch Hooker telescope, found a Cepheid in the Andromeda "nebula" on October 4/5, 1923 (plate marked "VAR!"); with Leavitt's law the distance came out ~900,000 light-years — far outside the Milky Way; announced to the AAS meeting January 1, 1925. In 1929 (PNAS, March) he published the velocity–distance relation with Milton Humason's spectra: the farther the galaxy, the faster it recedes — the expanding universe. Honesty note: Georges Lemaître (Belgium) had derived and published expansion in 1927 in a little-read journal; in 2018 the IAU renamed the law the Hubble–Lemaître law. (NASA "Hubble's discovery of a new universe"; Carnegie Observatories timeline; earthsky/APS histories.)
- **Robert Goddard:** 1919/January 1920 Smithsonian paper *A Method of Reaching Extreme Altitudes*; the **New York Times editorial of January 13, 1920 ("A Severe Strain on Credulity")** mocked him for thinking rockets could push in vacuum, saying he lacked "the knowledge ladled out daily in high schools." **March 16, 1926, Auburn, Massachusetts:** first liquid-fuel rocket flight — 41 feet up, 2.5 seconds, landing 184 feet away in a farm field. **July 17, 1969**, with Apollo 11 en route to the Moon, the Times printed its correction: "...it is now definitely established that a rocket can function in a vacuum as well as in an atmosphere. The Times regrets the error." (NASA history "95 Years Ago"; nss.org; Forbes/Smithsonian Air & Space accounts of the correction.)
- **Thomas Hunt Morgan:** Columbia "fly room"; the white-eyed fruit fly of 1910 opened 17 years of work proving genes sit in line on chromosomes; **Nobel in Physiology or Medicine 1933** — the first Nobel for genetics. (NobelPrize.org article and facts pages; Nature Scitable.)
- **Einstein in America:** arrived October 17, 1933 as the Nazis took Germany, joining the new Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton (a founding faculty member); naturalized a U.S. citizen October 1, 1940 (kept Swiss citizenship). **August 2, 1939:** signed the letter drafted by Leo Szilard (with Teller and Wigner) warning Roosevelt that Germany might build atomic bombs — the letter that started the chain to the Manhattan Project (the bomb itself → `war`). May 1946: honorary degree at Lincoln University, the Black university in Pennsylvania, where he called racism "a disease of white people" and said "I do not intend to be quiet about it." His great physics (relativity, 1905–1915) was done in Europe — our story is the refugee decades. (IAS; USCIS records; Atomic Heritage Foundation/dannen.com for the letter; Snopes/Harvard Gazette for Lincoln University.)
- **Fission and CP-1 — the science under the bomb.** Fission was discovered in Berlin in December 1938 (chemists Otto Hahn and Fritz Strassmann); the explanation came in January 1939 from Lise Meitner — a Jewish refugee then in Sweden — and her nephew Otto Frisch; Meitner was left out of the 1944 Nobel to Hahn (say it). The news crossed to America in January 1939. On **December 2, 1942**, under the west stands of the University of Chicago's disused Stagg Field, Enrico Fermi's team of 49 ran **Chicago Pile-1**, the first controlled, self-sustaining nuclear chain reaction — about half a watt, 28 minutes. Fermi himself: Italian Nobel laureate (1938) who sailed straight from the Stockholm ceremony to New York because Italy's new racial laws targeted his Jewish wife, Laura. (APS historic site; Atomic Heritage Foundation; Argonne history pages; APS News December 1938.) **Shared:** `war` leads the bomb and the decision; `elements` has plutonium; we own the physics and the laboratory system.
- **The Radiation Laboratory at Berkeley** — founded around Lawrence's cyclotrons (independent lab from July 1, 1936; housed from 1931 in a converted campus building). The model of the big-machine, big-team laboratory that came to define postwar American science. Lawrence's 1939 Nobel was the first at Berkeley. (LBL "Invention for the Ages" — carried over, verified for the elements bank.)
- **The wartime laboratory system:** the Metallurgical Laboratory in Chicago (CP-1; curium and americium identified), Clinton Laboratories at Oak Ridge (promethium 1945; became ORNL), Los Alamos — the Manhattan Project as the origin of the national-laboratory system. (ORNL history; Atomic Heritage — carried over.)
- **Seaborg's actinide concept:** Glenn Seaborg redrew the periodic table by pulling the actinide row out beneath the lanthanides — the idea that predicted the chemistry of unmade elements. (Nobel Foundation 1951; Science History Institute — carried over. `elements` tells the discovery run.)
- **The transistor's physics (December 1947 — this era by the boundary rule):** Bardeen and Brattain first amplified a signal with a solid-state device December 16, 1947 at Bell Labs (point-contact transistor), in William Shockley's group; Shockley's junction design followed; announced June 30, 1948; Nobel Prize in Physics 1956 to all three. Bardeen's quantum-mechanical surface-state work is the science story; the device → `technology` (told there). (Computer History Museum "Silicon Engine — 1947"; IEEE Milestone; NobelPrize.org — verified 2026-08-08.)
- **Barbara McClintock:** PhD Cornell 1927; maize cytogenetics; at Cold Spring Harbor from 1941–42. Discovered transposition — genes that move ("controlling elements," Ac/Ds) — in experiments begun in 1944; first description 1948; published *The Origin and Behavior of Mutable Loci in Maize* (PNAS, 1950); presented at the 1951 Cold Spring Harbor Symposium. Reception: commonly told as "ignored"; the record shows respect for her as a cytogeneticist but decades of little uptake of transposition until molecular biology caught up in the 1960s–70s — tell it that way. **Nobel in Physiology or Medicine, October 1983, unshared — the first woman to win that prize alone** (the Nobel event → next era; tell the work here, note the Nobel). (NLM Profiles in Science; Britannica; Cold Spring Harbor Laboratory; NobelPrize.org 1983 press release.)
- **Emilio Segrè:** Italian physicist visiting Lawrence's Berkeley Radiation Lab in summer 1938 when Mussolini's antisemitic racial laws stripped his Palermo professorship — he stayed, taking an underpaid research-assistant job from Lawrence; later co-discovered technetium, astatine, plutonium-239, and (with Owen Chamberlain) the antiproton — **Nobel in Physics 1959.** (Berkeley/OAC Segrè papers; Encyclopedia.com; NobelPrize.org.) Immigration-and-language thread; `elements` has his element work under `emilio-segre-elements`.
- **Citizen science begins a century of counting: the first Christmas Bird Count, December 25, 1900** — ornithologist Frank M. Chapman (American Museum of Natural History; founder-editor of *Bird-Lore*) proposed counting birds instead of the traditional Christmas "side hunt" (shooting everything in reach); **27 birders ran 25 counts** from Toronto to Pacific Grove, California, tallying about 18,500 birds of 90 species. The count has run every winter since — one of the longest-running citizen-science datasets on Earth. (Audubon "History of the Christmas Bird Count"; National Geographic.)

### PATCH 2026-09-26 (T-240a): Aleš Hrdlička and the Smithsonian's "racial brain collection"
- **Who:** Aleš Hrdlička, head of physical anthropology at the Smithsonian's National Museum of Natural History (from 1903, into the early 1940s). He believed white people were superior and collected body parts to support theories of anatomical difference between races, since shown false. In 1903 he began what he himself called the **"racial brain collection."** (Washington Post investigation, August 2023, https://www.washingtonpost.com/history/interactive/2023/ales-hrdlicka-smithsonian-brains-racism/ — journalism, label; reported via search summary.)
- **Numbers (Washington Post, Aug 2023):** the museum holds **more than 30,700** human remains; **more than 19,000 (about 62 percent)** were collected while Hrdlička led the division; **at least 255 brains**, most collected by him or at his direction. Most were gathered without consent of the dead or their families — from people who died in hospitals, were poor, or had no relatives nearby to claim and bury them — and collectors also dug up burial grounds. Hrdlička and a small team dug up the remains of **about 1,000 people in Alaska** and shipped them to the Smithsonian.
- Owner: `science`. Apology → era 10 patch.

## 9. 1950 to 2000

- **DNA, 1953 — the honest version.** James Watson (American, 24) and Francis Crick (British) at Cambridge, England, published the double-helix structure in *Nature*, April 25, 1953. They depended on Rosalind Franklin's X-ray work at King's College London: Maurice Wilkins showed Watson Franklin's "Photo 51" in early 1953 **without her knowledge** (accounts date it late January or February), and her data also reached them through an informal MRC report via Max Perutz. Franklin's own paper ran in the same issue. She died in 1958, at 37; the 1962 Nobel went to Watson, Crick, and Wilkins (no posthumous prizes). Watson's later story is also on the record: Cold Spring Harbor stripped his honorary titles in 2019 after repeated racist statements — the writer can say so in one line. (Nature 2023 "What Rosalind Franklin truly contributed"; history.com; PBS; UKRI archive; CSHL statements.) American angle: Watson is the American in the story, and American labs (Pauling's Caltech among them) were racing for the same answer.
- **The Big Bang's afterglow, 1964–65:** Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson at Bell Labs' Holmdel, New Jersey horn antenna could not remove a faint microwave hiss — they even cleared out nesting pigeons and their droppings — until Princeton physicists identified it as the cosmic microwave background, the cooled light of the early universe. Nobel 1978. (APS historic site "Holmdel Horn Antenna"; EBSCO; Nokia Bell Labs.)
- **Quarks:** Gell-Mann proposed and named quarks in 1964 (Nobel 1969); in 1968 the MIT–SLAC deep-inelastic scattering experiments (Friedman, Kendall, Taylor — Nobel 1990) showed point-like pieces inside the proton. (NobelPrize.org lectures; APS Physics Landmarks.)
- **Plate tectonics and Marie Tharp.** From 1948 at Columbia's Lamont Geological Observatory, Tharp turned shipboard soundings into seafloor profiles — women were barred from the research ships (she first sailed in 1968) — and in 1952–53 recognized a continuous rift valley splitting the Mid-Atlantic Ridge; her partner Bruce Heezen first dismissed it as "girl talk." Their *Physiographic Diagram of the Atlantic Ocean* appeared in 1957 and the painted World Ocean Floor map in 1977. Harry Hess's 1962 "History of Ocean Basins" proposed seafloor spreading; by about 1970 plate tectonics was the accepted frame — continents move, and the evidence was on Tharp's maps. The Library of Congress later named her among the great cartographers of the 20th century. (LOC blogs; Lamont-Doherty; Science News; Columbia oral history.)
- **What space flight taught (journeys → `exploration`; the LEARNING is ours):**
  - Six Apollo landings (1969–72) returned **382 kilograms (842 pounds)** of Moon rock and soil — 2,200 samples curated at NASA Johnson, still being opened and studied; the rocks anchored the Moon's history and ages across the solar system. (NASA JSC curation; LPI; National Air and Space Museum.)
  - **Viking 1 and 2 (1976):** first successful Mars landers; their life-detection experiments returned mixed signals — one (Labeled Release) looked positive, another found no organic matter; most scientists now credit soil chemistry (perchlorates), while LR investigator Gilbert Levin argued for life to the end. An honest unresolved episode. (space.com/EarthSky summaries of the Viking debate; NASA.)
  - **Voyager 1 and 2 (launched 1977):** on March 9, 1979, JPL navigation engineer **Linda Morabito**, processing a routine navigation image, spotted a 270-km plume off the edge of Io — the first active volcano found beyond Earth. The two probes went on to remake knowledge of the outer planets (Io's volcanoes, Europa's cracked ice shell, Titan's thick atmosphere, then Uranus 1986, Neptune 1989) and are now in interstellar space. (Morabito's own historical review, arXiv 1211.2554; Guinness/NASA; JPL.)
  - Science on television: Carl Sagan's *Cosmos* (PBS, fall 1980) became the most-watched American public-TV series of its decade, eventually seen by an estimated 500 million people worldwide. (PBS SoCal; spacedaily summary — journalism, label.)
- **Climate knowledge is built by measurement:** Charles David Keeling (Scripps) began continuous CO2 measurement at Mauna Loa in **March 1958** (first reading 313 ppm; about 315 ppm as the early-year average); the rising, saw-toothed **Keeling Curve** — past 430 ppm by 2026 — is the base measurement under climate science. (Scripps keelingcurve.ucsd.edu; ACS National Historic Chemical Landmark; NOAA; APS News.) `land-environment` leads the warming's story on the land; ours is how the knowledge was built.
- **The national-laboratory network in the Cold War:** Lawrence Berkeley, Lawrence Livermore, Argonne, Los Alamos, Oak Ridge — funding, machines, and teams, with the U.S.–Soviet rivalry running through the science (the element hunt as one thread — `elements` tells those discoveries). (LANL/LBL/LLNL/ORNL official histories — carried over.)
- **One-atom chemistry:** Darleane Hoffman's group did chemistry on single atoms of the heaviest elements — testing where the periodic table's patterns bend; in 1971 she reported plutonium-244 occurring in nature. (ACS biography; Physics Today — carried over.)
- **Richard Feynman:** Los Alamos in the war; rebuilt quantum electrodynamics — **Nobel 1965** with Schwinger and Tomonaga; the Feynman diagrams every physicist now draws; famous as a plain explainer (the Caltech lectures; *Surely You're Joking*, 1985). On **February 11, 1986**, on the televised Challenger commission, he dropped a piece of O-ring rubber into ice water and showed it lost its spring — the cold-launch failure made visible in one minute. Died 1988. (NobelPrize.org 1965; feynman.com Challenger appendix; APS/Nautilus accounts; the demo date from commission records.)
- **McClintock's Nobel, October 1983** — noted here; the work is told in the previous era. (NobelPrize.org.)
- **The Human Genome Project launched October 1, 1990** — NIH and the Department of Energy, planned as 15 years and about $3 billion; working draft announced June 2000. Completion → next era. (genome.gov HGP timeline.)

### PATCH 2026-09-26 (T-240a): the laws that began returning the dead (1989, 1990)
- **National Museum of the American Indian Act, November 28, 1989** — required the Smithsonian to inventory its Native human remains and return them on request; the Smithsonian's repatriation runs under this law, not NAGPRA (ProPublica repatriation database notes, https://projects.propublica.org/repatriation-nagpra-database/).
- **NAGPRA (Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act), November 16, 1990** — requires museums and agencies that take federal money to inventory Native American human remains and sacred objects and return them to descendants and nations (NPS NAGPRA program, https://www.nps.gov/subjects/nagpra/; already banked in `research-native-nations.md`, which leads). Our line: scientists and museums were the ones holding the dead, and the law made them give them back. `native-nations` leads the nations' campaign.

## 10. 2000 to Today (current through 2026)

- **The genome finished:** the International Human Genome Sequencing Consortium declared the sequence essentially complete in **April 2003** (~92% of the genome, <400 gaps), timed to the DNA structure's 50th anniversary; the Telomere-to-Telomere consortium published the first truly complete, gapless genome **March 31, 2022**. (genome.gov 2003 release and timeline; T2T announcement.)
- **The Higgs boson, July 4, 2012** — CERN's ATLAS and CMS experiments (Geneva) announced the particle that gives others mass; about 1,700 U.S. scientists worked on the LHC experiments through Fermilab (CMS) and Brookhaven (ATLAS). **The honest American part:** Congress cancelled the Superconducting Super Collider (Waxahachie, Texas — a planned 54-mile ring) in **October 1993** after $2 billion spent and 14+ miles of tunnel dug; the discovery machine was built in Europe instead. Say it plainly. (CERN press releases; DOE July 4 2012 page; Texas Monthly; Physics Today "A bridge too far"; Scientific American.)
- **Gravitational waves:** on **September 14, 2015**, the twin NSF-funded LIGO detectors (Hanford, Washington and Livingston, Louisiana) recorded spacetime ringing from two black holes that collided 1.3 billion light-years away — announced February 11, 2016, a century after Einstein predicted the waves. **Nobel 2017: Rainer Weiss, Barry Barish, Kip Thorne.** Weiss: born Berlin 1932, family fled the Nazis via Prague, arrived New York January 1939; MIT professor who worked out the laser-interferometer detector idea in 1972. (LIGO/Caltech press; NobelPrize.org 2017; Weiss Nobel biographical; Physics World.)
- **CRISPR:** Jennifer Doudna (UC Berkeley) and Emmanuelle Charpentier published the programmable CRISPR-Cas9 DNA-cutting paper in *Science*, June 28, 2012; **Nobel in Chemistry 2020 — the first science Nobel shared by two women alone.** The medicine built on it → `health` (first CRISPR therapy, Casgevy, FDA-approved December 8, 2023 — parked there). (Science/AAAS; NobelPrize.org 2020 press release; Berkeley News.)
- **JWST:** launched December 25, 2021; first science images released July 11–12, 2022; has pushed the record for earliest known galaxies deep into the universe's first few hundred million years and read gases (first clear CO2 detection, WASP-39b, 2022) in exoplanet atmospheres. (NASA/STScI; Nature Early Release Science papers.)
- **AI arrives in science:** DeepMind's AlphaFold2 (2020) solved practical protein-structure prediction; free databases now hold over 200 million predicted protein structures. **Nobel in Chemistry 2024:** half to **David Baker** (University of Washington — American, computational protein design), half to Demis Hassabis and John Jumper (DeepMind, a British lab) — state the mixed nationality honestly. Wider lab use of AI tools in the 2020s: report as ongoing, label journalism. (NobelPrize.org 2024; EMBL; press coverage — journalism.)
- **The superheavy element program as international science:** IUPAC/IUPAP joint working parties judging discovery claims (2015 recognitions); Dubna–Livermore–Oak Ridge collaborations; Berkeley's 2024 titanium-beam livermorium result setting up an element-120 attempt. The "island of stability" idea is the physics motive. (IUPAC press releases; Berkeley Lab News Center July 2024; DOE Office of Science — carried over. `elements` leads the discoveries.)
- **Story (ordinary, verified): Monica Dunford** — American experimental physicist, then a postdoc on the ATLAS experiment, one of the six physicists followed in the documentary *Particle Fever* (2013, dir. Mark Levinson, produced/narrated by physicist David Kaplan) through the LHC start-up to the July 4, 2012 Higgs announcement. A documented working scientist's daily life in a 3,000-person experiment. (Particle Fever credits/IMDb; Symmetry magazine "Particle Fever: where are they now?")
