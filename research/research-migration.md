## 1. Before the 1500s

- The land was already crossed by Native movement, over and over, long before any European saw it. Migration here does not begin with covered wagons; it begins with people who had been moving across this continent for thousands of years. The first peopling — on foot, from Asia, in the ice age — is told in Ch1 Exploration; noted here only in brief.
- **People moved to build, and moved again when places were left.** The largest town north of Mexico was **Cahokia**, on the Mississippi River near present-day St. Louis, Illinois. It was occupied from about 700 to about 1400 and peaked around the year 1100 with roughly **10,000 to 20,000 people** inside the city (some estimates put up to about 50,000 in the surrounding area) — a settlement comparable in size to London at that time. By about 1350 its people had dispersed, and it was largely empty by the 1400s. The reasons are debated: drought, floods, and strain on wood and food are the leading explanations, and there is no single settled cause. (Sources: Live Science; Illinois state historic-site materials; Cambridge/Berkeley research summaries.)
- **Chaco Canyon**, an Ancestral Puebloan center in present-day New Mexico, began to break up around 1140, after a run of severe droughts, and was largely abandoned by the end of the 1100s. Its people moved out and regathered into the Pueblo communities of the Southwest. (Sources: History.com; Discover Magazine; National Geographic.)
- **A long migration south — the Athabascan (Dené) move.** The ancestors of the **Navajo (Diné) and the Apache** came from the far north, in present-day western Canada and eastern Alaska, and moved south along the edge of the Rockies and Plains toward the Southwest. Scholars disagree on the timing: many place their arrival in the Puebloan Southwest in roughly the **1400s to about 1525**, while others argue they came earlier, in the 1200s. There is no settled date. State the disagreement, not one year. (Sources: EBSCO Research Starters; New Mexico history classroom materials; Native American Netroots; ResearchGate paper on Athapaskan migration.)
- **The whole land was in motion in smaller ways too.** Peoples followed the seasons (seasonal rounds for hunting, fishing, and gathering), and long trade routes carried goods across great distances — turquoise, seashells, copper, and obsidian moved hundreds of miles between nations. The continent was laced with Native trails and trade paths that later newcomers would follow.
- **State of the land:** entirely Native land, home to hundreds of nations. Nothing of Europe was here yet. No horses, no wagons, no roads in the later sense.
- **Featured person:** none. No named individuals are documented this far back for this chapter.

## 2. The 1500s

- **Still Native land, coast to coast.** Europeans reached the edges of the continent in this century (their journeys are Ch1; the few who came to stay are Ch2), but there was no mass movement of settlers across the interior. The interior stayed Native.
- **The one change that would reshape movement later: the horse comes back.** Horses had lived in North America during the ice age and had died out here thousands of years earlier. The Spanish brought them back. Christopher Columbus carried horses on his second voyage in 1493, and Spanish colonists brought them onto the mainland during the 1500s — with expeditions such as Francisco Vázquez de Coronado's (1540–1542) and, at the century's end, Juan de Oñate's colony in New Mexico (1598). (Sources: Smithsonian National Museum of the American Indian, "A Song for the Horse Nation"; History.com; Wind River materials.)
- The horse did not yet change life across the Plains in the 1500s. That spread — and what it did to how Native peoples traveled and hunted — belongs to the next sections.
- **Featured person:** none.

## 3. The 1600s

- **Newcomers clung to the Atlantic coast.** English, Dutch, and others planted settlements along the seaboard (Jamestown 1607, Plymouth 1620 — arrival is Ch2). Almost no one pushed across the interior. Two walls held them near the ocean: the Appalachian Mountains, and the many Native nations who lived beyond them. The interior remained Native land.
- **The big movement story of the 1600s is the spread of the horse among Native peoples — and it made whole nations more mobile.**
  - **The traditional account (Pueblo Revolt, 1680).** In 1680 the Pueblo peoples of present-day New Mexico, led by a Tewa religious leader named Popé, rose against Spanish rule, killed about 400 Spaniards, and drove the rest out for more than a decade. Large numbers of horses — perhaps more than 1,500, the most to pass into Native hands at one time — were left behind. From there, horses moved north and east along Native trade routes: to the Ute, Navajo, and Apache, then the Comanche, Kiowa, and Shoshone, and on to the northern Plains nations (Lakota, Crow, Cheyenne, Blackfoot, Nez Perce) over the following decades. (Sources: Smithsonian NMAI; History.com; EBSCO on the Pueblo Revolt.)
  - **A newer account (horses earlier than the revolt).** A 2023 study in the journal *Science* (led by Yale/University of Colorado archaeologist William Taylor and Lakota, Comanche, Pawnee, and other partners) radiocarbon-dated horse remains and found that many Native groups across the Plains and Rockies had already taken up the horse by the **early 1600s** — decades before 1680 — with specimens from Kansas, New Mexico, and Wyoming dating to the 1500s–1600s. Tooth and bone evidence showed the horses were bridled and fed maize, meaning they were being kept and cared for, not running wild. This matches Native oral histories. State both accounts; the exact timing is unsettled. (Sources: *Science*, 2023, "Early dispersal of domestic horses into the Great Plains and northern Rockies"; PBS NewsHour; Science News; Smithsonian.)
  - **What it changed:** mounted peoples such as the Comanche, Lakota, and Cheyenne could follow the buffalo herds and travel farther and faster than before. Over the next century the horse reshaped how the Plains nations lived and moved.
- **State of the land:** still overwhelmingly Native. Settler country was a thin strip on the coast.
- **Featured person:** none (told as history).

### PATCH 2026-09-26 (T-244): whose land Hooker's company settled at Hartford
- The Hartford site was **Suckiaug** (also spelled Saukiog, "black earth"), the main village of the River Indians led by the sachem **Sequassen**, eldest son of **Sowheag** (Native Northeast Portal, "Sequassen" biography, a Dartmouth/Yale-linked academic portal). Other writers call these people Saukiog or group them with the Wangunk (Wikipedia "Saukiog," "Wangunk," secondary).
- **The deed:** in 1636 Sequassen and his people conveyed the Suckiaug lands to **Samuel Stone and William Goodwin**, two of Hooker's company, for the settlers: "all the land from Wethersfield bounds on the south to Windsor bounds on the north," and six miles west of the Connecticut River. The deed was not recorded at the time, and the terms were disputed between settlers and the Native owners for generations (Native Northeast Portal; hartfordhistory.net "The Founding of Hartford"). What Sequassen's people received is not stated in these sources.
- **Dutch first:** Dutch traders had already built a fort, the House of Hope, where the Park River meets the Connecticut, before Hooker arrived (hartfordhistory.net; search summary). Hooker's company were not the first Europeans there.

## 4. 1700 to 1750

- **Settlers began pushing inland — into the "backcountry."** As the coastal areas filled and land there grew expensive, newcomers moved into the foothills and valleys behind the older settlements.
- **The Great Wagon Road — the main inland route.** It ran roughly **730 miles** from Philadelphia southwest through the Shenandoah Valley of Virginia and down the Piedmont of the Carolinas toward Augusta, Georgia. Heavy use began in the 1720s and grew after the **1744 Treaty of Lancaster**, in which the colonies extinguished Iroquois claims to the Shenandoah Valley, opening it to settlers. (Sources: Ancestry historical insights; Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia; NCpedia; South Carolina Encyclopedia.)
- **Who was moving:** German (the "Pennsylvania Dutch," from *Deutsch*) and **Scots-Irish** immigrant families who found Pennsylvania land too costly moved south and inland down the valley; Moravians and others came too. (These groups' arrival from Europe is Ch2; their movement inland along the Great Wagon Road is the migration angle here.)
- **State of the land:** the country beyond the mountains was still Native land, and the Appalachians were still the wall. Settlement was filling in the near valleys, not crossing to the far side yet.
- **Featured person:** none (connective history; documented named travelers come with the diaries of the 1800s).

### PATCH 2026-09-26 (T-244): why the Tuscarora moved north, and who did it
- **The war that drove the move:** Colonel **James Moore** of South Carolina led a force of North and South Carolina militia and allied Native warriors against the Tuscarora fort of **Neoheroka** (also spelled Nooherooka) on Contentnea Creek, in present-day Greene County, North Carolina. The siege began March 1, 1713, the main attack came March 20, and the fort fell **March 23, 1713**. Moore's men dug under an outer wall and set the fort on fire (NC Department of Natural and Cultural Resources [DNCR], "Culminating Battle of the Tuscarora War, 1713"; NCpedia, "Nooherooka").
- **Counts, with whose count:** DNCR: **950** Native people killed or captured at Neoheroka. A search summary of NCpedia/DNCR material splits this as nearly **600** men, women and children killed and almost **400** taken and sold into slavery. War-wide, DNCR gives about **200 colonists and 1,000 Native people dead**, about **1,000 Tuscarora sold into slavery**, and **more than 3,000** others forced from their homes. These are DNCR's figures. The enslaved captives were sold by the victors, and the sources checked do not name the buyers.
- **Link to the migration:** the survivors' walk north (the bank's "about 1,500 survivors," North Carolina History Project) followed this defeat and the enslavement of their relatives. `native-nations` leads on the war.

### PATCH 2026-09-26 (T-244): whose land the Great Wagon Road crossed
- When settlers began arriving in the Shenandoah Valley about **1729**, the **Shawnee** were the main residents near present-day Winchester. Iroquois, Delaware (Lenape), Shawnee and Conoy people used the valley, and its north-south trail was a warpath between the Seneca and Lenape to the north and the Catawba to the south (virginiaplaces.org, "Encouraging Settlement and Land Grants West of the Blue Ridge," by Charles Grymes, George Mason University. Wikipedia "Winchester, Virginia," secondary).
- At the **Treaty of Lancaster (1744)**, Virginia's Governor **Gooch**'s commissioners bought the Six Nations' (Haudenosaunee) claim to the valley. The treaty also gave colonists the right to use the "Indian Road," later the Great Wagon Road (virginiaplaces.org, "Key Treaties"; World History Commons, treaty excerpts). Payment: one secondary summary says £200 in goods and gold. Other accounts differ, so do not state a figure without checking the treaty text.

## 5. 1750 to 1800

- **The first big crowds crossed the Appalachian Mountains.**
- **Cumberland Gap and the Wilderness Road.** The Cumberland Gap is a natural notch in the mountains near where Kentucky, Virginia, and Tennessee meet. Daniel Boone and about 30 axmen cut the Wilderness Road through it in 1775 (Boone the trailblazer is Ch1). The crowds who followed are the story here: **as many as 300,000 settlers** traveled the Wilderness Road into Kentucky and beyond between 1775 and about 1810. Kentucky filled quickly and became a state in 1792. (Sources: Britannica; History.com; Legends of America.)
- **Into the Ohio Valley after the Revolution.** With the war over (1783), settlers poured into the Ohio Valley and the Northwest Territory — the land north of the Ohio River that became Ohio, Indiana, Illinois, Michigan, and Wisconsin.
- **How the land was organized for settlers (this shaped the whole march west):**
  - **Land Ordinance of 1785** (adopted May 20, 1785): set up the rectangular survey. Land was measured into **townships six miles square**, each divided into **36 sections of 640 acres**, with one section in each township set aside to fund public schools. This grid is why so much of the American countryside is laid out in squares, and it made western land easy to survey, sell, and settle. (Sources: Encyclopedia.com; Indiana Historical Bureau; Wikipedia, cross-checked.)
  - **Northwest Ordinance of 1787** (enacted July 13, 1787): set the rules for governing the territory and for turning it into new states on equal footing with the old ones. It banned slavery north of the Ohio River. Five states came from it. (Sources: Britannica; National Archives; American History Central.)
- **State of the land:** the crowds moving in pushed onto Native land, and fighting followed north of the Ohio in the 1790s (the war/conflict itself → Ch15; the injustice of the land loss → Ch17). Beyond the Mississippi the land was still Native, Spanish, and French.
- **Featured person:** none yet (the crowds are documented as groups, not named individuals; the great trail diaries begin in the next section).

### PATCH 2026-09-26 (T-244): who ordered and carried out the Acadian expulsion
- **Order:** on **July 28, 1755**, Lieutenant Governor **Charles Lawrence** of Nova Scotia and his council in Halifax (at that meeting including Admiral **Boscawen** and Chief Justice **Belcher**) decided to remove the "French inhabitants" from the colony. Acadian delegates who refused an unqualified oath of loyalty to Britain were jailed first. Council members included recent New England arrivals who wanted Acadian farmland (Nova Scotia Archives, "An Acadian Parish Reborn"; The Canadian Encyclopedia, "Acadian Expulsion (the Great Upheaval)").
- **Carrying it out:** Lieutenant Colonel **John Winslow**, commanding New England troops, ordered the men of Grand-Pré to gather in the church of Saint-Charles-des-Mines at 3 p.m. on **September 5, 1755**, and held them there. Colonel **Robert Monckton** and Major **John Handfield** led arrests and removals in other districts (The Canadian Encyclopedia; acadian.org "The Deportation Orders," heritage-society source).

### PATCH 2026-09-26 (T-244): whose land the Wilderness Road crowds entered
- **Kentucky:** on **March 14-17, 1775** at Sycamore Shoals on the Watauga River, **Richard Henderson**'s Transylvania Company obtained a deed from Cherokee leaders, among them **Attakullakulla**, **Oconostota** and **Old Tassel**, for about **20 million acres** between the Cumberland and Ohio rivers (most of Kentucky and part of Tennessee). The Cherokee received trade goods that some scholars value at about **£10,000**, "five wagons" of goods by one account (Tennessee Encyclopedia, "Transylvania Purchase"; Tennessee State Museum, "The Treaty of Sycamore Shoals"; NC DNCR, "Richard Henderson and the Transylvania Company").
- **Opposition:** **Dragging Canoe**, Attakullakulla's son, spoke against selling land the nation held in common and rejected the sale (Tennessee State Museum; search summary). The purchase broke the Royal Proclamation of 1763, which banned private purchase of Native land, and Virginia, North Carolina and the Continental Congress all refused to recognize the Transylvania colony (Tennessee Encyclopedia; NC DNCR).
- **Other claims:** the Cherokee held hunting rights in lands the Shawnee had left and also hunted in, and the Tennessee Encyclopedia calls the Cherokee claim "the strongest among competing claims" (search summary; Tennessee Encyclopedia). Boone had worked as Henderson's agent since 1764 and cut the road for the company in 1775 (NC DNCR).
- **North of the Ohio:** the land settlers entered after 1783 was Shawnee, Miami, Delaware and other nations' land (the war there → `war`, the loss → `native-nations`).

## 6. 1800 to 1850 — the trails era

### The door opens
- **The Louisiana Purchase (1803).** The United States bought about **827,000 square miles** of land west of the Mississippi from France for **$15 million**, which about **doubled the size of the country** and opened the interior West. All or part of 15 later states came from it. (Sources: Monticello; History.com; National Archives.)
- **"Manifest Destiny."** The journalist **John O'Sullivan** coined the phrase in **1845** (in the *Democratic Review*) — the idea, held by many Americans, that the United States was meant to spread across the continent to the Pacific. People used it to argue for moving west and for the war with Mexico. (Style note: it is an idea people held and acted on, not a force that acted on its own.) (Sources: American Yawp; American History Central.)

### The overland trails carried the crowds
Most trails started from towns on the Missouri River, especially **Independence and St. Joseph, Missouri**.
- **Santa Fe Trail (opened 1821).** **William Becknell** led the first trading trip from near Franklin/Independence, Missouri, to Santa Fe (then Mexican territory) in the fall of 1821. This was mainly a trade-and-freight road, not a family-settlement road. (Sources: Santa Fe Trail Association; Kansas Heritage Group; Wikipedia on Becknell.)
- **Oregon Trail.** Roughly **2,000 to 2,170 miles** from Independence, Missouri, to the Willamette Valley of Oregon; the trip took about **four to six months**. The **"Great Migration" of 1843**: about **1,000 people**, roughly **120 wagons**, and thousands of oxen and cattle set out on **May 22, 1843**, and reached Oregon that fall — proving that ordinary families in wagon trains could make the whole journey. Over the next two decades, hundreds of thousands followed. (Sources: History.com; Bureau of Land Management "Basic Facts"; Northwest Power and Conservation Council.)
- **California Trail.** Branched off the Oregon Trail and turned southwest toward California; its use exploded after the 1849 Gold Rush (below).
- **Mormon Trail (1847).** Members of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints (Mormons), fleeing persecution in the East, moved west to the Salt Lake Valley. **Brigham Young** led a first "vanguard" company — about **148 people** (accounts vary: one count is 143 men, including three enslaved Black men, plus 3 women and 2 children) — from Winter Quarters (in present-day Nebraska) about **1,300 miles** to the valley, arriving in July 1847 (Young himself arrived July 24). More than **70,000 Mormons** followed the trail by 1869; some of the poorer companies pulled handcarts instead of driving wagons. (Sources: NPS Mormon Pioneer National Historic Trail; Church of Jesus Christ history materials; History.com.)
- **Wagon-train life and its real dangers.** Families traveled in groups for safety, in covered wagons pulled by oxen. The great killer was **not** the attacks of legend but **disease** — cholera and typhoid, spread by fouled water at crowded camps — along with accidents and drownings at river crossings. Estimates are that about **1 in 10 emigrants died** on the overland trails (roughly 20,000 to 30,000 people over the era), most from disease. (Sources: NPS "Cholera: A Trail Epidemic" and "Death and Danger on the Emigrant Trails"; Oregon-California Trails Association; BLM.)

### Forced westward migration (movement angle here; injustice → Ch17)
- **Indian Removal and the Trail of Tears.** Congress passed the **Indian Removal Act in 1830**. The U.S. government forced the southeastern nations — the **Cherokee, Muscogee (Creek), Choctaw, Chickasaw, and Seminole** — from their homelands to "Indian Territory" (present-day Oklahoma). The **Cherokee removal of 1838–1839** is remembered as the **Trail of Tears**: **more than 16,000 Cherokee** were driven from Georgia, Tennessee, Alabama, and North Carolina; **over 4,000 died** of cold, hunger, and disease along the way (this is the traditional estimate; some scholars argue the toll was higher). Removal used several routes, by land and by river; the overland walk ran roughly **800 miles**, and the **Trail of Tears National Historic Trail** today marks 2,200+ miles across nine states. Tell the journey here — who, from where to where, the numbers, the winter conditions. The injustice — broken treaties, being driven off the land at gunpoint — → Ch17. (Sources: NPS Trail of Tears National Historic Trail; National Park Foundation.)
- **The domestic (internal) slave trade — a forced migration within the country.** More than **1 million enslaved people** were moved from the **Upper South** (Virginia, Maryland, the Carolinas, later Kentucky and Tennessee) to the **Deep South** cotton lands (Georgia across to Texas), roughly between **1790 and 1860** — about **200,000 people each decade from 1820 to 1860**. It was later called the **"Second Middle Passage."** They traveled two ways: marched overland on foot in chained groups called **coffles**, and shipped down the coast to **New Orleans**, the country's largest slave market. Tell the movement here — the numbers, the routes, from where to where. The system that made it possible — people held as property, families sold apart — → Ch17. (Sources: Equal Justice Initiative; PBS "The African Americans: Many Rivers to Cross"; Lumen/US History I.)
- **Crossover note:** some enslaved people were also carried west when their owners moved to new cotton land, so this forced migration and the settler migration overlapped.

### PATCH 2026-09-26 (T-244): who carried out the Cherokee removal, and the death-count range
- **Who:** in **May 1838**, U.S. Army troops under **General Winfield Scott**, with state militia including Georgia's, began rounding up Cherokee people who had not moved west. President **Martin Van Buren** sent Scott with about **7,000 soldiers** (City of Huntsville historic marker "Trail of Tears"; History.com "Trail of Tears"; Georgia Public Broadcasting "Tragedy in Georgia"). Soldiers took families from homes and farms, often with no time to pack (GPB). About **16,000** Cherokee were held in stockades in Tennessee and Alabama before removal (History.com).
- **The detachments:** three detachments went by water in **June 1838**. After that, Scott agreed with Principal Chief **John Ross** that Cherokee leaders would conduct the rest, and Ross organized **fourteen detachments** that left between **August and December 1838** (NPS, Trail of Tears NHT, "History & Culture").
- **Deaths, every figure with its source:** NPS: "more than a thousand" died on the trip west, and "an unknown number, perhaps several thousand," died from the results of the removal. Traditional estimate: about **4,000** (NPS/National Park Foundation, already in bank). Historians cited by History.com: **more than 5,000**. Demographer **Russell Thornton** ("Cherokee Population Losses during the Trail of Tears," 1984) argues total population losses of about **8,000 or more**, about twice the usual figure (Thornton paper via cooperative-individualism.org PDF, per search summary). Oklahoma Historical Society (Andrew K. Frank): "as many as one in four Cherokees" died. Correction note beside the bank's "over 4,000 died": state the range, about 1,000 on the road (NPS count) up to 8,000 or more (Thornton, counting all losses).

### PATCH 2026-09-26 (T-244): the other southeastern removals (the bank named them without numbers)
- **Choctaw, 1831-1833:** more than **14,000** removed, the first groups in 1831 in snow and river ice (Oklahoma Historical Society, "Trail of Tears," Andrew K. Frank, "hundreds died"). Other counts: about **15,000** moved with about **2,500** deaths (Wikipedia "Choctaw Trail of Tears" and Choctaw Nation materials, per search summary). State both.
- **Muscogee (Creek), 1836-1837:** after the Creek War of 1836, U.S. troops marched the remaining Creeks west in five large detachments in **August and September 1836**. Soldiers chained Creek prisoners from the war and marched them to Montgomery, with their families following in wagons (Encyclopedia of Alabama, "Creek Indian Removal"). More than **14,500** removed. Several hundred died on the way and about **3,200** died after arrival of disease, hunger and exposure (Oklahoma Historical Society). On the steamboat **Monmouth** in **October 1837**, about half of the roughly **600** Creeks aboard died in a collision (Encyclopedia of Alabama). Over 1827-1837, more than **23,000** Creeks left the Southeast (same).
- **Chickasaw:** more than **500** died of smallpox (Oklahoma Historical Society). Numbers removed not found in this pass.
- **Who ordered it:** President **Andrew Jackson** signed the Indian Removal Act in 1830 and directed the removals, and federal troops carried them out (Encyclopedia of Alabama; Oklahoma Historical Society).

### PATCH 2026-09-26 (T-244): the domestic slave trade, the traders by name
- **Franklin and Armfield**, founded **February 1828** in Alexandria, Virginia, was the largest domestic slave-trading firm in the country from 1828 to 1836. **John Armfield** (born 1797, North Carolina) bought people in Alexandria. **Isaac Franklin** (born 1789, Tennessee) sold them in Natchez, Mississippi, and from 1829 in New Orleans. **Rice Ballard** (born 1800, Virginia) became a third partner in the early 1830s and ran the Richmond office (Encyclopedia Virginia, "Franklin and Armfield").
- **How many:** "more than 8,500" people trafficked by the partnership. At its peak in the mid-1830s it moved **1,000 to 1,500** people a year (Encyclopedia Virginia).
- **The two routes:** coffles "in chains over the mountains," sent overland near the end of each summer, from several dozen to several hundred people at a time, to reach the Lower South for the fall selling season. And ships: the firm owned three brigs fitted to carry enslaved people, the **Tribune**, the **Uncas** and the **Isaac Franklin**, on a voyage of about **three weeks** from Virginia to Louisiana, at the peak one shipload **every two weeks** (Encyclopedia Virginia; Wikipedia "Franklin and Armfield Office," secondary). The last shipment left in **November 1836**. The firm was formally dissolved in 1841 (Encyclopedia Virginia).
- **How much of the movement was sale, and how much was owners moving with the people they held:** historians disagree. **Fogel and Engerman:** the trade accounted for about **16 percent** of the relocation. **Michael Tadman:** traders carried **60 to 70 percent** of interstate moves after 1808 (per search summary of the historiography). **Walter Johnson:** about two-thirds by the trade, one-third by owners moving. **Jonathan Pritchett:** about **50 percent**, about **835,000** people moved between 1790 and 1850 (the same article also gives 835,000 for 1790-1860) (all from Wikipedia "Slave trade in the United States / Interregional slave trade," secondary, which cites each). State the range, 16 to 70 percent, and whose estimate each is. Total moved: "upwards of one million" (same), matching the bank.

### The Gold Rush crowd
- **The Gold Rush (1849).** Gold was found at Sutter's Mill in early 1848; the rush came in 1849. About **80,000 "forty-niners"** arrived in 1849, and about **300,000 people** came to California from 1848 to 1855 — roughly half by sea and half overland on the California Trail. California's non-Native population grew from about **14,000 (1848) to about 250,000 (1852)**; San Francisco grew from roughly **200 people (1846) to about 36,000 (1852)**. The migration and settlement are the story here; the mining and the money → Ch9. (Sources: California Gold Rush references, cross-checked; Smithsonian; ushistory.org.)
- **New land from the war with Mexico.** The Mexican-American War (1846–1848) ended with the **Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo (1848)**, in which Mexico ceded the **Mexican Cession** — California, Nevada, Utah, most of Arizona and New Mexico, and parts of Colorado and Wyoming. (Texas had been annexed in 1845; the Oregon boundary with Britain was settled in 1846.) This opened the far West to migration. (Sources: American Yawp; Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo references.)

## 7. 1850 to 1900 — free land, rails, and the last open country

### Free land: the Homestead Act
- **The Homestead Act (1862).** Any adult who headed a household — including women, and, after the Civil War, freed people and immigrants who intended to become citizens — could claim up to **160 acres** of public land, and own it after living on it and farming it for **five years**. Over the law's life, roughly **4 million claims** were filed and about **270 million acres** — about **10 percent of all U.S. land** — passed into private hands. It drew settlers onto the Great Plains. (Sources: NPS Homestead National Historical Park / "The Homestead Act"; Britannica; American Battlefield Trust.)

### The railroad carried the crowds
- **The first transcontinental railroad was completed at Promontory Summit, Utah, on May 10, 1869** (the "golden spike"). It cut a coast-to-coast trip from four months or more by wagon to about **one week**, and the railroads — which were given large land grants to sell — pulled settlers and immigrants west by the trainload. The movement of people is the story here; the railroad as a machine, and the Chinese workers who built the western leg, → Ch7. (Sources: Library of Congress; History.com; Gilder Lehrman.)

### Cattle drives and cowboys
- After the Civil War, Texas cowboys drove **longhorn cattle** north to Kansas railheads such as **Abilene** along trails like the **Chisholm Trail** (used heavily after the railroad reached Abilene in 1867). An estimated **5 million or more cattle** were driven north from Texas between 1866 and 1884 (some estimates put about 6 million over the whole era); in 1871 alone about 700,000 reached the Kansas railheads. The long drives faded after the mid-1880s — barbed wire fenced the range, railroads reached deeper into Texas, an 1885–86 Kansas quarantine law shut out Texas cattle, and the brutal winter of 1886–87 killed much of the herd. (Sources: Texas State Historical Association; Oklahoma Historical Society; HistoryNet.)

### Black migration west: the Exodusters
- **The Exodusters (about 1879).** Tens of thousands of Black Southerners left the South for **Kansas** and the Plains, seeking their own land and safety as Reconstruction collapsed. As many as **40,000** moved to Kansas, Oklahoma, and Colorado around 1879–1881; about **6,000 reached Kansas in the spring of 1879** alone. **Benjamin "Pap" Singleton**, called the "Father of the Exodus," helped steer thousands there. Tell the movement here — from where (Mississippi, Louisiana, Texas, Tennessee), to where, and how many. The Jim Crow terror and broken promises that drove them → Ch17. (Sources: Smithsonian National Museum of African American History and Culture; History.com; Wikipedia "Benjamin 'Pap' Singleton.")

### The Oklahoma Land Rush
- **The Land Rush of April 22, 1889.** The government opened about **1.9 million acres** of former Indian Territory to settlers. About **50,000 people** lined up and raced in at noon; nearly **2 million acres** were claimed in a single day. **Guthrie** and **Oklahoma City** went from empty ground to towns of about 10,000–15,000 people that same day. People who sneaked in early to grab land were the **"Sooners."** (Sources: History.com; U.S. Census Bureau history; National Cowboy & Western Heritage Museum.) The land had been taken from Native nations — the injustice → Ch17.

### Native peoples forced onto reservations (movement angle; injustice → Ch17)
- As settlers, railroads, and cattle filled the Plains, the U.S. confined Native nations to **reservations**, often far from their homelands. One documented example of the forced journey is the **Long Walk of the Navajo (Diné), 1863–1864**: about **8,500 people** were marched **more than 300 miles** from their homeland in present-day Arizona and New Mexico to the **Bosque Redondo** reservation at Fort Sumner, New Mexico. About **200 died on the march** in winter, and thousands more died at Bosque Redondo before the Navajo were allowed to return home in 1868; in all, more than 10,000 Navajo were removed between 1863 and 1866. Many nations were confined this way through the 1870s and 1880s. Tell the journey here; the injustice of removal and confinement → Ch17. (Sources: Smithsonian NMAI "The Long Walk"; Wikipedia "Long Walk of the Navajo"; National Library of Medicine "Native Voices.")

### The "closing of the frontier"

## 8. 1900 to 1950 — two great internal migrations

### The Great Migration (Black Americans out of the South)
- Between about **1910 and 1970**, roughly **6 million Black Americans** moved from the rural South to cities in the North, the Midwest, and the West — one of the largest internal migrations in the nation's history. The first wave (about **1910–1940**) headed mostly to Northern and Midwestern cities. Most traveled by **train**, riding the rail lines north (later also by car and bus).
- The numbers show up in the cities: between **1910 and 1920** the Black population of **Chicago grew about 148 percent, Detroit about 611 percent, Philadelphia about 500 percent**, and **New York City** roughly doubled (about 152,000 to 328,000). In 1900 about **90 percent** of Black Americans lived in the South; by 1970 fewer than **55 percent** did. (Sources: U.S. Census Bureau "The Great Migration, 1910 to 1970"; Britannica; History.com; Smithsonian.)
- Tell the movement here — from where (the rural South), to where (Chicago, Detroit, New York, Philadelphia, Pittsburgh, and later Los Angeles, Oakland, Seattle), how many, and how they traveled. The Jim Crow laws, lynching, and sharecropping debt that pushed them out, and the discrimination that met them in the North, → Ch17. Where they settled inside the cities → Ch4.

### The Dust Bowl migration
- In the 1930s, drought and windstorms tore the topsoil off farms across the **southern Great Plains** (parts of Oklahoma, Texas, Kansas, Colorado, and New Mexico) — the **Dust Bowl**. From about **1935 to 1940, more than 250,000 people** left the Dust Bowl states for California; net out-migration from Oklahoma alone may have reached about **440,000**. Many traveled west on **U.S. Route 66**. They were called **"Okies,"** and they often met hostility — in 1936 the Los Angeles police even set up a "bum blockade" at the state line to turn migrants away. (Sources: Oklahoma Historical Society "Okie Migrations"; Smithsonian/American Experience; Route 66 references.) The road itself, as a machine/route, → Ch7.

### PATCH 2026-09-26 (T-244): the "bum blockade," who ordered it
- **Los Angeles Police Chief James Edgar "Two-Gun" Davis** ordered it. ## 9. 1950 to 2000 — the Second Great Migration and the Sun Belt

### The Great Migration continues, and turns west
- The later wave of the Great Migration (**about 1940–1970**) was larger than the first and shifted more toward the **West** — Los Angeles, Oakland, Seattle, Portland — pulled by wartime and postwar factory jobs. It is part of the same ~6 million total. (Sources: U.S. Census Bureau; Britannica.)

### The Sun Belt shift
- After World War II, Americans moved in large numbers from the older industrial North (later nicknamed the **"Rust Belt"**) to the South and West (the **"Sun Belt"**) for jobs, warmer weather, and cheaper land. **Air conditioning** made hot climates comfortable year-round and helped drive the boom. Between **1970 and 1990 the South grew about 36 percent and the West about 51 percent**, both well above the national average. (Sources: Britannica "Sun Belt"; University of Central Florida materials; state-growth analyses.)
- **California became the most populous state in 1962**, passing New York; it had about **17 million** people then, roughly triple its prewar size. (Sources: California State Capitol Museum; population-history references.)

### What made moving easier
- **The Interstate Highway System**, begun under the **Federal-Aid Highway Act of 1956**, made long-distance moving and suburban living far easier and sped both the Sun Belt shift and the growth of suburbs. The highway as a machine → Ch7; the suburbs themselves → Ch4. (Sources: Federal Highway Administration; Bill of Rights Institute.)

### Featured person for this section (verified; full facts below)
- **Robert Joseph Pershing Foster** — Great Migration; Louisiana to California, 1953 (Wilkerson) — a doctor who drove west, fitting the later westward wave.

## 10. 2000 to Today — the Sun Belt pull sharpens

- **The move to the Sun Belt continued and grew.** From **2020 to 2024** the South gained about **2.7 million** net domestic migrants (people moving in from other states, minus those leaving). **Texas and Florida** led the country: between **2023 and 2024** each gained over **half a million** residents from other states. (Sources: U.S. Census Bureau; Pew; StorageCafe analysis of Census data.)
- **People moved out of high-cost coastal states and the Rust Belt.** From **April 2020 to July 2022**, California lost about **343,000** and New York about **300,000** residents to other states (net domestic migration). The metros with the largest net out-migration included New York, Los Angeles, San Francisco, and Chicago. (Sources: U.S. Census Bureau; AOL/Bankrate summaries of Census data.)
- **Remote work** (especially after 2020) let some people choose where to live by cost and space rather than by where a job sat, adding to the flow toward the South and the interior West. (Sources: Pew Research Center; U.S. Census Bureau.)

### Era 2 (1500s) — Oñate up the Camino Real, 1598. VERIFIED.
- **Juan de Oñate** left **Santa Bárbara**, in northern New Spain, in **January 1598** leading a colony of about **400 men, about 130 of them with families** — soldiers, Franciscan friars, farmers, laborers, servants, and enslaved people — with **83 wagons and carts** and **more than 7,000 head of livestock**. Instead of following the Conchos River as earlier expeditions had, the column cut straight across the Chihuahuan Desert, reached the **Rio Grande at El Paso del Norte** (near present-day El Paso / Ciudad Juárez) in **late April 1598**, and on **April 30, 1598** Oñate formally claimed New Mexico for Spain (the ceremony called **La Toma**, "the taking"). In **July 1598** he set the colony's headquarters at **San Juan Pueblo (Ohkay Owingeh)** on the upper Rio Grande, extending the road by **more than 600 miles**. The route became **El Camino Real de Tierra Adentro** — the "royal road of the interior," running from Mexico City to northern New Mexico; its U.S. stretch is a National Historic Trail today. This is the first documented migration of a wagon-borne settler colony within the future United States — a **migration**, not a scouting trip: families, carts, and herds moving to stay. (Sources: NPS, "History and Significance of El Camino Real de Tierra Adentro"; Texas State Historical Association, Handbook of Texas, "Oñate, Juan de" and "The Conquest and Colonization of New Mexico by Don Juan de Oñate.")
- **Hard part (belongs to `native-nations`, parked there):** the land the colony moved into was Pueblo homeland. In 1599 Oñate's soldiers attacked **Acoma Pueblo** after Acoma people killed Spanish soldiers; the killings and punishments that followed are the nations' story — this chapter carries one honest sentence and hands off.

### Era 3 (1600s) — Thomas Hooker's walk to Hartford, 1636. VERIFIED.
- **Thomas Hooker**, pastor of the church at **Newtown** (now Cambridge), Massachusetts Bay, led **about 100 members of his congregation** overland in **May–June 1636** to the Connecticut River, where they settled **Hartford**. The journey took **about two weeks**; the party **drove 160 cattle** and lived partly on the cows' milk along the way. Governor **John Winthrop's journal (May 31, 1636)** records the departure — "Mr. Hooker, pastor of the church of Newtown, and most of his congregation, went to Connecticut," his wife carried on a horse litter. They followed Native trails (the route later remembered as the **Old Connecticut Path**). In 1639 Hartford and its neighbor towns adopted the **Fundamental Orders of Connecticut** (the document and government → `government-politics`; the move itself is this chapter's). (Sources: ConnecticutHistory.org, "Thomas Hooker: Connecticut's Founding Father"; Winthrop's journal, May 31, 1636; Society of Colonial Wars in the State of Connecticut, "1636 Hartford.")

### Era 5 (1750–1800) — the Acadian expulsion and the road to Louisiana. VERIFIED.
- **Placement correction:** the expulsion began in **1755**, so under the boundary rule this material sits in **1750–1800**, not 1700–1750 (where the seed outline had parked the target).
- Between **1755 and 1764** British forces deported **about 11,500 Acadians** — French-speaking Catholic farm families — from Nova Scotia and nearby areas (*Le Grand Dérangement*, "the great upheaval"), out of an Acadian population of about **14,000–18,000**. They were shipped to the thirteen colonies, England, and France. **Death counts are disputed:** one accounting says close to **5,000** died of disease, shipwreck, or violence; some sources say **as many as half** of the deported died. State the range. (Sources: TheCollector, "The Acadian Expulsion of 1755"; 64 Parishes [Louisiana Endowment for the Humanities], "Cajuns.")
- Between **1764 and 1785** about **2,600–3,000 Acadians** made their way to Louisiana (then under Spain). In **1785** seven Spanish-funded ships carried **1,596 Acadians** from Nantes, France, to Louisiana; the first arrived at New Orleans **July 29, 1785**. They settled along the bayous and prairies west of New Orleans; their descendants became the **Cajuns** — the word comes from "Acadian." (Sources: 64 Parishes "Cajuns"; Acadian-Cajun genealogy ship lists [heritage-society source] for the seven ships; KNOE News [journalism] for the July 29, 1785 arrival.)
- **Joseph Broussard, called Beausoleil (about 1702–1765). VERIFIED.**
  > **Who:** an Acadian resistance leader who led one of the first Acadian groups to Louisiana.
  > **When and where:** resisted the expulsion in Acadia (Nova Scotia / New Brunswick) 1755–1762; led about 200 Acadians to Louisiana, arriving February 1765.
  > **Movie:** none found. Leave the Movie line out.
- Broussard organized armed resistance to the deportation and was captured and held at **Halifax** until the **Treaty of Paris (1763)**. In **late 1764** he and about 600 Acadians chartered a ship to Saint-Domingue (present-day Haiti); in **February 1765** he and about **193–200 Acadians** sailed on to Louisiana, arriving at New Orleans in late February. On **April 8, 1765** colonial officials named him militia captain and commander of the **"Acadians of the Attakapas"** district (around present-day St. Martinville). He died in **October 1765** in an epidemic that struck the new settlement (commonly given as yellow fever). Parks Canada designated him a National Historic Person. (Sources: Dictionary of Canadian Biography, "BROSSARD, dit Beausoleil, Joseph"; Parks Canada designation page; Wikipedia "Joseph Broussard" cross-checked.)

- **Charles Ball (born about 1780, Calvert County, Maryland). VERIFIED (from his narrative).**
  > **Who:** an enslaved man marched south in a chained coffle, who later escaped, walked back north, and dictated his life story.
  > **When and where:** sold from Maryland to a Georgia trader about 1805; marched about five weeks to Columbia, South Carolina; narrative published Philadelphia, 1837.

- About 1805 Ball was sold away from his wife and children in Maryland to a Georgia slave trader. He was chained into a **coffle of 52 people**: a strong iron collar padlocked around each neck, each padlock closed on a link of a **chain about 100 feet long**, the men also handcuffed in pairs. The coffle **walked more than a month** from Maryland to **Columbia, South Carolina**, and Ball was sold on into Georgia. ### Era 7 (1850–1900) — Knight's placement, the handcart companies, and the Ingalls family
- **Amelia Stewart Knight moves to this era.** Director ruling 3 (2026-07-23): her 1853 journey sits in 1850–1900 under the boundary rule. The [VERIFY] in the seed outline is cleared; her verified facts stand in the featured-person section above.
- **The Willie and Martin handcart companies, 1856. VERIFIED.** In August 1856 **more than 1,000** Latter-day Saint emigrants in two late-starting companies — the **Willie company** (about 500 people, left Florence, Nebraska Territory, **August 17**) and the **Martin company** (left **August 27**) — set out to pull two-wheeled handcarts to Salt Lake City. An early blizzard struck **October 19** in present-day Wyoming; the Martin company had just waded the North Platte River near present-day Casper. Rescue wagons sent from Salt Lake (more than **300 rescuers**) brought the survivors in through November. **Death counts differ and must be stated as a range:** modern counts run about **67–77 dead in the Willie company** and about **103–150 in the Martin company**; older accounts say about **200–250** died across the stranded companies and the wagon trains traveling with them. (Sources: WyoHistory.org, "Journey to Martin's Cove"; BYU Studies, "Weather, Disaster, and Responsibility"; Wikipedia "Willie and Martin handcart companies" cross-checked.)
- **Levi Savage Jr. (born 1820, Ohio). VERIFIED.**
  > **Who:** a sub-captain of the Willie handcart company whose journal records the disaster — and his warning against it.
  > **When and where:** Florence, Nebraska Territory, to Salt Lake City, August–November 1856.
  > **Movie:** *17 Miracles* (2011, dir. T. C. Christensen) — a feature film about the Willie company, built around Savage's journal.
- At a camp meeting at Florence on **August 13, 1856**, Savage, then 36, urged the roughly 500 emigrants to wait until spring, warning of snow and deaths if they started that late with over 1,000 miles to go. He was overruled and reprimanded for speaking so freely — and then stayed with the company anyway, working through the blizzards to keep people alive. His journal survives and anchors most modern tellings. (Sources: WyoHistory.org; BYU Studies; Savage journal excerpts via church history materials; film verified via IMDb / Deseret News, June 2, 2011.)
- **The Ingalls family — a real migrating family, 1867–1894. VERIFIED (route), with the books' fictions flagged.**
  > **Who:** Laura Ingalls Wilder, author of the Little House books, whose childhood was one long series of covered-wagon moves.
  > **When and where:** born February 7, 1867, near Pepin, Wisconsin; family moves 1869–1879; her own last move 1894.
  > **Movie:** *Little House on the Prairie* (NBC series, 1974–1983) — nine seasons, 205 episodes, Michael Landon as Charles Ingalls; loosely based on the books.
- **The real route:** Pepin, Wisconsin (1867–1869) → **Montgomery County, Kansas**, near Independence (1869–1871), where the cabin stood on the **Osage Diminished Reserve — land that still belonged to the Osage Nation**; the family left in 1871 → back to Pepin (1871–1874) → **Walnut Grove, Minnesota** (1874–1876), where grasshopper plagues destroyed the wheat crops in 1875–76 → **Burr Oak, Iowa** (1876–1877), helping run a hotel → Walnut Grove again (1877–1879) → **De Smet, Dakota Territory** (1879), where Charles Ingalls took a railroad-camp job and then a homestead claim. Laura married **Almanzo Wilder** in 1885; in **1894**, in a drought year, Laura, Almanzo, and their daughter Rose drove a covered hack about **650 miles** from De Smet to **Mansfield, Missouri**, where she later wrote the books. Her 1894 trip diary was published in 1962 as *On the Way Home*. (Sources: Kansas Historical Society [Kansapedia], "Laura Ingalls Wilder"; Minnesota Historical Society [MNopedia]; littlehouseontheprairie.com historical timeline; *On the Way Home*, Harper, 1962.)
- **What the books fictionalize — say so honestly:** the novels (published 1932–1943) rearrange and simplify the real moves. *Little House on the Prairie* makes Laura several years older than she was in Kansas (she was 2–4); the Burr Oak years are left out entirely; and the family was living on Osage land it had no right to. The TV series is looser still. (Sources: Kansas Historical Society; littlehouseontheprairie.com timeline; Britannica on the TV series.)
- **TV series verified:** *Little House on the Prairie*, NBC — pilot March 30, 1974; series September 11, 1974 to March 21, 1983; nine seasons, 205 episodes; Michael Landon starred, wrote, directed, and produced. (Sources: Britannica; Wikipedia "Little House on the Prairie (1974 TV series)" cross-checked.)

### Era 10 (2000–today) additions. VERIFIED.
- **Hurricane Katrina, 2005 — the biggest sudden displacement of the era.** The storm and the levee failures displaced **more than 1 million people** from the New Orleans metro area and the Gulf Coast; **up to 600,000 households** were still displaced a month later. New Orleans fell from **484,674 people (2000 census)** to an estimated **230,172 (July 2006)** — a loss of more than half the city — and stood at about **363,000 in 2024**, still roughly 100,000 below pre-storm. The storm and rescue → `disasters`; this chapter tells where the people went and that many never came back. (Sources: The Data Center [New Orleans], "Facts for Features: Katrina Impact" and 2000-census analysis; HUD; The Center Square, 2025, on 2024 population.)
- **Puerto Rico after Hurricane María (September 2017) — the territories thread.** About **130,000 people — almost 4 percent of the island — left for the mainland** between July 2017 and July 2018 (Census Bureau estimate); estimates from air-travel, phone, and social-media data run higher (roughly 160,000 to 470,000 — the count depends on the data used; state that). About **a third of 2018's movers went to Florida**. Puerto Ricans are U.S. citizens; this is internal migration, not immigration. (Sources: U.S. Census Bureau, "A Third of Movers From Puerto Rico to the Mainland United States Relocated to Florida in 2018"; PNAS 2020, "Quantifying the dynamics of migration after Hurricane Maria"; CBS News on the 130,000 figure.)
- **The New Great Migration continues:** metro **Atlanta's** Black population grew about **five-fold from 1970 to 2020** and passed Chicago to rank second nationally by 2010; Atlanta was the top destination for young Black adult movers (ages 16–26) from 2000 to 2018, and seven of their top ten destinations were in the South. (Source: Brookings, Frey.)
