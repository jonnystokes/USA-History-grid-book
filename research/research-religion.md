## ERA 1 — Before 1500

### 1a. What this era can honestly say, and why there is no story block

There is no `hb-story` block in this era and there should not be one. `hb-story` is for named,
documented individuals (`control/hard-subjects-policy.md` §4). The written record for North
America begins with Europeans in the 1500s, so almost no individual person on this continent
before 1500 is named in a surviving record. The cell is `state="full"` for **content** — religious
life before 1500 is a large, well-evidenced subject — and carries no named person.

**How anything is known about it, and the cell should say so:** from what people built and left in
the ground (buildings, mounds, graves, objects, the direction a doorway faces), from the oral
traditions the nations themselves kept and still keep, and — only for the very end of this era —
from what Europeans wrote down after 1500, which is an outsider's record and often a hostile one.
Beliefs do not fossilise. A wall lined up with the midwinter sunrise proves the alignment; it does
not by itself tell a reader what the people who built it believed. Say that.

**Do not call any of this "myth" or "prehistory."** These are the religions of nations that still
exist. Nations descended from the places below hold ceremonies at them now, and decide for
themselves what to make public.

### 1b. Chaco Canyon — buildings lined up with the sun and moon (mid-800s onward)

- "The cultural flowering of the Chacoan people began in the mid 800s and lasted more than 300
  years." Pueblo Bonito, Una Vida and Penasco Blanco were built in the middle and late 800s.
- "Dozens of great houses in Chaco Canyon were connected by roads to more than 150 great houses
  throughout the region."
- The buildings "were often oriented to solar, lunar, and cardinal directions," and the canyon
  holds "sophisticated astronomical markers."
- **The nations' own statement, quoted because it is theirs:** "Pueblo descendants say that Chaco
  was a special gathering place where many peoples and clans converged to share their ceremonies,
  traditions, and knowledge." Many Southwest Native people "look upon Chaco as an important stop
  along their clans' sacred migration paths."
- (Source: National Park Service, Chaco Culture National Historical Park, "History & Culture,"
  https://www.nps.gov/chcu/learn/historyculture/index.htm .)

### 1c. Cahokia — the largest town north of Mexico, and a post circle aimed at the sunrise

- Cahokia is "the largest pre-Columbian settlement north of Mexico," about 13 km north-east of
  St Louis, occupied mainly in the Mississippian period (**800-1400**); at its extent it covered
  nearly 1,600 hectares and included **some 120 mounds**, of which **70** survive inside the
  protected site. (Source: UNESCO World Heritage Centre, "Cahokia Mounds State Historic Site,"
  https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/198/ .)
- The National Park Service page for the same World Heritage site says Cahokia "flourished between
  A.D. 1000 and 1350," calls it "a cultural, religious, and economic center," gives population
  estimates of **10,000-20,000 for Cahokia proper** and **40,000-50,000 for "Greater Cahokia,"** and
  says the mounds "served variously as construction foundations for public buildings and as burial
  mounds." (Source: NPS, "Cahokia Mounds State Historic Site: World Heritage Site,"
  https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/cahokia-mounds-state-historic-site-world-heritage-site.htm .)
- **Monks Mound:** built in **fourteen stages**, rising in **four terraces to 30 metres**. UNESCO
  gives its footprint as "over 5 hectares"; NPS gives "six hectares." **State it as about six
  hectares or give both — do not silently pick one.**
- **Woodhenge:** a circle of upright wooden posts used to mark the sunrise at the solstices and the
  equinoxes. The circle was rebuilt about five times and ranged from **73 to 146 metres across**;
  the earliest had **24 posts**, and each rebuilding added twelve more. The post marking the equinox
  sunrise lines up with the front of Monks Mound. (Source: Cahokia Mounds State Historic Site,
  "Q & A: Measuring Cahokia," by site archaeologist Bill Iseminger,
  https://cahokiamounds.org/measuringcahokia/ ; the NPS World Heritage page, above, independently
  describes Woodhenge as "a circle of wooden posts that aligned with the movements of the sun
  throughout the year." The rebuild count and post numbers are the site's own staff archaeologist's
  — attribute them to him rather than stating them as settled.)
- **Do not write that anyone knows what was said or believed at Woodhenge.** What is documented is
  the alignment and the rebuilding.

### 1d. The Hopewell earthworks — geometry built for gatherings (1-400 CE)

- The Hopewell culture "is characterized by the construction of enclosures made of earthen walls
  and ditches, often built in geometric patterns and mounds of various shapes," and it "flourished
  in the woodlands of eastern North America **from 1 to 400 CE**."
- NPS calls it "a dynamic social and ceremonial phenomenon" and describes a network of contacts
  reaching "from the Atlantic coast to the Rocky Mountains," which brought in mica, shark's teeth,
  obsidian, copper and marine shells.
- (Source: NPS, Hopewell Culture National Historical Park, "History & Culture,"
  https://www.nps.gov/hocu/learn/historyculture/index.htm .)
- **Note for the writer:** NPS says "different Native American groups," not one nation. Do not
  attach a single modern nation to the earthworks, and do not use the old phrase "Mound Builders"
  as though it named a people.
- **Where the word "Hopewell" comes from — put this in the cell, because the name misleads.**
  "Hopewell" is not the name of any American Indian tribe. Early archaeologists named the site for
  its owner, **Mordecai C. Hopewell**, whose farm near Chillicothe, Ohio, held the mounds. The name
  entered archaeology after **Warren K. Moorehead** excavated **twenty-eight mounds** on that farm
  and the finds were shown at Chicago's Columbian Exposition in **1893**. (Sources: NPS, "Hopewell
  Mound Group," https://www.nps.gov/hocu/learn/historyculture/hopewell-mound-group.htm ; NPS,
  "Hopewell Culture NHP: Administrative History," chapter 1,
  https://www.nps.gov/parkhistory/online_books/hocu/adhi/adhi1c.htm .)
- UNESCO inscribed the sites in 2023 under the name **Hopewell Ceremonial Earthworks**
  (https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1689/ ), which is the plainest available support for calling the
  enclosures ceremonial rather than defensive or residential.

### 1e. The honest limits — these belong in the cell

- Hundreds of separate nations, each with its own religious life. There is no single "Native
  religion," and writing one is the error to avoid.
- No shared book, no single founder, no one weekly service across all of them. Ceremony was tied to
  a particular place, to the season, and to the community.
- What survives from before 1500 is objects and buildings, plus the oral traditions the nations
  kept. Written description begins with Europeans after 1500, from outside and usually hostile.

### 1f. Not this chapter's

`native-nations` leads on the nations themselves. This chapter takes only the religious life.
`landmarks` carries the mound sites as places. Do not retell either.

## ERA 2 — The 1500s (`state="thin"`)

The era is thin outside the missions, and the mission record is one-sided: nearly all of it was
written by the missionaries. Say so in the cell.

### 2a. The Florida and Georgia missions — dates and structure

- **Missionary work in Florida began in 1568.** The Jesuits went first and "were not successful,"
  and "they left the Florida mission field in **1571**." **The first Franciscans arrived in St
  Augustine in 1573.** (Source: Florida Museum of Natural History, "The Church and the Missions,"
  *St. Augustine: America's Ancient City*,
  https://www.floridamuseum.ufl.edu/staugustine/timeline/the-church-and-the-missions/ .)
- "After a brief effort among the coastal Guale in **1574-75**, the Franciscan mission era shifted
  into full gear after the **1587** arrival of a group of friars from Spain." **San Pedro de Mocama**
  was "the first successful mission established in Georgia," and "by the end of **1595** missions
  had also been established in at least one other Mocama town and no fewer than **five** main towns
  of the Muskogee-speaking Guale chiefdom." (Source: New Georgia Encyclopedia, "Spanish Missions,"
  https://www.georgiaencyclopedia.org/articles/arts-culture/spanish-missions/ .)
- **A mission town with a resident friar was a *doctrina*.** The first Franciscan *doctrina* was set
  up at Nombre de Dios "after 1587." (Source: Florida Museum, "The Nombre de Dios Mission Sites,"
  https://www.floridamuseum.ufl.edu/histarch/research/st-augustine/menendez/nombre-de-dios/ .)
- **The mission was also a labor draft — do not leave this out.** New Georgia Encyclopedia: the
  missions were "the primary means of integrating Indians into the political and economic
  structure," through the ***repartimiento*** system, which required "a specified number of
  unmarried male Indians" to work each year in St Augustine. In plain words for the cell: every
  year a set number of unmarried Native men from mission towns had to go and work for the Spanish
  at St Augustine.
- **Source conflict on the friar at Nombre de Dios — state it or avoid the name.** The Florida
  Museum's "Church and the Missions" page names **Father Alonso de Escobedo**, arriving 1586, who
  "is thought to have baptized more than a hundred Indians"; its own "Nombre de Dios Mission Sites"
  page names **Franciscan friar Antonio de Escobedo** as the friar assigned there who helped build
  the first mission church. Two pages of the same museum give different first names. **Do not print
  a first name for him without settling this.**

### 2b. Luis Cáncer de Barbastro — the friar who argued the mission should come without soldiers

- A Dominican who "first came to the New World from Spain in **1518**," working among Native people
  in Puerto Rico and Hispaniola, and who "continued practicing a non-violent type of evangelical
  work" in Guatemala. He carried a royal decree from **King Charles V** for his Florida attempt.
- He "sailed from Havana to southwest Florida in early June" 1549, and on **26 June 1549** he "went
  ashore to greet a group of Indians and was soon killed" — killed by Tocobaga people near Tampa
  Bay.
- (Source: Florida Historical Society, "Luis Cancer de Barbastro Killed near Tampa Bay,"
  https://myfloridahistory.org/date-in-history/june-26-1549/luis-cancer-de-barbastro-killed-near-tampa-bay .)
- **A caution.** Fuller accounts circulating online add the caravel's name, a party of four or five
  Dominicans, a Native interpreter called Magdalena who left them at the landing, and the manner of
  the killing (clubbed on the beach). Those details come from secondary retellings this pass could
  not run down to a primary or institutional source. **Write only what the Florida Historical
  Society states, and do not describe the method of his death, because the sources reached here do
  not establish it.**

### 2c. New Mexico, 1598 — the mission field begins

- "In early May 1598, Juan de Oñate formally took possession of the province of New Mexico for
  Spain at the ford of the Rio Grande at El Paso del Norte." His column included Franciscan friars.
- "In fall 1598, Oñate visited all the pueblos and through translators required their allegiance to
  the Spanish government, and in those meetings, Oñate assigned missionaries to each pueblo, thus
  initiating the Franciscan mission field of New Mexico."
- (Sources: NPS, "New Mexico: Salinas Pueblo Missions National Monument,"
  https://www.nps.gov/articles/salinas.htm ; NPS, "San Gabriel del Yunque-Ouinge and San Miguel,"
  https://www.nps.gov/subjects/travelspanishmissions/san-gabriel-del-yunque-ouinge-and-san-miguel.htm ;
  NPS, "San Jose de los Jemez Mission and Giusewa Pueblo Site,"
  https://home.nps.gov/subjects/travelspanishmissions/san-jose-de-los-jemez-mission-and-giusewa-pueblo-site.htm .)
- **NPS pages disagree on how many friars came with Oñate** — one account gives ten Franciscan
  friars, eight secular priests and two lay brothers; another refers to "the five Franciscans from
  Oñate's expedition." **Do not print a count.** Write that Franciscans travelled with him.
- **Do not backdate the suppression of Pueblo ceremony into the 1500s.** The documented campaign
  against kivas, masks and dances, and the Pueblo response, belong to the 1600s cell (§3f).

### 2d. THE 1597 GUALE UPRISING — and why this chapter refuses to name a leader

- What is established: in the autumn of 1597, Guale people killed **five Franciscan friars** and
  destroyed their missions; "northern missions were abandoned completely until **1604**." (Source:
  New Georgia Encyclopedia, "Spanish Missions," as above.)
- What is **not** established: who did the killing, and why. J. Michael Francis and Kathleen M.
  Kole, *Murder and Martyrdom in Spanish Florida: Don Juan and the Guale Uprising of 1597*,
  Anthropological Papers of the American Museum of Natural History no. 95 (American Museum of
  Natural History, **2011**), translate the earliest surviving documents and state that the work
  "seeks to move beyond the two central questions that have dominated the historiography of the
  uprising, namely who killed the five friars and why, **neither of which can be answered with any
  certainty**." They also record that most earlier versions of the story rest on "a young Indian
  from Tolomato named Juanillo, the heir to Guale's paramount chieftaincy," whom "Tolomato's
  resident friar publicly reprimanded ... for practicing polygamy." (Source:
  https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/books/65/ ; full text at
  https://digitallibrary.amnh.org/handle/2246/6123 .)
- **Ruling for this chapter:** the killings go in the cell as fact. The name Juanillo, the polygamy
  rebuke, and the phrase "Juanillo's Revolt" stay out, with the uncertainty stated in the prose. No
  `hb-story` block is built on a person the leading study says cannot be identified.

### 2f. Doña María Meléndez — the filled story

- "Doña Maria Melendez, a member of the Timucuan noble class ... was the Chieftainess (*cacica*) of
  the Timucuan town of Nombre de Dios during the late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries."
- "Doña Maria was a Christian, and her mother (who had been the ruling Chief of Nombre de Dios
  before her) was one of the very early Timucua converts to Christianity."
- "Doña Maria married a Spanish soldier named Clemente de Vernal, and he lived with her and their
  children at Nombre de Dios."
- "By 1606 she had become the ruler of the Timucuan tribes extending along the coast between St.
  Augustine and approximately Cumberland Island, Georgia, **possibly through Spanish
  intervention**." (The hedge is the source's own; keep it and say the record does not settle it.)
- "The generation of Timucuans born during the 1560's reached adulthood in the 1580's, which was
  when missionary efforts in St. Augustine realized their first successes."
- (Sources: NPS, Castillo de San Marcos National Monument, "Women,"
  https://www.nps.gov/casa/learn/historyculture/women.htm ; NPS, Fort Matanzas National Monument,
  "Women," https://www.nps.gov/foma/learn/historyculture/women.htm — the two NPS pages carry the
  same text.)
- **Her part in the mission's success:** "Their success was largely owing to Doña María Meléndez,
  the ruling chief of the town of Nombre de Dios." (Source: Florida Museum of Natural History, "The
  Church and the Missions," https://www.floridamuseum.ufl.edu/staugustine/timeline/the-church-and-the-missions/ .)
- **Spelling:** NPS prints "Doña Maria Melendez"; the Florida Museum prints "Doña María Meléndez."
  Same person. Use the accented form and do not treat the difference as two people.
- **A claim NOT used:** a search summary attributed to her the supply of corn that saved St
  Augustine from starvation in 1587. **Neither NPS page nor either Florida Museum page reached in
  this pass contains it.** It is left out of the outline.
- **The honest limit, and it belongs in the story:** everything known about her was written by
  Spaniards. Nothing in her own words survives, and no source reached here records why she became
  Christian. Do not supply a motive.

## ERA 3 — The 1600s (`state="full"`)

### 3a. Pilgrims and Puritans — get the difference right

- **The Puritans were English Protestants who wanted to purify the Church of England** of what they
  saw as leftovers of Roman Catholicism. In the 1620s English church and state leaders "insisted
  that the Puritans conform to religious practices that they abhorred, removing their ministers
  from office and threatening them with 'extirpation from the earth' if they did not fall in line."
- **What was done to Puritan laymen in England, stated as the source states it:** "in 1630 a man
  was sentenced to life imprisonment, had his property confiscated, his nose slit, an ear cut off,
  and his forehead branded 'S.S.' (sower of sedition)." Use this. It is what the reader needs in
  order to understand why 20,000 people crossed an ocean, and it is what the same colonists later
  did to Quakers.
- **"Beginning in 1630 as many as 20,000 Puritans emigrated to America from England to gain the
  liberty to worship God as they chose."**
- **The Pilgrims are not the Puritans.** LOC: "Unlike the Pilgrims, who came to Massachusetts in
  1620, the Puritans believed that the Church of England was a true church, though in need of major
  reforms." The Puritans were "non-separating Congregationalists."
- **Church membership was not automatic.** "The membership was composed, at least initially, of men
  and women who had undergone a conversion experience and could prove it to other members."
- (Source for all of the above: Library of Congress exhibition, "America as a Religious Refuge: The
  Seventeenth Century," https://www.loc.gov/exhibits/religion/rel01.html . **Access note:** loc.gov
  returns HTTP 403 to WebFetch; the page was retrieved with `curl -sL -A "Mozilla/5.0 ..."` and read
  locally on 2026-09-10. That technique works and should be reused.)
- **CAUTION — an error in an NPS page.** The NPS Roger Williams National Memorial timeline
  (https://www.nps.gov/rowi/learn/historyculture/rowi-timeline.htm ) says "1620: The Mayflower
  arrives in Plymouth. The Mayflower transported Puritans." **That is wrong** and it contradicts the
  Library of Congress. The *Mayflower* carried Separatists, the people called Pilgrims. Follow LOC.

### 3b. The Bible commonwealths, and the first books printed here

- The New England colonies "sought the guidance of the scriptures in regulating all aspects of the
  lives of their citizens. Scripture was cited as authority for many criminal statutes."
- **The Bay Psalm Book, 1640, is the first book published in British North America** — the Psalms
  turned into verse so they could be sung in the Massachusetts churches, by Richard Mather and two
  other ministers. Eleven copies survive. Full title: *The Whole Booke of Psalmes Faithfully
  Translated into English Metre*, Cambridge, Massachusetts: Stephen Daye, 1640.
- **John Eliot** (1604-1690), Congregational minister at Roxbury, translated the Bible into the
  Algonquian language (Cambridge: Samuel Green and Marmaduke Johnson, 1663). "At one time Eliot
  ministered to eleven hundred 'Praying Indians,' organized into fourteen New England style towns."
  LOC frames the missionary effort plainly: "ministers in all of the first British North American
  colonies strove to convert the local native populations to Christianity, often with only modest
  results."
- (Source: LOC exhibition part 1, as above. **`education` leads on the praying towns as schooling**
  — see `outlines/education.md`, 1600s, "Native children and the first schools built for them," which
  names Natick 1651, Waban, Cutshamekin, John Speen and the teacher Monequassun. Do not repeat it.)

### 3c. [VERIFY] CLEARED — Massachusetts 1642 and 1647, and the 1647 act naming Satan

**All three of the outline's tags here are cleared, and the wording is confirmed.** The verified
material already sits in `research/research-education.md` §3d, sourced there to the Paul Revere
House ( https://www.paulreverehouse.org/that-old-deluder-satan-puritan-emphasis-on-compulsory-education/ )
and the Middle Tennessee State University First Amendment Encyclopedia
( https://firstamendment.mtsu.edu/article/old-deluder-satan-act-of-1647/ ), both quoting the laws.

- **1642 — both the date and the substance are right.** The Massachusetts General Court ordered
  parents and masters to "indeavour to teach by themselves or others, their children & apprentices
  so much learning as may enable them perfectly to read the English tongue, & knowledge of the
  Capital Lawes." No school, no teacher — a duty on households.
- **1647 — the date is right and the Satan wording is real.** The act's preamble says it is "one of
  the chief projects of that old deluder Satan to keep men from the knowledge of the Scriptures."
  Towns of 50 households had to appoint someone to teach reading and writing, paid by the parents
  or masters; towns of 100 families had to set up a grammar school "to instruct youth so far as they
  may be fitted for the university."
- **This chapter's angle is the reason, not the schools.** The stated purpose of compulsory reading
  in New England was religious: so that a child could read scripture without a priest in between.
  `education` leads on the schools themselves and already carries them in full.

### 3d. [VERIFY] CLEARED — Harvard, 1636, and the ministers

- **Harvard was founded in 1636.** The 1647 law's phrase "fitted for the university" means Harvard.
  (Source: `research/research-education.md` §3e, sourced to MTSU First Amendment Encyclopedia and to
  the Harvard University Native American Program, https://hunap.harvard.edu/history .)
- **The ministry reason is documented in the founders' own promotional tract.** *New Englands First
  Fruits*, printed in England in **1643**, gives the college's purpose: **"to advance Learning and
  perpetuate it to Posterity; dreading to leave an illiterate Ministery to the Churches, when our
  present Ministers shall lie in the Dust."** (Source: Harvard Library research guide, "Early
  Curriculum at Harvard: Historical Sources," https://guides.library.harvard.edu/hua/earlycurriculum ,
  which quotes the passage and dates the pamphlet.)
- So the outline's "founded largely to keep a supply of ministers" is supported by a primary
  statement of purpose. Quote the tract rather than paraphrasing it.

### 3e. [VERIFY] — The New England Primer's first printing: NOT SETTLED, and the outline must say so

- **What is known.** The printer **Benjamin Harris** made *The New England Primer* for American
  colonial readers **between 1687 and 1690**. That range is the American Antiquarian Society's, and
  the AAS says the only evidence for the book's origins is publishers' and booksellers'
  advertisements, because the production records do not survive. The first notice of the book is an
  advertisement Harris placed in **1690**. **No copy printed before 1727 survives anywhere.**
  (Source: American Antiquarian Society, "'To Which is Added': The History, Structure, and
  Conservation of New England Primers at the American Antiquarian Society,"
  https://pastispresent.org/2024/fellowsfinds/to-which-is-added-the-history-structure-and-conservation-of-new-england-primers-at-the-american-antiquarian-society/ ,
  as recorded in `research/research-education.md` §3b.)
- **Ruling: do not print a single first-printing date.** Write the range and write why the range
  exists. A bare "1690" would be a fact the record does not carry.
- **The religion angle `education` does not take — the Primer taught children to hate Catholics.**
  The Primer carried a woodcut of the burning of **John Rogers** at Smithfield on **4 February 1555**,
  the first Protestant executed under England's Catholic Queen Mary, with his wife and ten children
  added to the picture. Beside it ran a long verse speech presented as the dying man's advice to his
  children, telling them to "Keep always God before your Eyes" and to "Abhor the arrant Whore of
  Rome, and all her Blasphemies." The Library of Congress states the consequence directly: this
  material, "read by generations of young New Englanders, doubtless helped to fuel the anti-Catholic
  prejudice that flourished in that region well into the nineteenth century." (Source: LOC
  exhibition part 1, https://www.loc.gov/exhibits/religion/rel01.html , describing the woodcut from
  *The New-England Primer Improved*, Boston: A. Ellison, 1773.) **This links straight to the 1800s
  Bible-in-schools fights in era 6. Hand it forward.**

### 3f. [VERIFY] CLEARED — Maryland's Act Concerning Religion, 1649, with its own wording

- **Passed 21 April 1649** by the Maryland General Assembly at St Mary's City. The freemen met from
  2 April and on 21 April voted twelve bills into law, this among them. **Cecil Calvert, second Lord
  Baltimore, submitted the proposals**; the Assembly rewrote the measure in part before passing it.
- **What it protected:** no person "professing to believe in Jesus Christ shall from henceforth be
  in any ways troubled, molested, or discountenanced for or in respect of his or her religion, nor
  in the free exercise thereof."
- **What it did to everyone else — this is the part the name "Toleration Act" hides, and it must be
  in the cell.** The act's own words: "whatsoever Person or Persons within this province ... shall
  from henceforth Blaspheme God that is Curse him or deny our Saviour Jesus Christ to be the Son of
  God or shall deny the holy Trinity the Father Son & Holy Ghost on the Godhead of any of the said
  three Persons of the Trinity or the unity of the Godhead or shall use or utter any reproachful
  speeches words or Language Concerning the said holy Trinity or any of the said three Persons
  thereof **shall be punished with death & Confiscation or forfeiture of all his or her lands and
  Goods to the Lord Proprietary**."
- **The name-calling clause.** Calling a person "an Heretick, Schismatick, Idolator, Puritan,
  Independent Presbyterian ... Popish Priest ... or any other name or term in a reproachful manner
  relating to matters of Religion" cost a fine of **ten shillings sterling**, half to the person
  insulted and half to the Lord Proprietary. Someone who could not pay was "publickly whipt and
  shall suffer imprisonment without bail or mainprize" until they asked forgiveness publicly before
  the magistrate.
- **What happened to it.** Maryland Puritans swept the act away in **1654** and put Catholics, Jews,
  Quakers, atheists and other dissenters under legal disabilities. In **1661**, after the monarchy
  was restored in England, Marylanders repealed the Puritan regime's laws and the act was in force
  again.
- (Sources — Maryland State Archives, the state's own archive of the document: "Religious Toleration
  in Maryland — Introduction,"
  https://msa.maryland.gov/msa/speccol/sc2200/sc2221/000025/html/intro.html ; the transcription
  pages https://msa.maryland.gov/msa/speccol/sc2200/sc2221/000025/html/transactrel1.html and
  https://msa.maryland.gov/msa/speccol/sc2200/sc2221/000025/html/transactrel3.html ; the document
  itself at https://msa.maryland.gov/msa/stagser/s1259/121/7989/html/tolerga.html .)
- **How to teach it, plainly:** the first law in this land protecting freedom of worship protected
  only Christians who believed in the Trinity, and made denying the Trinity a hanging matter.

### 3g. Quakers, and four people hanged in Boston for their religion

- **In England:** "By 1680, 10,000 Quakers had been imprisoned in England, and 243 had died of
  torture and mistreatment in the King's jails." (Source: LOC part 2,

### 3h. Pennsylvania — a colony that let people alone

- **1681:** William Penn (1644-1718) "parlayed a debt owed by Charles II to his father into a
  charter for the province of Pennsylvania." **By 1685 as many as 8,000 Quakers had come.**
- **Penn's *Frame of Government*, 1682**, promised that everyone who believed in "One Almighty and
  eternal God ... shall in no wayes be molested or prejudiced for their Religious Perswasion or
  Practice in matters of Faith and Worship, **nor shall they be compelled at any time to frequent or
  maintain any Religious Worship, Place or Ministry whatever**." The second half matters as much as
  the first: nobody had to attend, and nobody had to pay for, a church.
- **What made Quaker worship offensive to other colonists:** the "insistence on equality for women,
  including the right ... to speak in Meeting for Worship and to preach the Gospel."
- **Germans arrived for the same reason.** "The first group of Germans to settle in Pennsylvania
  arrived in Philadelphia in **1683** from Krefeld, Germany, and included Mennonites and possibly
  some Dutch Quakers." Most early German arrivals were Mennonites, Dunkers, Schwenkfelders,
  Moravians and German Baptists fleeing religious persecution; the province looked like "an asylum
  for banished sects."
- (Source: LOC part 2, https://www.loc.gov/exhibits/religion/rel01-2.html .)

### 3j. New Mexico — ceremonies punished, and the rising of 1680

- **The Pueblo nations' own account.** Indian Pueblo Cultural Center: "For over eighty years, the
  Pueblo people suffered violence, cruelty, and hunger; exploitation of labor and resources;
  harassment and persecution for practicing long-held religious traditions that are interwoven in

  "Pueblo Revolt," https://indianpueblo.org/pueblo-revolt/ . **This is a Pueblo-run institution

### 3k. STORY — Roger Williams (`roger-williams`, status upgraded to `verified`)

- Born in London about **1603**, son of a merchant, studied at Cambridge; an ordained minister in
  the Church of England who "was dissatisfied with the hierarchy, political power, wealth, and
  ornamentation" of it.
- **Arrived in Massachusetts Bay in 1631**, a year after the Puritans settled it. "In the
  Massachusetts colony, all aspects of daily life were regulated by Puritan religious belief and
  enforced by the government."
- **His idea:** "that the government should not enforce moral or religious beliefs, and that civic
  life should be separate from individuals' spiritual lives." NPS calls what he preached in
  Massachusetts "new and dangerous ideas."
- **Banished.** NPS timeline: "**1636:** The Massachusetts government banishes Roger Williams for
  preaching his dangerous political and religious ideas about 'soul liberty' and limiting the
  government's power to only secular matters." LOC: "Expelled from Massachusetts in the dead of
  winter in 1636." He fled rather than be sent back to England, "where he may have been put to
  death." **Date note:** many accounts put the General Court's sentence in **October 1635** with the
  flight in the winter that followed. NPS and LOC both print 1636. **Do not print a month. Write
  that the court ordered him out and he fled in the winter.**
- **How he survived.** "Williams survived the harsh winter thanks to a rescue from the Wampanoag
  people," with whom he had traded and whose language he had learned. He then travelled by canoe,
  with aid from Wampanoag and Narragansett people, to a freshwater spring at Moshassuck, and settled
  there **with the permission of the Narragansett**. He named it **Providence**, crediting "God's
  merciful providence unto me." In **1638** the Narragansett sachems **Miantonomi and Canonicus**
  signed a deed formally establishing the settlement, keeping trading rights inside it.
- **What he wanted the place to be, in his own words:** "a shelter for persons distressed for
  conscience."
- **What Rhode Island actually was:** "Anglicans, Puritans, Quakers, Catholics, Jews, atheists, and
  members of other faiths lived as neighbors with equal rights." LOC: "He welcomed people of every
  shade of religious belief, even some regarded as dangerously misguided."
- **His books.** *A Key into the Language of America*, **1643**, written on a voyage to England to
  get a patent for the colony. ***The Bloudy Tenent of Persecution, for cause of Conscience***,
  **1644**. From it: "God requireth not an uniformity of Religion to be inacted and inforced in any
  civill state; which inforced uniformity (sooner or later) is the greatest occasion of civill
  Warre, ravishing of conscience, persecution of Christ Jesus in his servants, and of hypocrisy and
  destruction of millions of souls." Also his: **"forced worship stinks in God's nostrils."**
- **The charter.** Parliament granted Rhode Island a patent in **1644**, joining Providence, Newport
  and Portsmouth. Williams went back to England in **1663** and Charles II signed a charter
  recognising the colony's "livlie experiment."
- **Died 1683** in Providence; buried at Prospect Terrace, Providence. **Congress established Roger
  Williams National Memorial in 1965** for his "outstanding contributions to the development of the
  principles of freedom in this country."
- **Do not flatten him into a modern liberal.** He held his own beliefs strongly and thought many of
  his neighbours wrong; his argument was that the government had no business enforcing any of it.
- (Sources: NPS, "Roger Williams — Minister, Merchant, Magistrate,"
  https://www.nps.gov/people/roger-williams.htm ; NPS, Roger Williams National Memorial, "History &
  Culture," https://www.nps.gov/rowi/learn/historyculture/index.htm , and its timeline
  https://www.nps.gov/rowi/learn/historyculture/rowi-timeline.htm ; LOC exhibition part 2,
  https://www.loc.gov/exhibits/religion/rel01-2.html .)
- **Not verified, so not written:** the common claim that Williams founded the first Baptist church
  in America, in Providence in 1638 or 1639. No institutional source was reached for it in this
  pass. **Left out.**

### 3l. STORY — Anne Hutchinson (`anne-hutchinson-religion`, status upgraded to `verified`)

**This chapter takes her theology. `rights-movements` (slug `anne-hutchinson-rights-movements`)
already tells the trial and the sentence in full, verbatim from the record, and `immigration`
(`anne-hutchinson-immigration`) tells the crossing. Do not repeat either.** The rights-movements
block itself says: "Her crossing is told in `immigration`; her theology in `religion`."

Facts below are already verified per-source in `research/research-rights-movements.md` §"Anne
Hutchinson" (NWHM; Mass Moments; NPS Saugus Iron Works; NPS Roger Williams NM; two independent
transcriptions of the trial record).

- Born about **1591** at Alford, Lincolnshire; crossed on the *Griffin* in the autumn of **1634**,
  following her minister **John Cotton** to Boston.
- **The meetings, which are the religion angle.** From **1636** she held a meeting in her own house
  once a week to go back over the Sunday sermon. It began as a meeting for women; men came too,
  including men who mattered in the colony. In a colony where the minister's word in the meeting
  house was the authorised teaching, a second meeting in a private house was a rival service.
- **What she taught.** That **grace alone** decided who was saved, not good behaviour — and that she
  could tell which ministers and which neighbours had received God's grace and which had not. The
  second half is what made it unanswerable: she was judging the colony's ministers.
- **The word her opponents used.** The NPS timeline calls her "the 'Antinomian' preacher." *Antinomian*
  means "against the law." **Define it at first use and attribute it:** it was what her opponents
  called her teaching, meaning they said it freed a saved person from having to obey rules. It is a
  charge, not a self-description.
- **What finished her at the trial:** she said God had spoken to her directly, by "an immediate
  revelation," and would curse the Puritans if they harmed her. The court called that blasphemy.
- **Two separate proceedings, five months apart — do not merge them.** The General Court tried and
  banished her at Newtown (now Cambridge) in **November 1637**. The **church** at Boston tried her
  again and **excommunicated her in March 1638**, its charge including that she had "rather been a
  Husband than a Wife and a preacher than a Hearer; and a Magistrate than a Subject."
- **After.** Spring 1638 to Aquidneck Island in what became Rhode Island, where she founded the town
  of **Portsmouth** (NPS timeline). After her husband died she moved in **1642** onto Dutch land on
  Long Island Sound. In **1643** she and several of her children were killed in a Siwanoy attack;
  one daughter survived.
- **The point this chapter is making:** a colony founded by people who had crossed an ocean to
  worship as they chose put a woman out of it for worshipping as she chose, and then put her out of
  the congregation as well.

## ERA 4 — 1700 to 1750 (`state="full"`)

### 4a. The era frame — churchgoing was already high before the revival

- "Between 1700 and 1740, an estimated **75 to 80 percent of the population attended churches**,
  which were being built at a headlong pace." Scholars now describe religion in the colonies after
  1700 as in "the ascension rather than the declension."
- **The Great Awakening** "swept the English-speaking world, as religious energy vibrated between
  England, Wales, Scotland and the American colonies in the **1730s and 1740s**." Its core idea was
  the **"new birth"** — a conversion the preacher's words were meant to produce.
- **It split churches.** Supporters were called **"New Lights"** among Congregationalists and **"New
  Side"** among Presbyterians; opponents were **"Old Lights"** and **"Old Side."** Many New England
  New Lights became Separate Baptists. Supporters of the revival — Presbyterians, Baptists and
  Methodists — "became the largest American Protestant denominations by the first decades of the
  nineteenth century." Anglicans, Quakers and Congregationalists "were left behind."
- The revival "had spent its force in New England by the mid-1740s" and was carried south, setting
  off revivals that ran into the 1800s.
- **A countercurrent in the same era: Deism**, which "stressed morality and rejected the orthodox
  Christian view of the divinity of Christ." Thomas Jefferson and John Adams were among its
  American adherents. Deists were "never more than 'a minority within a minority.'"
- **A visible measure for the cell:** three views of New York City — 1690, 1730 and 1771 — show the
  churches multiplying. "An empty vista in 1690 had become a forest of eighteen steeples by 1771."
- (Source for all of this: Library of Congress exhibition, "Religion in Eighteenth-Century America,"
  https://www.loc.gov/exhibits/religion/rel02.html . Retrieved by `curl` on 2026-09-10; loc.gov
  refuses WebFetch.)

### 4b. [VERIFY] RESOLVED — the Awakening colleges, and the outline's claim was too wide

### 4c. STORY — George Whitefield (`george-whitefield`, status upgraded to `verified`)

- **1714-1770.** Born Gloucester, England, **16 December 1714**. Ordained in the Church of England,
  "with which he was constantly at odds."
- **Seven visits to America**, the first in **1738** — he reached Georgia in **May 1738**. He came
  back in **November 1739**, landing at Philadelphia, and reached Savannah in **January 1740**.
- **His preaching tour of the colonies, 1739 to 1741, "was the high-water mark of the Great
  Awakening there."** LOC: **"A sermon in Boston attracted as many as 30,000 people."**
- **Why he preached outdoors.** "The doors of many churches were closed to him," so he carried a
  **collapsible field pulpit**. Its first recorded use was at **Moorfields, England, 9 April 1742**,
  before a crowd estimated at "twenty or thirty thousand people," where hecklers "showered the
  evangelist with 'stones, rotten eggs and pieces of dead cat.'" He is estimated to have preached
  **two thousand sermons** from that pulpit.
- **What he preached.** The "new birth" — the conversion experience. His own admission, from *The
  Marks of the New Birth. A Sermon*, New York: William Bradford, 1739: "how this glorious Change is
  wrought in the Soul cannot easily be explained."
- **Why the crowds came.** LOC lists it plainly: "his resonant voice, theatrical presentation,
  emotional stimulation, message simplification and clever exploitation of emerging advertising
  techniques."
- **He had enemies, and what they accused him of.** A 1763 London engraving, *Dr. Squintum's
  Exaltation or the Reformation*, mocked his eye disease and charged him with sexual immorality and
  with getting rich off his ministry.
- (Sources for the above: LOC, "Religion in Eighteenth-Century America,"
  https://www.loc.gov/exhibits/religion/rel02.html ; New Georgia Encyclopedia, "George Whitefield
  (1714-1770)," https://www.georgiaencyclopedia.org/articles/history-archaeology/george-whitefield-1714-1770/ .)

**WHITEFIELD AND SLAVERY — this must be in the story, in his own words. No softening.**

- He founded **Bethesda**, an orphanage near Savannah, in **1740**, in a colony where slavery was
  then banned. The New Georgia Encyclopedia says he "had provided approximately $2,539 toward the
  cost of constructing Bethesda Orphan House."
- **1740, his open letter** to "the Inhabitants of Maryland, Virginia, North and South Carolina
  concerning their Negroes," printed in the *Pennsylvania Gazette*, told enslavers off for
  mistreating the people they held and for neglecting their souls: "I know the general Pretence for
  this neglect of their Souls is, That teaching them Christianity would make them proud, and
  consequently unwilling to submit to Slavery; But what a dreadful Reflection is this on your Holy
  Religion?" **He never condemned slavery itself in it.** The Penn & Slavery Project states that
  directly.
- **1741**, in *An Account of the Orphan House in Georgia*: "as for manuring more land than the
  hired servants and great boys can manage, it is impracticable without a few negroes."
- **1747**, to an unnamed benefactor: "God has put into the hearts of my South Carolina friends, to
  contribute liberally towards purchasing a plantation and slaves in this province which I purpose
  to devote to the support of Bethesda ... One negroe has been given me — Some more I purpose to
  purchase this week."
- **From 1747 he lobbied to have slavery legalised in Georgia**, arguing both his orphanage and the
  colony would fail without it: "Had negroes been allowed, I should now have had a sufficiency to
  support a great many orphans." "Georgia never can be a flourishing province, unless negroes are
  employed." He gave the trustees an ultimatum — he could not "cultivate the plantation in any
  manner" unless the ban went.
- **The ban went.** The Penn & Slavery Project dates the legalisation to **1752, when Georgia became
  a royal colony**; other accounts date it to **1751**. **State the disagreement or write "in the
  early 1750s."**
- **At his death in 1770 he left 4,000 acres in Georgia and 50 enslaved people** to the Countess of
  Huntingdon.
- (Source: Penn & Slavery Project, University of Pennsylvania Archives, "George Whitefield," by
  VanJessica Gladney, https://pennandslaveryproject.archives.upenn.edu/2025/07/10/george-whitefield/ ,
  which quotes each of the passages above. A statue of Whitefield was put up at the University of
  Pennsylvania in 1919; the university announced plans to remove it in 2020.)
- **He died at Newburyport, Massachusetts, 30 September 1770**, and is buried there. His death "made

### 4d. STORY — Jonathan Edwards (`jonathan-edwards`, status upgraded to `verified`)

- Born **5 October 1703** at East Windsor, Connecticut, the fifth of eleven children of the Rev.
  Timothy and Esther Edwards. Yale undergraduate 1716-1720, valedictorian of the class of 1720;
  master's degree three years later; then two years as a college tutor.
- **In 1726 he joined his grandfather Solomon Stoddard at the church in Northampton, Massachusetts**
  — "the largest and most influential church outside of Boston" — and succeeded him there. Married
  **Sarah Pierpont** in 1727; ten children.
- **The revival: 1734-1735**, in his own congregation. LOC calls it "a harbinger of the Awakening."
- **His book about it:** *A Faithful Narrative of the Surprising Work of God in the Conversion of
  Many Hundred Souls in Northampton*. **DATE CONFLICT WITHIN ONE INSTITUTION:** the Jonathan Edwards
  Center at Yale dates it **1738** on its biography page
  ( http://edwards.yale.edu/research/about-edwards/biography ) and **1737** on its Major Works page
  ( http://edwards.yale.edu/research/major-works/faithful-narrative ). **Do not print a year in the
  outline.** It described the awakening in his church and "served as an empirical model for American
  and British revivalists alike."
- **"Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God," preached at Enfield, Connecticut, on 8 July 1741** —
  called by Yale "the most famous sermon in American history." (Source: Connecticut History, a
  CTHumanities project, "Jonathan Edwards' Famous Sermon — Today in History: July 8,"
  https://connecticuthistory.org/jonathan-edwards-delivers-famous-sermon/ ; Yale, biography, as
  above.)
- **The sermon's own words, verified from a published edition on 2026-09-10.** "...your own care
  and prudence, and best contrivance, and all your righteousness, would have no more influence to
  uphold you and keep you out of hell than a spider's web would have to stop a falling rock." And:

## ERA 5 — 1750 to 1800 (`state="full"`)

### 5a. [VERIFY] CLEARED — Virginia's Statute for Religious Freedom, 1786, and what it replaced

**What was there before.** Virginia had an established church — the Church of England. Colonists
were required to attend its services and to pay taxes that supported its ministers. Parish vestries
also ran government business such as poor relief and the supervision of orphans. (Encyclopedia
Virginia, "Virginia Statute for Establishing Religious Freedom (1786),"
https://encyclopediavirginia.org/entries/virginia-statute-for-establishing-religious-freedom-1786/ )

**The fight, in order, with dates:**
- **1779** — the Virginia Assembly stopped tax support for Church of England ministers. Jefferson
  had drafted his bill in **1777**; it was introduced in the House of Delegates in **1779** and did
  not pass. (LOC, "Religion and the State Governments,"
  https://www.loc.gov/exhibits/religion/rel05.html ; Encyclopedia Virginia, as above.)
- **24 December 1784** — **Patrick Henry** put in *A Bill Establishing a Provision for Teachers of
  the Christian Religion*: a tax for religion that let each taxpayer send his share to the church of
  his choice. Voting against postponing it — that is, **for** the religious tax — was the future
  Chief Justice **John Marshall**. Madison led the push to postpone it to the fall 1785 session,
  which gave opponents time to organise. (LOC rel05, with the broadside itself, Manuscript Division,
  item 133.)
- **20 June 1785** — **James Madison** published, anonymously, his *Memorial and Remonstrance
  Against Religious Assessments*. (Encyclopedia Virginia, as above.)
- **3 October 1785** — **George Washington** wrote to George Mason that he did not in principle
  object to "making people pay towards the support of that which they profess," but thought it
  "impolitic" to pass a measure that would disturb public tranquility. (LOC rel05, item 137;
  Washington to Mason, Letterbook 1785-1786, Manuscript Division, LOC.) **Washington was on the
  other side of this from Madison and Jefferson.** Do not write the founders as one voice on it.
- **Fall 1785** — Madison had the votes; the religious tax was defeated. **31 October 1785** he
  reintroduced Jefferson's bill among 117 revised laws. (Encyclopedia Virginia.)
- **16 January 1786** — the General Assembly adopted the Statute for Establishing Religious Freedom.
  **19 January 1786** — signed into law. (Encyclopedia Virginia.)

**Its own words:** "no man shall be compelled to frequent or support any religious worship, place,
or ministry whatsoever," and citizens are "free to profess, and by argument to maintain, their
opinions in matters of Religion." (Encyclopedia Virginia, quoting the statute.)

**Who was on each side.** For the tax: Anglicans, arguing Christianity was the "best means of
promoting Virtue, Peace, and Prosperity"; **Richard Henry Lee** backed it too, writing to Madison on
26 November 1784 that "the experience of all times shows religion to be the guardian of morals."
Against it: Baptists, most Presbyterians, and theological liberals — they argued that state money
corrupts a church, and that government in religion breaks a person's natural rights. (LOC rel05.)

**Jefferson's own description of who the statute was meant to cover**, from his account of the
debate: the protection was meant to reach "the Jew and the Gentile, . . . the Mahometan, the Hindoo,
and Infidel of every denomination." (Quoted in John Witte, Jr., "'A Most Mild and Equitable
Establishment of Religion': John Adams and the Massachusetts Experiment," *Journal of Church and
State* 41 (1999): 213-252, author's draft p. 1,
https://www.johnwittejr.com/uploads/5/4/6/6/54662393/a67.pdf )

**LOC's caution, which the outline must carry:** "the outcome in Virginia of the state-church
debate did not . . . represent the views of the majority of American states that wrestled with this
issue in the 1780s." General-assessment religious taxes **took effect in Massachusetts, Connecticut
and New Hampshire**, and were **passed but not carried out in Maryland and Georgia.** (LOC rel05.)
Virginia was the exception, not the pattern. **The era-5 cell must not leave a reader thinking the
Virginia statute ended church taxes in America.**

### 5b. The Constitution and the First Amendment — dates from the LOC exhibit

- **Article VI, "no religious Test shall ever be required as Qualification"** for federal office.
  Proposed by **Charles Pinckney of South Carolina on 20 August 1787**, adopted by the full
  Convention **30 August 1787**; the Constitution was adopted **17 September 1787**. Apart from that
  clause the Constitution said almost nothing about religion, and people complained: "many pious
  people" said it contained "no recognition of his mercies to us . . . or even of his existence."
- **Benjamin Franklin, 28 June 1787**, moved that the Convention open each day with prayers, saying
  "the longer I live, the more convincing proofs I see of this Truth — that God governs in the
  Affairs of Men." **The motion failed**, reportedly because the Convention had no money to pay
  local clergymen to act as chaplains.
- **28 September 1789** — both houses of Congress voted to send twelve amendments to the states.
  **December 1791** — the required three quarters of the states had ratified ten of them. The
  religion clause: "Congress shall make no law respecting an establishment of religion, or
  prohibiting the free exercise thereof."
- **Who pushed for it:** Madison's Virginia constituents included **large numbers of Baptists who
  wanted freedom of religion secured**, and they expected him to get a bill of rights. In his notes
  for the **8 June 1789** speech introducing it, Madison set himself against a "national" religion.
- (All: LOC, "Religion and the Federal Government, Part 1,"
  https://www.loc.gov/exhibits/religion/rel06.html )
- **Shared with `government-politics`** (FULLY RESEARCHED — it owns the establishment-clause cases as
  law). This chapter's angle is what it meant for worship, not the doctrine the courts built later.

### 5c. [VERIFY] CLEARED — Massachusetts, the last state, 1833

**What Massachusetts had.** Article III of the 1780 Declaration of Rights let and required the
legislature to make towns and parishes pay "for the support and maintenance of **public Protestant
teachers of piety, religion and morality**," and let the legislature "enjoin upon all the subjects an
attendance upon the instructions of the public teachers aforesaid, at stated times and seasons."
A person's church tax went to his own denomination's teacher if he had one; otherwise it went to the
parish's. (Massachusetts Constitution, Part the First, Article III, Massachusetts General Court,
https://malegislature.gov/Laws/Constitution )

**How it was adopted.** Massachusetts spent 1778-1780 trying to write a constitution voters would
accept; a 1778 draft was voted down. Article III was the most contested piece. **"Despite
substantial doubt that Article Three had been approved by the required two thirds of the voters, in
1780 Massachusetts authorities declared it and the rest of the state constitution to have been duly
adopted."** (LOC rel05 — the Library of Congress states this plainly; keep it.)

**The two sides, both named and quotable.** **Phillips Payson**, Congregational minister at Chelsea,
in his election sermon of **27 May 1778**: "let the restraints of religion once be broken down . . .
and one might well defy all human wisdom and power to support and preserve order and government in
the state." **Isaac Backus (1724-1806)**, the leader of the New England Baptists, answered him in
*Government and Liberty Described and Ecclesiastical Tyranny Exposed* (Boston: Powars and Willis,
1778): state support of religion corrupts churches, and the best thing a government can do for
religion is leave it alone. (LOC rel05, items 126 and 127.)

**How it ended.** Article III stood **53 years**. **In 1833 Amendment Article XI replaced it.** The
amendment kept the language about public worship promoting "the happiness and prosperity of a
people" but ended the tax: religious societies may elect their own pastors, contract for their
support, and **raise their own money**; and any member may leave by filing a written notice with the
society's clerk and owes nothing after that. The Massachusetts Constitution's own text carries the
note "[Art. XI of the Amendments substituted for this]" under Article III. Witte, who quotes
Amendment XI in full, notes that it "outlawed the institutional establishment of the public
religion" while leaving the constitution's language about public worship and morality standing.
(Massachusetts General Court, https://malegislature.gov/Laws/Constitution ; Witte, as above, §III.)

**NOT SETTLED — the exact ratification date.** The state's own published constitution gives the
amendment as "Article XI" with no date, and 1833 is the year the sources carry. **The outline says
1833 and gives no day.** Do not write "11 November 1833" — nothing consulted here supports a
specific day.

**Boundary note.** 1833 falls in era 6. It is verified here because the era-5 cell has to say where
disestablishment ended up, and the sentence must make the later date clear.

### 5d. STORY — Richard Allen (`richard-allen`, status upgraded to `verified`)

- **Born 14 February 1760**, enslaved to **Benjamin Chew**, a lawyer in Philadelphia; sold with his
  family to **Stokely Sturgis**, a farmer near **Dover, Delaware**. (National Archives,
  "Rediscovering Black History: Richard Allen and the Origins of the AME Church," 9 June 2021,
  https://rediscovering-black-history.blogs.archives.gov/2021/06/09/richard-allen/ )
- **He began preaching as a teenager while still enslaved.** He **bought his freedom in 1780 for
  $2,000**, working on his own time over about five years. (National Archives, as above.) **Note the
  money:** that is $2,000 in the **Continental currency of 1780**, which had lost most of its value.
  Do not convert it to modern dollars in the book without saying which currency it was.
- **February 1786 he came to Philadelphia.** In his own account: "I preached in the commons, in
  Southwark, [in] Northern Liberties, and wherever I could find an opening. I frequently preached
  twice a day, at 5 o'clock in the morning and in the evening, and it was not uncommon for me to
  preach from four to five times a day. I established prayer meetings; I raised a society, in 1786,
  of forty-two members."
- **The three men who joined him** in wanting a church of their own: **Absalom Jones, William White,
  and Dorus Ginnings.** The elder at St. George's forbade their prayer meetings; Allen wrote that
  the Black members "were considered as a nuisance."
- **THE INCIDENT AT ST. GEORGE'S, in Allen's own words.** Black members were moved out of the seats
  they usually sat in and placed around the wall; then one Sunday the sexton sent them up to the
  gallery. They knelt to pray, and a trustee took hold of Absalom Jones and pulled him up off his
  knees: "You must get up — you must not kneel here." Jones answered, "Wait until prayer is over, and
  I will get up and trouble you no more." The trustee said, "No, you must get up now, or I will call
  for aid and force you away," and beckoned a second trustee, who went to pull up William White. "By
  this time, prayer was over, and we all went out of the church in a body, and they were no more
  plagued with us in the church." Allen adds that the Black members had themselves **paid toward
  finishing St. George's, "in building the gallery and laying new floors,"** and were turned out just
  as the building was made comfortable.
- **[VERIFY] — THE DATE OF THE WALKOUT IS DISPUTED, AND THE OUTLINE MUST SAY SO.** Allen's
  autobiography does not date it. The traditional date is **1787**; **Richard S. Newman**,
  *Freedom's Prophet: Bishop Richard Allen, the AME Church, and the Black Founding Fathers* (New

  the new church's 1793 groundbreaking and would make it a planned protest rather than a sudden one.
  The Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia places the confrontation "in the early 1790s." **Give
  both and name the disagreement; do not pick one.**

- **After the walkout:** they hired a storeroom and were threatened with being "read publicly out of

### 5e2. George Liele and Andrew Bryan — the first Black Baptist congregation in the South

## ERA 6 — 1800 to 1850 (`state="full"`)

### 6a. The era frame — evangelicalism as the country's working religion

- LOC: "The religion of the new American republic was evangelicalism, which, between 1800 and the
  Civil War, was the 'grand absorbing theme' of American religious life." Revivals were so frequent
  that the journals tracking them gave up: in **1827** one wrote that "revivals, we rejoice to say,
  are becoming too numerous in our country to admit of being generally mentioned in our Record."
- Historians see "evangelicalism emerging as a kind of national church or national religion" between
  Jefferson's inauguration and Lincoln's.
- The people running it tied the religion to the country: a preacher in **1826** claimed "an
  association between Religion and Patriotism"; the **American Home Missionary Society** told its
  supporters in **1826** that "we are doing the work of patriotism no less than Christianity."
- **The link to era 5's disestablishment, in LOC's own words:** "With the disappearance of efforts by
  government to create morality in the body politic (symbolized by the termination in 1833 of
  Massachusetts's tax support for churches) evangelical, benevolent societies assumed that role."
- **The other side.** **Thomas Paine's *The Age of Reason*** (Philadelphia, 1794) was called "the
  atheist's bible"; it was actually a deist argument, and its tone scandalised readers.
- (All: LOC, "Religion and the New Republic,"
  https://www.loc.gov/exhibits/religion/rel07.html )

### 6b. Camp meetings and the Kentucky revival

- **1800** — major revivals began at both ends of the country: "the decorous Second Great Awakening
  in New England and the exuberant Great Revival in Kentucky."
- **The camp meeting was the Kentucky revival's invention.** Presbyterian ministers organised the
  first ones, modelling them on the long outdoor "communion seasons" of the Church of Scotland.
  Families loaded wagons, drove to the meeting, pitched tents and stayed several days.
- **The Presbyterians and Baptists who started them soon repudiated them** because of the emotion
  they produced. **The Methodists kept them** and carried them east, where camp meetings became a
  Methodist signature for decades.
- **Cane Ridge, near Lexington, Kentucky, summer 1801**, organised by the Presbyterian minister
  **Barton Stone**. Stone and **Alexander Campbell** later left the Presbyterians and formed the
  **Disciples of Christ in 1832**.
- **A camp meeting's layout, from a first-hand drawing.** **Benjamin Henry Latrobe** sketched a
  Methodist camp meeting in **Fairfax County, Virginia, on 8 August 1809**. His plan shows the men's
  seats separated from the women's and the **"negro tents" separated from the whites'**. LOC calls
  this "an example of the racial segregation that prompted black Methodists to withdraw from the
  denomination a few years later." Latrobe also drew, at the right of the main camp, "a boarded
  enclosure filled with straw, into which the converted were thrown that they might kick about
  without injuring themselves."
- **Lorenzo Dow (1777-1834)**, a travelling Methodist evangelist, whose hearers went into what was
  called the jerking exercise.
- (All: LOC rel07, items 184-187, 195, 197.)

### 6c. [VERIFY] CLEARED — Sunday schools: they began as reading schools

- **The Sunday School movement started in England in the 1780s.**
- **1791 — "a group of Philadelphia pastors and merchants founded the First Day Society."** It "paid
  local schoolmasters to teach boys and girls, as well as illiterate men and women, to read and write
  while using the Bible as the textbook." **Sunday was the day poor working children were not at
  work.** This is the point the outline must make: at first the Sunday school was where a working
  child learned to read, because there were no free public schools yet.
- **1817 — Philadelphia businessmen set up the Sunday and Adult School Union**, using volunteer
  teachers in poorer neighbourhoods. **"Within one year, the union had opened forty-three Sunday
  schools in the city and was instructing 5,970 pupils."**
- It became the **American Sunday School Union**, which "expanded nationally, hiring missionaries who
  started Sunday schools in both urban and rural areas, as well as Native American reservations," and
  became a major publisher of children's books.
- (Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia, "Great Awakenings,"
  https://philadelphiaencyclopedia.org/essays/great-awakenings/ )
- **The Sunday school was one of the six biggest benevolent societies.** LOC lists the six largest of
  1826-1827: the American Education Society, the American Board of Foreign Missions, the American
  Bible Society, the **American Sunday-School Union**, the American Tract Society, and the American

### 6d. [VERIFY] CLEARED — the Bible in the public schools, and the Philadelphia riots of 1844

**The city is Philadelphia and the years are 1842 to 1844.**

- **1842** — Bishop **Francis Patrick Kenrick (1796-1863)** wrote to the Board of Controllers of the
  public schools. He objected to Protestant teachers leading pupils in Protestant hymns and requiring
  them to read the **King James Bible**, and asked that Catholic children be allowed the **Douay
  Bible** and be excused from Protestant religious exercises.
- **Early 1843 — the board's compromise, and why it failed.** The board excused Catholic children
  from the King James Bible and allowed any Bible "without note or comment" as a substitute.
  **The Douay has notes and comments, so that ruling banned it.** (Villanova University, Falvey
  Library exhibit, "Chaos in the Streets: The Philadelphia Riots of 1844 — Bible Controversy,"
  https://exhibits.library.villanova.edu/index.php/chaos-in-the-streets-the-philadelphia-riots-of-1844/bible-controversy )
- **February 1844** — **Hugh Clark (1796-1862)**, a Catholic school director in Kensington,
  suggested suspending Bible reading until the board could write a policy both sides could accept.
  **A rumour spread that Clark had ordered a teacher, Miss Bedford, to stop reading the Protestant
  Bible. Testimony showed the rumour was false.** Nativists used it anyway.
- **The May riots, 3-10 May 1844, in Kensington.** A nativist rally on 3 May in Independence Square
  moved to Kensington. On **6 May** a nativist named **George Shiffler (1825-44)** was shot dead; by

  Queen Street. Militia under Brigadier General **George Cadwalader (1806-79)** fought the crowd with
  cannon. **By the morning of 7 July four militiamen and probably a dozen rioters were dead, with

### 6e. Latter-day Saints — founding, and the expulsions

- **Joseph Smith (1805-1844)** and many of his first followers grew up in the part of western New
  York called the **"Burned Over District,"** so named because it had been "scorched" by so many
  revivals. LOC: the church "cannot be considered as the product of revivalism or as a splintering

- **The Book of Mormon, Palmyra, New York: E. B. Grandin, 1830.** Smith said he translated it from

  witnesses." **Report this as what Smith and his followers said, in their terms. Do not endorse it
  and do not mock it.**
- **The church moved New York → Ohio → Missouri → Illinois.** (LOC rel07, items 198-200.)
- **MISSOURI. The state's own archives on what happened, and it must not be softened.** Missouri
  created **Caldwell County in 1836** as a Mormon refuge — the Missouri State Archives calls it a
  "separate but equal" proposal. Converts kept arriving and settled beyond it. After an election riot
  in 1838 and a Mormon attack on a militia unit they took for a mob, **Governor Lilburn Boggs, on 27
  October 1838, ordered the Latter-day Saints expelled from the state or, in the order's own word,
  "exterminated," if necessary.** (Missouri Secretary of State, Missouri State Archives, "The

  Executive Order 44, 27 October 1838, in the Archives' Mormon War Papers,

  attack on the small Mormon community of Haun's Mill, massacring **eighteen** unsuspecting men and
  boys." **Over the next year about eight thousand church members, "often ragged and deprived of

- **Boggs's order stayed on the books until Governor Bond rescinded it on 25 June 1976.** (Missouri
  State Archives, executive orders finding aid, as above.)
- **ILLINOIS.** The church settled at **Nauvoo**. NPS lists the causes of the trouble there:
  neighbours' anger at "Mormons' clannish business practices, accusations of theft, their electoral

  conflict inside the church as well as outside it. **After Smith ordered an opposition newspaper's
  press destroyed, he was jailed at Carthage, and on 27 June 1844 "a mob broke into the jail and

  Smith was apparently shot to death by a mob, one of whose members approached him with the
  intention, which was thwarted, of beheading him." **Vigilantes then attacked Mormon farms around
  Nauvoo.**

### 6f. Religion under slavery — what the record says, from both sides, named

**This is the chapter's own hard material and it is not `slavery-freedom`'s to carry alone.**

- **Enslaved people were kept from ordinary church attendance.** Charles Colcock Jones's own 1842

  administration of the word in company with their masters," and that in the Southern states "some
  portion of the churches where the white population assemble is usually set apart for the blacks."
- **What the planters' missions were told to teach.** **Charles Colcock Jones (1804-1863)**, a
  Presbyterian minister at Riceboro, **Liberty County, Georgia**, wrote *The Religious Instruction of
  the Negroes in the United States* (dated 4 July 1842) to persuade planters and ministers to teach
  Christianity to the people they held. **The scripture he told ministers to use is about obedience.**
  He cites Ephesians 6:5 and Colossians 3:22 as apostolic commands "to servants," and 1 Timothy 6:1
  — "let as many servants as are under the yoke count their masters worthy of all honor" — as proof
  that a minister's duty is "to give religious instruction to servants, and to inculcate upon them
  the duties of their station."
- (Charles Colcock Jones, *The Religious Instruction of the Negroes in the United States*, full text
  at Documenting the American South, University of North Carolina,
  https://docsouth.unc.edu/church/jones/jones.html ; the quoted passages are at pp. 54-55 and 189-190
  of the 1842 edition as paginated in that text.)
- **What it looked like to the person it was done to — a named, first-hand, published account.**
  **Frederick Douglass**, *Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass, an American Slave* (1845):
  - **"In August, 1832, my master attended a Methodist camp-meeting held in the Bay-side, Talbot
    county, and there experienced religion."** Douglass hoped the conversion would free him or at
    least make Thomas Auld kinder. "I was disappointed in both these respects. . . . I believe him
    to have been a much worse man after his conversion than before. Prior to his conversion, he
    relied upon his own depravity to shield and sustain him in his savage barbarity; but after his
    conversion, he found religious sanction and support for his slaveholding cruelty."
  - **Auld became a Methodist class-leader and exhorter, and his house was the preachers' home**:

  - **The Sabbath school that was broken up.** At St. Michael's, Maryland, a white young man named

  - **The whipping of Henny, with the verse quoted over it.** Douglass: "I have seen him tie up a
    lame young woman, and whip her with a heavy cowskin upon her naked shoulders, causing the warm

    stripes.'" Auld kept her tied "four or five hours at a time," whipping her before breakfast,
    going to his store, and whipping her again at dinner "in the places already made raw." **Henny's

### 6g. The churches split over slavery, before the country did

### 6i. STORY — Charles Grandison Finney (`charles-grandison-finney`, status upgraded to `verified`)

- **Born Warren, Litchfield County, Connecticut, 29 August 1792.** His own first sentence: "I was
  born in Warren, Litchfield county, Connecticut, August 29, 1792. When I was about two years old, my
  father removed to Oneida county, New York, which was, at that time, to a great extent, a
  wilderness." He wrote that his parents were not church members, that there were few religious books,
  and that he "seldom heard a sermon." (*Memoirs of Rev. Charles G. Finney, Written by Himself*
  (New York: A. S. Barnes, 1876), ch. 1.)
- **He was training as a lawyer at Adams, Jefferson County, New York**, when he was converted in
  **1821**. Oberlin describes it as a "three-day experience" that turned "a worldly lawyer into an
  evangelist."
- **What he said the next morning, in his own words**, to a client whose case he was due in court to
  argue: "Deacon B, I have a retainer from the Lord Jesus Christ to plead His cause, and I cannot
  plead yours." (*Memoirs*, ch. 2.)
- **The anxious seat, in his own words, and when it started.** "I had never, I believe, except in
  rare instances, until I went to Rochester, used as a means of promoting revivals, what has since
  been called the anxious seat." He explains the reasoning: with "the higher classes especially, the
  greatest obstacle to be overcome was their fear of being known as anxious inquirers. They were too
  proud to take any position that would reveal them to others as anxious for their souls." He wanted
  "something that would commit them publicly to the service of Christ." (*Memoirs*.)
- **Rochester, from September 1830, for six months** — "preaching everyday of the week and three
  times on Sunday, while spending most of his days praying with individuals and leading prayer
  meetings." The anxious bench was developed there. (University of Rochester, Rare Books, Special
  Collections and Preservation, student project on a Finney letter, https://rbscp.lib.rochester.edu/2263 )
- **His methods were called the New Measures and they were controversial** because he "emphasized the
  will of man in the process of regeneration" — that a person could decide to be converted — and
  because the measures "evoked a highly emotional response." His ***Lectures on Revivals* (1835)
  became a handbook for American revivalists.**
- **Oberlin, 1835.** The college was near collapse. The New York abolitionist businessman **Arthur
  Tappan** rescued it on two conditions: **that Finney be made professor of theology, and that
  African Americans be admitted as students on equal terms with whites.** Finney also became pastor
  of the Oberlin church.
- **President of Oberlin 1851-1866**; pastor until 1872; lectured occasionally until his death in
  **1875**. **He was not Oberlin's first president — that was Asa Mahan**, and Oberlin's own alumni
  magazine says people often get this wrong.
- **What he taught about religion and doing something:** LOC quotes him — "The evidence of God's
  grace . . . was a person's benevolence toward others." That is the link between the revivals and
  the reform societies.
- (Sources: Oberlin College, "Remembrances of Charles Finney," *Oberlin Alumni Magazine*, Fall 1998,
  https://www2.oberlin.edu/alummag/oamcurrent/oam_fall98/finney.html ; Oberlin College Library

### 7b. [VERIFY] CLEARED — public money for church schools, and the hostility behind the fight

- **The Blaine Amendment, 1875.** Congressman **James G. Blaine of Maine** proposed a constitutional
  amendment, at President **Ulysses S. Grant's** suggestion: "No money raised by taxation in any state
  for the support of public schools, or derived from any fund therefor, nor any public lands devoted
  thereto, shall ever be under the control of any religious sect; nor shall any money so raised or lands
  so devoted be divided between religious sect or denominations."
- **It passed the House in 1875 and failed in the Senate by four votes. Thirty-seven states later put
  their own version into their state constitutions.**
- **What "sectarian" meant.** The National Constitution Center quotes the finding that the amendment and
  its state copies "arose at a time of pervasive hostility to the Catholic Church and to Catholics in
  general, and it was an open secret that 'sectarian' was code for 'Catholic.'"
  **CAUTION — the origin of that quoted sentence was not pinned down here.** The National Constitution
  Center presents it as a quotation but the consulted page does not name the opinion or author it comes
  from. **The outline therefore states the fact in its own plain words and does not attribute the
  quotation to a court.** An audit pass should trace it (the likely source is a Supreme Court opinion of
  2000 or later on aid to religious schools) before any prose quotes it.
- **What it cost Blaine.** Running for president in **1884**, he did not repudiate a supporter's line
  calling the Democrats the party of "rum, Romanism and rebellion." Historians hold that it cost him
  Irish Catholic votes and the election.
- (National Constitution Center, "Constitution Check: Are the states' 'Blaine Amendments' on shaky

- **The meeting.** **12 January 1865**, at the house of Charles Green in Savannah. Present: Secretary of

  ordained or licensed ministers. **The men chose Garrison Frazier to speak for all of them.**
- **Garrison Frazier: 67 years old, born in Granville County, North Carolina.** He **bought his own

### 8a. [VERIFY] CLEARED — the 1925 case, the Oregon law behind it, and who wrote the law

**Both era-8 [VERIFY] tags on this span are cleared: the case is *Pierce v. Society of Sisters*, decided
1 June 1925, and the state is Oregon, the law the Compulsory Education Act adopted by the voters on
7 November 1922.**

**The law itself, from the Supreme Court's own printing of its text.** Oregon voters amended Section 5259
of the Oregon Laws by initiative on **7 November 1922**, to take effect **1 September 1926**. Any parent,
guardian or other person having control of a child aged eight to sixteen who failed to send that child
**to a public school** was "guilty of a misdemeanor," **each day's failure a separate offense**, punishable
by "a fine of not less than $5, nor more than $100, or to imprisonment in the county jail not less than
two nor more than thirty days, or by both such fine and imprisonment." The exemptions were for a child
who was "abnormal, subnormal or physically unable to attend school," a child who had finished the eighth
grade, a child living more than one and a half miles (under ten) or three miles (over ten) from a public
school, and a child taught by a parent or private teacher — and that last one only with **written
permission from the county superintendent**, renewable each year, with the child reporting for an
examination every three months. (*Pierce v. Society of Sisters*, 268 U.S. 510 (1925), footnote to the
opinion at 530-531, U.S. Reports scanned by the Library of Congress,
https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep268/usrep268510/usrep268510.pdf )

**Who sued, and what made one of them a religious school.** Two Oregon corporations, and only one of them
was religious. The **Society of Sisters of the Holy Names of Jesus and Mary** was incorporated in Oregon in
**1880** to care for orphans and run schools; the Court's own summary of its schools says "Systematic
religious instruction and moral training according to the tenets of the Roman Catholic Church are also
regularly provided." The other plaintiff, **Hill Military Academy**, incorporated 1908, was not religious
at all: a boys' school of about a hundred pupils paying about eight hundred dollars a year. (Same source,
at 531-533.)

**The decision.** Argued 16 and 17 March 1925, **decided 1 June 1925**. The Court held the Act
"unreasonably interferes with the liberty of parents and guardians to direct the upbringing and education
of children under their control." (Same source, at 510 and 534-535.)

**Who wrote the law, which is this chapter's angle.** The initiative was **proposed on 26 June 1922** and
**put on the ballot by the Ku Klux Klan through the Scottish Rite Masons**, and the appeal was backed by
the **National Catholic Welfare Conference**. The vote was close: **219,191 votes cast, a margin of
11,821** — that is **115,506 for and 103,685 against**. (The Catholic University of America, "Catholics and
Education: The Oregon School Case," https://guides.lib.cua.edu/c.php?g=1404150&p=10408573 )

**What this chapter tells and what it must not retell.** `outlines/education.md:122-127` already tells
*Pierce* as school law and quotes "The child is not the mere creature of the state";
`outlines/government-politics.md:11` quotes the same line as constitutional law. **Do not quote that line
a third time.** This chapter's slice is the religion: a state where a majority voted to close Catholic
schools, the penalty aimed at the parents rather than the schools, and the Catholic body that paid for
the appeal.

### 8c. The faith argument at Dayton, in the words used in the courtroom

**What this chapter adds that the others do not.** `outlines/government-politics.md:12` already carries
the Butler Act's signing on 21 March 1925, the trial dates, the $100 fine, the 1927 reversal on a
technicality, and the 1967 repeal. `education` owns what a school may teach. **Do not retell any of
that.** This chapter's slice is the religious argument itself, and both sides put it on the record.

**The prosecution's religious argument — William Jennings Bryan's own words.** On the seventh day of the
trial, **Monday 20 July 1925**, Bryan took the witness stand and Clarence Darrow questioned him about the
Bible. Bryan: "I accept the Bible absolutely." When the state objected and asked what the questioning was
for, Bryan answered: "The purpose is to cast ridicule on everybody who believes in the Bible, and I am
perfectly willing that the world shall know that these gentlemen have no other purpose than ridiculing
every Christian who believes in the Bible." Darrow answered: "We have the purpose of preventing bigots
and ignoramuses from controlling the education of the United States and you know it, and that is all."
(*The World's Most Famous Court Trial: Tennessee Evolution Case*, complete stenographic report, 1925,
pp. 286 and 299, https://archive.org/details/scopes-transcript )

**Bryan's fuller argument was never spoken in court.** Both sides gave up their closing speeches, so the
argument Bryan had written was printed as a supplement to the trial report instead. In it he put the case
as a parents' case: "The parents of Tennessee have reason enough to fear the effect of evolution on the
minds of their children," and "Can the fathers and mothers of Tennessee be blamed for trying to protect
their children from such a tragedy?" The tragedy he named was losing "faith in God, trust in the Bible,
and belief in the supernatural character of Christ." (Same volume, supplement, "Bryan on Evolution,"
p. 329.) **Say plainly that this was written and printed but not delivered.**

**The other religious argument was also in the record, and it was made by Christians.** The defence could
not get its expert witnesses in front of the jury, so its lawyer Arthur Garfield Hays read their written
statements into the record and had them filed. Two of them argue about God, not only about biology.

- **Kirtley F. Mather**, chairman of the geology department at Harvard and a graduate of Denison
  University, a Baptist college: "Evolution is not a power, nor a force; it is a process, a method. God is
  a power, a force; he necessarily uses processes and methods in displaying His power and exerting force."
  He also claimed the right of interpretation for himself — "as does every Christian, be he literalist,
  trivialist or modernist." (Same volume, statement of Kirtley F. Mather, pp. 241 onward.)
- **Maynard M. Metcalf**, the zoologist who was the one expert allowed to testify before the jury was sent
  out: "There is no conflict, no least degree of conflict, between the Bible and the fact of evolution, but
  the literalist intepretation of the words of the Bible is not only puerile; it is insulting, both to God
  and to human intelligence." And of the fundamentalist: "He is in reality, although he doesn't realize
  this, trying to shut man's mind to God's ever-growing revelation of Himself to the human soul."
  (Same volume, statement of Maynard M. Metcalf, pp. 247 onward. The spelling "intepretation" is the
  printed report's own; quote it as printed or fix it silently, but do not change the wording.)

**The teaching point for this chapter:** the Scopes trial was not religion against science. It was one
group of Christians against another group of Christians, arguing about how to read Genesis, with the
public school as the ground they fought on.

### 8d. [VERIFY] CLEARED — Azusa Street, 1906, and what a Pentecostal meeting was

**The [VERIFY] on "Pentecostalism from the Azusa Street revival, 1906" is cleared, with one date left
open on purpose (see the disagreement below).**

**The place.** 312 Azusa Street, Los Angeles. The building had been put up as an African Methodist
Episcopal church founded by **Biddy Mason (1818-1891)**, who had been enslaved and won her freedom in a
California court. By 1906 it was no longer a church: it had been used as **a tenement and a livery
stable**. **William J. Seymour (1870-1922)** rented it and ran his Apostolic Faith Mission there from
**1906 to 1931**. The University of Washington's architecture database calls it "one of the first
religious congregations in the U.S. that consisted of black and white parishioners together under a
African-American pastor." The building is gone; the site is now the Japanese American Community Cultural
Center Plaza, with a plaque reading "Site of the Azusa St. Revival from 1906 to 1931. Cradle of the
Worldwide Pentecostal Movement." (Pacific Coast Architecture Database, University of Washington
Libraries, https://pcad.lib.washington.edu/building/3605 )

**The starting date.** The first speaking in tongues came at a house at **214 North Bonnie Brae Street on
9 April 1906**, after a month of prayer and fasting; the meetings moved to Azusa Street in **mid-April
1906**. (*Christian History* magazine, Christian History Institute, "Pentecostalism: William Seymour,"
https://christianhistoryinstitute.org/magazine/article/pentecostalism-seymour — **a history magazine, not
an archive; labelled as such**.)

**What Pentecostal means, in plain words, for the book.** These were Christians who held that the events
of the Bible's Day of Pentecost happen now: that God fills a believer with the Holy Spirit, and that
speaking in tongues — speaking sounds the speaker has not learned and does not understand — is the proof
it has happened. That teaching is what Seymour had learned from **Charles Fox Parham** in Houston.

**Where the record disagrees, and the outline must not resolve it.** Sources give **January 1906**
(Association of Religion Data Archives, Pennsylvania State University, US Religion timeline entry 211),
**early 1906** (*Christian History*) and **22 February 1906** (repeated in several accounts) for Seymour's
arrival in Los Angeles. **Write "early 1906" and do not pick a day.**

### 8g. Native ceremonies were still punished, inside living memory — the era-8 continuation of §7d

**This is the Native-continuity-past-1900 thread the brief asks for, and it is a fact about law, not about
belief.** Era 7 (§7d) gives the 1883 *Rules Governing the Court of Indian Offenses*. It did not stop in
1900. What follows is era 8.

- **Money withheld for dancing, with names, 1916-17.** At the Kiowa Agency in Oklahoma, Superintendent
  **C. V. Stinchecum** ruled that a man would not "receive his money until he has given his word of honor
  to the Superintendent that he will not again indulge in either a ghost dance or a give-away dance at or
  near the Kiowa reservation." By early December 1916 he had **withheld the 4 percent trust funds of about
  ninety people**; in **early January 1917** he sent the Indian Office **a list of 109 names** whose money
  he had held back that autumn. The named Kiowas on the list include **Humming Bird, Kiowa Bill, Adongky
  (White Buffalo), Konad (White Buffalo), Henry Poolaw, George Mopope, Bert Geigaumah (Crow Lance), White
  Horse, Frank Givens, Red Buffalo, Charlie Buffalo, Silver Horn, Guy Quoetone, Conklin Humming Bird, Max
  Frizzlehead, Glen White Fox, Edgar Keabone, Lewis Toyebo, Little Joe** and **Kiowa Jim Tongkeamha
  (Comes Out Of Water)**. To get their own money they had to sign an affidavit promising not to dance.
- **A second instance, 1921.** After a Fourth of July dance near Anadarko at which eleven horses were
  given away, Stinchecum cited **nine Kiowa men** for "the reckless giving away of property" and held
  their 4 percent annuity funds until they signed an affidavit promising never to do it again. **By early
  November only three had signed.**
- **The federal order behind it.** **Circular 1665, "Indian Dancing," issued 26 April 1921 by Commissioner
  of Indian Affairs Charles H. Burke**, let agency superintendents suppress any dance they judged immoral
  or against the community's interest. Burke issued a **Supplement to Circular No. 1665 on 14 February
  1923** and a message **"To All Indians" on 24 February 1923.**
- (Benjamin R. Kracht, "War Dancing, Tourism, and the Rise of Powwows in the Early Twentieth Century,"
  *The Chronicles of Oklahoma* 90:3 (2012), Oklahoma Historical Society, at pp. 288-292 and notes 12,
  22-24, 34-38, https://gateway.okhistory.org/ark:/67531/metadc2017337/m2/1/high_res_d/2012-v90-n03_COO_Kracht.pdf )
- **When it changed, and by how much.** **John Collier became Commissioner of Indian Affairs in April
  1933.** Secretary of the Interior **Harold Ickes** approved Collier's **BIA Circular 2970, "Indian
  Religious Freedom and Indian Culture,"** which went to every agency and said **"no interference with
  Indian religious life or ceremonial expression will hereafter be tolerated."** It was **the federal
  government's first policy statement protecting Native religious rights.** (Lee Irwin, "Freedom, Law,
  and Prophecy: A Brief History of Native American Religious Resistance," at 6-7,
  http://sacredland.org/wp-content/PDFs/Irwin.pdf ; the National Library of Medicine's *Native Voices*
  timeline confirms that Collier issued a circular on American Indian religious freedom in 1934 addressing
  "local and regional bans on traditional ceremonies and practices,"
  https://www.nlm.nih.gov/nativevoices/timeline/453.html ; Kracht records that suppression at the Kiowa
  Agency "ceased shortly after John Collier became Indian commissioner in 1933.")
- **Do not write that this ended the matter.** The 1883 code's restrictions "remained legally enforceable
  until Congress enacted the American Indian Religious Freedom Act in 1978" (UND, quoted in §7d), and the
  **Bald Eagle Protection Act regulations of 1940** made possessing eagle feathers a federal offence,
  under which traditional practitioners were prosecuted (Irwin, at 7). **Era 9 carries the 1978 Act.**

**The families in *Engel* were religious people, and they were not one religion.** The parents of **ten

**The Schempps were Unitarians, and they went to church.** Edward Lewis Schempp, his wife Sidney and
their children Roger and Donna "are of the Unitarian faith and are members of the Unitarian Church in
Germantown, Philadelphia, Pennsylvania, where they, as well as another son, Ellory, regularly attend

**Why Edward Schempp would not use the excusal, in the trial court's summary of his testimony — this is
the strongest single passage in the record for this chapter's angle.** He decided against having Roger or
Donna excused because he thought they would be "labeled as 'odd balls'" by teachers and classmates every
school day; because classmates were liable "to lump all particular religious difference[s] or religious
objections [together] as 'atheism'"; because in 1962 "atheism" was connected with "atheistic communism"
and carried "very bad" meanings like "un-American"; because the exercises ran straight into the school
announcements, so a child who left would miss them; and because an excused child "would have to stand in
the hall outside their 'homeroom' and that this carried with it the imputation of punishment for bad
conduct." (374 U.S. 203, at 208 n. 3, quoting 201 F. Supp., at 818.)

**Two expert witnesses, one on each side, and what they said about the Bible.**
- **Dr. Solomon Grayzel**, for the families: the most obvious difference between the Jewish scriptures and
  the Christian Bible is that the Jewish scriptures have no New Testament; from the standpoint of Jewish
  faith the idea of Jesus as the Son of God is "practically blasphemous"; parts of the New Testament
  "tended to bring the Jews into ridicule or scorn"; read without explanation those parts had, in his own
  experience with children, been "psychologically harmful to the child."
- **Dr. Luther A. Weigle**, for the school district, said on direct examination "that the Bible was
  non-sectarian." **"He later stated that the phrase 'non-sectarian' meant to him non-sectarian within
  the Christian faiths."** (374 U.S. 203, at 209-210.)
- **That exchange is the teaching point of the whole span** and it is in the Court's own record.

**The Murray case, and what atheism meant in 1963.** Baltimore's rule, dating from 1905, opened each
school with "reading, without comment, of a chapter in the Holy Bible and/or the use of the Lord's
Prayer," and let pupils use the Douay version if they preferred. **Mrs. Madalyn Murray and her son
William J. Murray III "are both professed atheists."** They got the rule amended so a child could be
excused, William was excused, and they carried on with the case anyway, arguing the rule "threatens their
religious liberty by placing a premium on belief as against non-belief." (374 U.S. 203, at 211-212.)

### 9c. [VERIFY] CLEARED — what schools did instead: released time, a moment of silence, student-led prayer

- **RELEASED TIME — two cases, four years apart, opposite results.**
  - ***Illinois ex rel. McCollum v. Board of Education*, decided 8 March 1948** (era 8 by date). In
    Champaign, Illinois, a private group called the **Champaign Council on Religious Education**,
    including Catholic, Protestant and Jewish teachers, came into the public school buildings **once a
    week** and taught religion for **thirty minutes in the lower grades and forty-five in the higher**.

    **Vashti McCollum**, a resident, taxpayer and the mother of a pupil, sued. The Court held that using
    the public schools' buildings and their compulsory-attendance machinery to let religious groups teach
    religion violated the First Amendment. (333 U.S. 203, syllabus and at 207-212,
    https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep333/usrep333203/usrep333203.pdf )
  - ***Zorach v. Clauson*, decided 28 April 1952.** New York City let pupils **leave the school buildings
    and grounds**, on their parents' written request, and go to religious centres for instruction; the
    churches reported back to the schools the names of released children who failed to show up. **The
    Court upheld it**, distinguishing *McCollum*, because the programme "involves neither religious
    instruction in public schools nor the expenditure of public funds." (343 U.S. 306, syllabus,
    https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep343/usrep343306/usrep343306.pdf )

    children released to go elsewhere is in.** That is a plain thing to explain to a child.
- **A MOMENT OF SILENCE — *Wallace v. Jaffree*, decided 4 June 1985.** Alabama passed three laws in a
  row: **section 16-1-20 in 1978**, a one-minute silence "for meditation"; **section 16-1-20.1 in 1981**,
  a silence "for meditation or voluntary prayer"; and **section 16-1-20.2 in 1982**, which let teachers
  lead "willing students" in a set prayer to "Almighty God . . . the Creator and Supreme Judge of the
  world." The Court struck down the 1981 law because its purpose was to endorse religion. **It did not
  strike down the 1978 law providing plain silence for meditation.** (472 U.S. 38, syllabus and at 40,

**EDWARDS v. AGUILLARD — the facts, from the opinion.**
- The Louisiana law at issue was the **"Balanced Treatment for Creation-Science and Evolution-Science in
  Public School Instruction" Act**, and it "forbids the teaching of the theory of evolution in public
  elementary and secondary schools unless accompanied by instruction in the theory of 'creation
  science.'" Neither had to be taught; but neither could be taught alone. (482 U.S. 578, syllabus.)
- **Who sued is this chapter's point.** The people who brought the case "include parents of children
  attending Louisiana public schools, Louisiana teachers, and religious leaders." (482 U.S. 578, at
  581.) **Religious people were on both sides of this case, and the record shows it on its face.**
- **The friend-of-the-court briefs, listed in the opinion's own footnote, are the clearest evidence of
  that split.** Urging that the law be upheld: the **Catholic League for Religious and Civil Rights**,
  the **Christian Legal Society**, and **Concerned Women for America**. Urging that it be struck down:
  the **New York Committee for Public Education and Religious Liberty**, People for the American Way,
  the American Association of University Professors, the American Federation of Teachers, the State of
  New York and other states, and **72 Nobel Laureates**. Separate briefs came from the **Rabbinical

- **What the Court held about the law's purpose:** the Act "impermissibly endorses religion by advancing
  the religious belief that a supernatural being created humankind," and "The preeminent purpose of the

  humankind." (Syllabus 1(b), and at 591.)

  merely a matter of personal preference, but one of deep religious conviction, shared by an organized
  group, and intimately related to daily living," and it comes "in response to their literal
  interpretation of the Biblical injunction from the Epistle of Paul to the Romans, 'be not conformed to
  this world . . . .' This command is fundamental to the Amish faith." (406 U.S. 205, at 216.)
- **What the objection actually was.** They did not object to the first eight grades, because children
  need the basic skills "in order to read the Bible, to be good farmers and citizens, and to be able to
  deal with non-Amish people." They objected to high school, which they saw as "an impermissible exposure
  of their children to a 'worldly' influence." (406 U.S. 205, at 210-211.)
- **The expert witness.** **Dr. John Hostetler**, an authority on Amish society, testified that
  compulsory high school could cause great psychological harm to Amish children and would in his opinion
  end in "the destruction of the Old Order Amish church community as it exists in the United States
  today." (406 U.S. 205, at 212.)
- **THE PART NO OTHER CHAPTER HAS, AND THE REASON THIS BELONGS TO RELIGION. One of the children
  testified, and Justice Douglas dissented in part over the other two.** The Court's own footnote 21:
  "The only relevant testimony in the record is to the effect that the wishes of the one child who
  testified corresponded with those of her parents. Testimony of Frieda Yoder, Tr. 92-94, to the effect
  that her personal religious beliefs guided her decision to discontinue school attendance after the
  eighth grade. The other children were not called by either side." Justice Douglas: "Frieda Yoder has in
  fact testified that her own religious views are opposed to high-school education. I therefore join the
  judgment of the Court as to respondent Jonas Yoder. But Frieda Yoder's views may not be those of Vernon
  Yutzy or Barbara Miller. I must dissent, therefore, as to respondents Adin Yutzy and Wallace Miller as
  their motion to dismiss also raised the question of their children's religious liberty." (406 U.S. 205,
  at 237 n. 21, and at 241-242, Douglas, J., dissenting in part.)
- **Douglas's question in plain words, for the writer: when a family's religion decides a child's
  schooling, is it the parents' religion or the child's? He said the Court had never squarely faced it.**

- **The church was built in 1926** in Collegeville, a Black working-class neighbourhood six miles north
  of the centre of Birmingham. **Fred L. Shuttlesworth was its pastor from March 1953.**
- **Why a church body had to do this work.** An Alabama circuit court **banned the NAACP from operating
  in the state on 1 June 1956.** Shuttlesworth and other ministers drew up a declaration on **2 June**
  and founded the **Alabama Christian Movement for Human Rights at Sardis Baptist Church on 5 June
  1956**, at a meeting of about **a thousand people**. The Encyclopedia of Alabama records his reasoning
  for the name: officials could outlaw an organisation but not a movement, and the word "Christian" both
  set it apart from groups being called Communist and gave it "a foundation and Christian orientation."
  **Bethel Baptist Church was the ACMHR's headquarters from 1956 to 1961.**
- **What the meetings were.** Weekly mass meetings **every Monday night**, led by Shuttlesworth at
  churches around Birmingham, with, in the National Park Service's words, "emotional testimony,
  soul-lifting music, impassioned preaching, and fundraising." The Encyclopedia of Alabama records
  several hundred people at them routinely, and roughly **1,000 members in 1959, 60 per cent of them
  women**, most of them working people. The NHL nomination adds that by 1958 weekly meetings were also
  being held at **55 other working-class churches** in the city.
- **THE FIRST BOMB. On the night of 25 December 1956 — Christmas night — twelve sticks of dynamite
  exploded in the space between the church and the parsonage.** The parsonage was destroyed. Shuttlesworth
  was inside talking with a church deacon and his family was watching television. The blast "shattered
  windows, caved in the roof of the living room and wrecked furnishings," and Shuttlesworth was among the
  injured, with a minor head injury. The bomb was set the night before the ACMHR had announced its
  members would ride the segregated buses; **250 people rode them the next day and 22 were arrested.**
  (NHL nomination, pages 13 and 4; the nomination cites "Bombing in Alabama Injures 5, Wrecks Minister's
  Home," *New York Times*, 26 December 1956, page 1.)
- **THE SECOND BOMB, AND THE GUARDS. At about 1.30 in the morning on Sunday 29 June 1958, Bethel Church
  guards carried a smoking explosive away from the side of the church and put it in the street, where it
  went off**, blowing a hole in the road and out some church windows without damaging the structure of
  the building. Birmingham police went to the house of **James Revis**, a Bethel deacon living across the
  street, and took four rifles from the guards on his porch. The nomination records that after the
  Christmas 1956 bomb the ACMHR guards had been guarding the church with what they called their
  "nonviolent Winchesters." **A third bomb in 1962 shattered the church windows.** The 1958 bombing was
  not solved until **1980**, when **J. B. Stoner** was convicted in *Alabama v. Stoner*.
- **THE GUARDHOUSE — this is the ordinary-people material, and it comes from recorded oral history.**
  After the 1956 bomb destroyed the old parsonage, ACMHR and Bethel church members volunteered to guard
  the church and the new parsonage being built next to the Revis house. **James Revis's family closed in
  their own front porch in 1957 so the guards could watch from it.** His daughter **Laverne Revis
  Martin**: "[m]y parents had a little room that they closed out on the front of the porch just so the
  guards would be able to watch." Her older sister **Robbie Revis Smith**: "they decided that they needed
  to close in the front porch so they could see better . . . with our house sitting on the corner, it
  gave a very good view several ways that they could see because they had the glass out there and they
  put a little gas heater out there so they wouldn't be too cold sitting up. They did this year round."
  The volunteers were called the **Civil Rights Guards**, and they worked **shifts of 6 p.m. to midnight
  and midnight to 6 a.m.** Robbie Revis Smith on what that was like to come home to: "So it was kind of
  a good feeling coming home because you never came home without a person out there watching you, so you
  didn't have to worry about that." **Laverne Revis Martin also recorded that women in the movement took
  a weekly shift in the guardhouse**, among them **Desta Brooks**, a florist on the movement's executive
  committee alongside James Revis, and **Minnie Eaton**, a schoolteacher. Guards were also posted in a
  shack behind the parsonage to watch the back of the property.
  **It was Laverne who spotted the second bomb, coming home from work, and alerted the guards.**
  (Interviews: Laverne Revis Martin by Dr. Horace Huntley of the Birmingham Civil Rights Institute, **17

### 10c. [VERIFY] CLEARED — the 2022 ruling on a coach praying at a school game

***Kennedy v. Bremerton School District*, 597 U.S. 507, argued 25 April 2022 and decided 27 June 2022.**
Read in full from https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/21pdf/21-418_i425.pdf

- **Joseph Kennedy began coaching football at Bremerton High School in Washington State in 2008**, after
  nearly two decades in the Marine Corps. He gave thanks by prayer on the field at the end of each game,
  after the players and coaches had shaken hands, by taking a knee at the 50-yard line and praying for
  about thirty seconds. (597 U.S. 507, slip op. at 2.)
- **At first he prayed alone.** Over time players asked whether they could pray with him and he said,
  "This is a free country. You can do what you want." The number who joined him grew to most of the team
  after some games; sometimes players from the other team were invited; sometimes he still prayed alone.
  He later began adding short motivational speeches to the prayer when others were there. Separately, the
  team sometimes prayed in the locker room, which the opinion calls a school tradition older than his
  time there. (Slip op. at 2-3.)
- **Nobody complained for more than seven years.** The district's superintendent learned of it only in
  **September 2015**, after an employee of another school praised the practice to Bremerton's principal.
  The district wrote to him on **17 September 2015.** (Slip op. at 3.)
- **The three games.** After a letter of **16 October** forbidding "any overt actions" that could appear
  to endorse prayer while on duty, he prayed after the games of **16, 23 and 26 October 2015**. On 16
  October most Bremerton players were singing the school fight song; players from the other team and
  members of the public joined him before he finished. On 23 October "no one joined him." On 26 October
  other adults gathered around him while he knelt. **After the 26 October game the district put him on
  paid administrative leave** and barred him from taking any part in the football programme, in a letter
  criticising "public and demonstrative religious conduct while still on duty as an assistant coach."
  (Slip op. at 4-7.)
- **The Court ruled for him, six to three.** Justice Gorsuch wrote the opinion, joined by Roberts,
  Thomas, Alito and Barrett, and by Kavanaugh except as to one part. Held: "The Free Exercise and Free
  Speech Clauses of the First Amendment protect an individual engaging in a personal religious
  observance from government reprisal."
- **THE DISPUTE IS PART OF THE FACT, AND THE BOOK MUST SAY SO.** Justice Sotomayor, dissenting with
  Breyer and Kagan: "To the degree the Court portrays petitioner Joseph Kennedy's prayers as private and
  quiet, it misconstrues the facts. The record reveals that Kennedy had a longstanding practice of
  conducting demonstrative prayers on the 50-yard line of the football field. Kennedy consistently
  invited others to join his prayers and for years led student athletes in prayer at the same time and
  location." She also wrote that the case "is about whether a public school must permit a school
