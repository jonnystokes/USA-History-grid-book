## 1. Before the 1500s

- No overseas immigration to stay yet. The land was already home to hundreds of Native nations speaking hundreds of languages. Immigration here means newcomers arriving into an inhabited continent, not an empty one.
- How many people lived north of Mexico in 1492 is debated, and the range is wide. Geographer William Denevan's often-cited consensus figure is about 3.8 million for North America north of Mexico (part of a total for the Americas of about 54 million, in a range of roughly 43 to 65 million). An older figure (1978 *Atlas of World Population History*) put only about 1 million north of the Rio Grande; some later "high counters" argued for numbers up to about 18 to 20 million. Historian Donald Fixico gives a range of a few million to 15 million. There is no settled number. (Sources: Denevan, *The Native Population of the Americas in 1492*, Univ. of Wisconsin Press; historian Donald L. Fixico.)
- The first people to come to this land came on foot, long ago, across the ice-age land bridge from Asia. That first peopling was the original arrival. (Told in full in Ch1 Exploration; noted here only in brief, no cross-reference in prose.)
- The one earlier arrival from across the ocean, the Norse around the year 1000, did not stay. They built a short-lived camp at L'Anse aux Meadows in Newfoundland, stayed about ten years, and left. No lasting settlement. (Detail in Ch1.)
- No featured story in this section (named immigrants come later).

## 2. The 1500s

- Europeans arrived, but almost none came to stay. Most were soldiers, explorers, sailors, and missionaries — this was conquest and exploration, not settlement. (The journeys themselves are Ch1.)
- The land stayed overwhelmingly Native throughout the century.
- **St. Augustine, Florida, founded September 8, 1565**, by the Spanish admiral Pedro Menéndez de Avilés, who landed with about 600 soldiers and settlers. It is the oldest continuously occupied settlement of European origin in the continental United States — founded 42 years before Jamestown and 55 years before Plymouth. Menéndez named it for Saint Augustine, on whose feast day he first sighted land. (Sources: National Park Service; City of St. Augustine; Britannica.)
- Failed attempts to stay show how hard settlement was:
  - **Fort Caroline** (1564), a French Huguenot (Protestant) settlement near present-day Jacksonville, founded by René de Laudonnière. Menéndez destroyed it in 1565 and killed most of the French defenders. This was partly a religious war carried across the ocean. (Sources: NPS; Britannica.)
  - **Roanoke, the "Lost Colony"** (1587), an English settlement on Roanoke Island off present-day North Carolina, organized under Sir Walter Raleigh and led by John White. Eleanor Dare's daughter Virginia Dare, born there August 18, 1587, is considered the first English child born in the Americas. War with Spain delayed White's return with supplies until 1590, when he found the colony gone and the word "CROATOAN" carved on a post. The fate of the settlers is unknown. (Sources: NPS Fort Raleigh; Britannica.)
- No featured story (kept as connective history).

## 3. The 1600s — the first people who came to STAY

### English settlement begins
- **Jamestown, Virginia, 1607** — the first permanent English settlement. Funded by the Virginia Company of London, a joint-stock company, for profit. Early years were deadly (disease, hunger, conflict with the Powhatan). Tobacco, grown for export after John Rolfe's 1610s experiments, made the colony pay and pulled in more settlers. (Sources: NPS Historic Jamestowne; Britannica.)
- **Plymouth, 1620** — the Pilgrims, English Separatists who wanted to worship apart from the Church of England. About 102 passengers crossed on the *Mayflower*. They had first moved to Leiden, in Holland, before deciding to cross the Atlantic. (Sources: Britannica; NPS.)
- **Massachusetts Bay, 1630** — Puritans led by John Winthrop. The Winthrop Fleet of 1630 was 11 ships (flagship *Arbella*) carrying about 700 passengers. This began the "Great Migration": roughly 20,000 English Puritans came to New England over the 1630s, driven partly by religious pressure after Charles I made William Laud Archbishop of Canterbury in 1633. (Sources: New England Historical Society; history-of-massachusetts sources. Note: about 80,000 left England in the 1630s for all destinations — Ireland, the West Indies, the Netherlands, and New England — roughly 20,000 to each, so the New England figure is one-quarter of that outflow.)
- **Dutch and Swedish:**
  - New Netherland, with its capital New Amsterdam on Manhattan (settled 1620s). The English took it in 1664: four English frigates arrived, Governor Peter Stuyvesant surrendered without a fight (surrender articles signed late August 1664), and it was renamed **New York** after the Duke of York. (Sources: Gilder Lehrman; History.com; LOC.)
  - **New Sweden** (1638–1655), a small Swedish and Finnish colony along the Delaware River. The Dutch absorbed it in 1655. (Source: encyclopedic references; verify further if used prominently.)
- **Why they came:** religious freedom (Pilgrims, Puritans, later Quakers under William Penn in Pennsylvania from 1681, and Catholics in Maryland from 1634) and economic opportunity (land, trade, tobacco).

### Indentured servitude (how most English arrivals came)
- Most early English arrivals to the Chesapeake (Virginia, Maryland) came as indentured servants: they signed away four to seven years of labor in exchange for ship's passage, and often food, lodging, and "freedom dues" at the end. Indentured servants made up about **80 to 90 percent of European immigrants to the Chesapeake in the 1600s** (Smithsonian; EH.net). Across British North America over the whole colonial period, roughly **one-half to two-thirds** of white immigrants arrived as indentured servants (Encyclopedia.com; EH.net). Framing note: the two figures measure different areas — Chesapeake alone vs. all the colonies.

### Forced migration — arrival angle (the injustice angle is Ch17)
- **First documented Africans in English Virginia, 1619.** An English privateer, the *White Lion*, brought "20. and odd Negroes" (the words of colonist John Rolfe) to Point Comfort (Old Point Comfort), near the mouth of the James River, in late August 1619. (Sources: NPS; National Geographic; Hampton, VA historian Beth Austin.)
- **Where they came from and how they got there:** these people were captured in West Central Africa, in the wars around the Kingdom of Kongo and Ndongo (present-day Angola), marched to the Portuguese port of Luanda, and loaded aboard the Portuguese ship *San Juan Bautista*, which carried about 350 captives bound for Veracruz, Mexico. In the Gulf of Mexico two English privateers, the *White Lion* and the *Treasurer*, attacked the *San Juan Bautista* and seized 50 to 60 Africans between them; the *White Lion* carried its share to Virginia. (Sources: NPS; National Geographic.)
- **Date disagreement:** NPS and many accounts say "late August 1619"; History.com gives August 20, 1619; Hampton historian Beth Austin argues the *White Lion* arrived about August 25, 1619. State the range, not a single day.
- This 1619 landing began a forced migration of Africans into the mainland. (Whether these first arrivals were treated as servants or as slaves in 1619 is debated — that legal/status question belongs to Ch17. Here the point is the arrival: who came, from where, on what ship.)

## 4. 1700 to 1750

### The colonial population diversifies beyond the English
- **Germans:** from the Rhineland (the Palatinate) and southwest Germany, fleeing war, taxes, and crop failures and pulled by cheap land. About 2,100 "Palatines" reached New York in 1709–1710. The larger flow went to Pennsylvania from about 1725 to 1775; by 1749 roughly 12,000 German speakers had come to Pennsylvania, and by the Revolution Germans were about one-third of the colony's people. ("Pennsylvania Dutch" comes from *Deutsch*, meaning German.) (Sources: Ancestry; PA ancestry timelines; Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia.)
- **Scots-Irish** (Ulster Presbyterians, descendants of Scots settled in northern Ireland): about 200,000 emigrated to the future United States between 1710 and 1775, fleeing rents and religious restrictions. Pennsylvania was the main gateway; many then moved down the Appalachian backcountry. (Sources: Discover Ulster-Scots; Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia.)
- **French Huguenots** (Protestants) fled after King Louis XIV revoked the Edict of Nantes in 1685. Most went to other European countries; only about 1,500 to 2,000 reached the American colonies by 1700, settling in places such as New York, Massachusetts, and Charleston, South Carolina. (Sources: Britannica; South Carolina Historical Society; Huguenot Society of America.)
- **Small Jewish communities** (mostly Sephardic, of Spanish and Portuguese descent): the first group arrived at New Amsterdam in 1654 — 23 refugees from Recife, Brazil, aboard the *Ste. Catherine*, after Portugal retook Dutch Brazil, plus Jacob Barsimson who came earlier that year. Governor Stuyvesant tried to expel them; the Dutch West India Company overruled him. Communities followed at Newport (Rhode Island), Charleston, New York, and Savannah, Georgia (42 Jews landed at Savannah on July 11, 1733). (Sources: Jewish Women's Archive; New Georgia Encyclopedia; Gilder Lehrman.)
- **Convict transportation:** after Britain's Transportation Act of 1718, about **50,000 convicts** (some counts say up to 60,000) were shipped to the American colonies and sold as bound labor, mostly for seven years, until the practice ended in 1775. They were roughly a quarter of British emigrants that century. (Sources: NPR; Encyclopedia Virginia; earlyamericancrime.com.)
- Indentured servitude continued alongside all of this.

### Forced migration grows — arrival angle
- The transatlantic slave trade to the mainland rose sharply, above all with the **South Carolina Lowcountry rice economy**, which imported large numbers of West and Central Africans (some chosen by planters for rice-growing knowledge).
- **Charleston, South Carolina, was the main mainland port of entry** for enslaved Africans. **Sullivan's Island**, at the harbor mouth, was the quarantine station where arriving Africans were held. In 1974 historian Peter Wood suggested it "might well be described as the Ellis Island of black Americans." Estimates say roughly 40 percent of African Americans today can trace an ancestor through the Charleston/Sullivan's Island gateway, and that not fewer than about 200,000 Africans (perhaps far more) passed through Charleston harbor. Some historians say these figures have been overstated. (Sources: National Parks Traveler; Charleston County Public Library; *Post and Courier*.)
- **African origin regions** for the mainland trade: Senegambia, the Windward Coast, the Gold Coast, the Bight of Biafra, and West Central Africa (Kongo/Angola).
- **Scale (whole transatlantic trade, for context):** of about **12.5 million Africans embarked** and about **10.7 million who survived the Middle Passage**, fewer than 4 percent came to mainland North America — roughly **305,000 landed directly from Africa, and close to 388,000 in total** counting those brought by way of the Caribbean. Most enslaved Africans went to the Caribbean and Brazil. (Source: SlaveVoyages / Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade Database.)

### State of the land
- Colonists were still a thin strip along the Atlantic. The interior remained Native land.

## 5. 1750 to 1800

- **The Revolution (1775–1783) interrupted arrivals.** War slowed immigration across the Atlantic, and convict transportation from Britain ended in 1775. During and after the war tens of thousands of Loyalists left, many for Canada — an outflow, not an arrival.
- **Naturalization Act of 1790** — the first federal law on who could become a citizen. It limited naturalization to "a free white person" of "good character" who had lived in the United States for two years (at least one year in the state of application) and swore allegiance to the Constitution. Children of citizens born abroad were included. This was the first federal statement of who could belong; the "free white persons" limit shaped citizenship by race and was not fully removed until 1952. (Sources: Library of Congress / Constitution Annotated; Mount Vernon; Congress.gov.)
- **Haitian Revolution refugees (1791–1810).** The revolution in the French sugar colony of Saint-Domingue (begun 1791) sent refugees — white planters, free people of color, and enslaved people brought by their owners — to United States cities. Nearly 20,000 came across the 1790s and 1800s. In Philadelphia the number grew from 15 refugees on the *Charming Sally* in 1791 to more than 3,000 by 1794. In New Orleans, about 10,000 refugees arrived in 1809–1810 (by way of Cuba), roughly doubling the city's population. (Sources: Smithsonian; 64 Parishes; Historic New Orleans Collection.)
- **The 1808 clock (arrival angle).** The 1787 Constitution, Article I, Section 9, barred Congress from ending the importation of enslaved people before 1808 — a twenty-year protection for the trade. This set a deadline on legal arrival from Africa (the actual ban is in section 6).
- **State of the land.** The United States was still an Atlantic strip. After the 1783 peace its western border reached the Mississippi River, but Spain and France held the land beyond, and most of the interior was Native land.
- **Story candidate:** a documented Haitian Revolution refugee (needs a specific verified individual).

## 6. 1800 to 1850 — the first great wave

### The end of legal arrival from Africa (arrival angle)
- **The Act Prohibiting Importation of Slaves**, signed by President Thomas Jefferson on March 2, 1807, took effect **January 1, 1808** — the earliest date the Constitution allowed. Legal transatlantic arrival from Africa ended. (Sources: Wikipedia summary of the Act; National Constitution Center.)
- Smuggling continued after 1808 (the *Clotilda* landed captives illegally in 1860 — see section 7). But legal importation was over, and afterward the enslaved population grew mainly by birth within the country.
- Note (do not retell here): the large forced movement of enslaved people in this period was the **domestic** slave trade, from the Upper South to the Deep South. That is forced movement *within* the country → Migration West (Ch3) and Ch17, not the arrival angle of this chapter.

### The first great voluntary wave
- **Irish (the Great Famine, 1845–1852).** A potato blight caused mass starvation; about 1 million people died in Ireland. Nearly 2 million left the country in that decade, most for the United States; more than 1.5 million Irish arrived at U.S. ports between 1845 and 1855. Most were poor and settled in northeastern cities (New York, Boston, and cities in Pennsylvania, Massachusetts, and beyond). (Sources: National Museum of Ireland; academic emigration studies.)
- **Germans.** From about 1840 to 1890 Germans were the largest immigrant group. The 1850s alone brought more than 950,000 German immigrants; 1854 was an early peak with about 220,000 arriving. Push factors: crop failures and, for some, the failed democratic revolutions of 1848. Pull: farmland, especially in the Midwest, where roughly three-fifths settled to farm. The political refugees of 1848, the "Forty-Eighters," numbered only about 4,000 to 10,000 but included many journalists, professionals, and future leaders. About 6 million Germans came between 1820 and World War I. (Sources: LOC; Ancestry; German Americans references.)
- **Arrival ports:** New York was the chief port; Boston and New Orleans also received large numbers.

### Reception — nativism begins
- Anti-Irish and anti-Catholic feeling grew. The **Know-Nothing (American) Party** flourished in the 1850s, born of secret anti-immigrant, anti-Catholic orders (members answered questions about the group by saying "I know nothing"). It swept Massachusetts in the 1854 elections but split over slavery and faded by 1860, with many members moving to the new Republican Party. (Sources: Britannica; Smithsonian.)

### Featured person — Carl Schurz (German Forty-Eighter)
> **Who:** a refugee from the failed 1848 German revolution who became a Union general, the first German-born U.S. senator, and Secretary of the Interior.
> **When and where:** born 1829 near Cologne, Prussia; immigrated to the United States in 1852; died 1906.
> **Movie:** none found (no known feature film; leave the Movie line out unless one is confirmed).

- Carl Schurz was born March 2, 1829, in the Rhine Province of Prussia. He joined the democratic revolutions of 1848–1849; when Prussia crushed them he fled to France, then London. He immigrated to the United States in 1852 and settled in Watertown, Wisconsin. He served as a Union general in the Civil War, represented Missouri in the U.S. Senate (1869–1875) as the first German-born senator, and served as Secretary of the Interior under President Hayes (1877–1881). He died May 14, 1906. (Sources: Britannica; American Battlefield Trust; Congressional Bioguide.)
- **Timing note:** Schurz's arrival year (1852) falls just inside the 1850–1900 window, but his story belongs with the German Forty-Eighter wave described here. The writer can place him at the 1848→1852 hinge.

**The Ursuline Convent, Charlestown, Massachusetts, August 11–12, 1834**
- A mob of "largely Protestant workmen of Charlestown" burned the convent school of the Ursuline nuns in Charlestown (today part of Somerville), near Boston, on the night of August 11–12, 1834. Placards before the attack were signed by the "Truckmen of Boston." John R. Buzzell was the self-confessed ringleader. (Source: Wikipedia, "Ursuline Convent riots," citing the Boston Evening Transcript and Mercantile Journal of 1834. Label: encyclopedia summary.)
- Size of the mob: 50 to 60 men at first (Boston Evening Transcript count) or 150 to 200 (Mercantile Journal count). A crowd of about 2,000 people gathered to watch. (Same source; the two newspaper counts are both kept.)
- Who was inside: the nuns, including the Mother Superior, Mary Edmond St. George, and 47 girl students, only six of them Catholic. (Same source.)
- What the mob did: set fire to tar barrels, broke down the doors and windows, ransacked the buildings, and burned them to the ground within an hour or two. They broke open the convent's tomb, where several dead sisters were buried. The source records no deaths. (Same source.)
- What happened to the attackers: police arrested 13 men. Trials began December 2, 1834. Twelve of the 13 were found not guilty (acquitted), including Buzzell. He later wrote that the evidence "was point blank and sufficient to have convicted twenty men, but somehow I proved an alibi." The one man convicted was 16 years old, and Governor John Davis pardoned him. (Same source; Buzzell's trial record: "Trial of John R. Buzzell," reported by Chief Justice Lemuel Shaw, Providence College Digital Commons, https://digitalcommons.providence.edu/trialbuzzell/1/.)
- Payment for the loss: the Massachusetts legislature voted down, by a large majority, the Catholic diocese's request to be paid for the destroyed property. In 1846 the state voted $10,000. The diocese refused it and put the real loss at about $100,000. (Source: Wikipedia, "Ursuline Convent riots.")

**The Philadelphia "Bible Riots," May 6–8 and July 5–7, 1844**
- What set it off: Francis Patrick Kenrick, the Catholic bishop of Philadelphia, objected to public-school teachers making Catholic children read the Protestant King James Bible and sing Protestant hymns. In February 1844 Hugh Clark, a Catholic school director in Kensington, suggested pausing Bible reading in the Kensington schools. Nativists (people who wanted power kept for native-born Protestants) used this to claim Catholics were trying to throw the Bible out of schools. Lewis Levin, a newspaper editor, had founded the nativist American Republican Association in December 1843. (Source: Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia, "Nativist Riots of 1844," Rutgers-Camden, https://philadelphiaencyclopedia.org/essays/nativist-riots-of-1844/.)
- May 6, 1844: the American Republicans held an anti-Catholic rally in Kensington, a mostly Irish Catholic neighborhood. Rain drove it into a market house next to an Irish fire company. Shooting and fighting broke out between the two sides. George Shiffler, an 18-year-old nativist, was shot dead. A second man, apparently a bystander, died, and two more nativists were fatally wounded. (Sources: Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia; EBSCO Research Starters, "Philadelphia Nativist Riots," https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/philadelphia-nativist-riots; Shiffler's age from Wikipedia, "Philadelphia nativist riots.") Who fired the shot that killed Shiffler is not identified in the sources checked.
- May 7–8, 1844: nativist crowds burned Irish Catholic homes, including Hugh Clark's house, the Hibernia fire station, a market, a Catholic seminary of the Sisters of Charity, St. Michael's Church (Second and Master Streets), and St. Augustine's Church (Fourth and Vine Streets) with its school. Wikipedia counts about 30 homes burned and $150,000 in damage; about 200 people fled their homes. The Library of Congress holds a print of the ruins of St. Augustine's (https://www.loc.gov/item/2021670230/). (Sources: Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia; Wikipedia, "Philadelphia nativist riots.")
- May deaths and injuries, as each source counts them: at least 14 killed and about 50 injured (Wikipedia); 20 dead and at least 100 wounded (EBSCO). The Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia names Shiffler, a bystander and two mortally wounded nativists and gives no total. Joseph Rice is one named Catholic killed (Wikipedia). The total is not settled.
- July 5–7, 1844, Southwark: nativist crowds gathered at the Catholic Church of St. Philip de Neri after learning that the priest's brother had stored guns in its basement for defense. The crowd brought a cannon. State militia under General George Cadwalader fired on the crowd, which attacked the soldiers with bricks, stones, bottles and guns. By the next morning four militiamen and "probably a dozen rioters" were dead (Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia). Other counts: "at least fifteen" killed and at least 50 injured (Wikipedia); two soldiers and at least 12 rioters killed and 26 wounded (EBSCO).
- Whole-year totals: EBSCO's figures add to about 34 dead and at least 126 wounded across both riots. No source checked gives an agreed total.
- What happened to the attackers: a Philadelphia grand jury indicted 19 rioters, and "only a handful were convicted" (Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia, "Grand Juries," https://philadelphiaencyclopedia.org/essays/grand-juries/). The June 1844 grand jury blamed the May riots on "the efforts of a portion of the community to exclude the Bible from the public schools," meaning the Catholics who had been attacked (Villanova University Falvey Library exhibit, "Chaos in the Streets: The Philadelphia Riots of 1844 — Aftermath," https://exhibits.library.villanova.edu/chaos-in-the-streets-the-philadelphia-riots-of-1844/aftermath). Catholics answered that the grand jury called no Catholics to testify (same Villanova page). The exact number convicted and their sentences are not stated in any source checked.
- Payment: the Augustinian friars sued for the loss of St. Augustine's. The award is recorded differently: $45,000 of $80,000 claimed (Wikipedia, "Philadelphia nativist riots"); $27,000, granted December 1847, in a suit against Philadelphia County (Wikipedia, "St. Augustine Church (Philadelphia)"). Both figures kept; not settled.
- What followed: in the October 1844 elections, with the heaviest turnout in the city's history, Philadelphia voters elected Lewis Levin and another nativist to Congress. In 1845 the Pennsylvania legislature required at least one police officer for every 150 taxpaying residents, a step toward Philadelphia's first unified police force in 1850. (Source: Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia.)

### PATCH 2026-09-26 (T-238): The Irish Famine, 1845–1852: the human causes, the deaths, the leaving
*Hard-subject patch. Why the Irish arrived in such numbers. The famine in Ireland is background for this chapter; its arrival side is the point.*

**CORRECTION NOTE (T-238) on the existing line above, "A potato blight caused mass starvation."** The blight is only half the cause as historians state it. Written alone, that line is lossy summarization (policy §2, move 7): it leaves out the people who decided what happened next. Use the facts below with it. The existing line is not deleted (append-only task).

- The crop disease: a water mold, *Phytophthora infestans*, rotted the potato crop. It first appeared in the eastern United States in the summer of 1843, reached Belgium in cargo shipments, and spread across Europe by the summer of 1845. At least two-thirds of Ireland's people, the farm laborers and "cottiers" (tenants renting a tiny plot), lived mainly on potatoes. (Source: Ireland's Great Hunger Museum, Quinnipiac University, https://www.ighm.org/learn.html.)
- Food leaving Ireland: food kept going out of Ireland to Britain while people starved. The historian Christine Kinealy counted almost 4,000 ships carrying food from Ireland to Bristol, Glasgow, Liverpool and London in 1847, the year about 400,000 people in Ireland died. The food included butter, grain and livestock, some shipped under military guard. (Source: Ireland's Great Hunger Museum, citing Kinealy.) Wikipedia's grain-trade table gives Irish grain exports of 1,826,000 quarters in 1846 and 970,000 in 1847, while imports rose (Wikipedia, "Great Famine (Ireland)"; label: encyclopedia summary). Historians still argue about how much of the need those exports could have met; the sources agree the exports happened.
- Who decided: in an earlier food crisis, 1782–84, the government had banned grain exports from Ireland and the ban had worked. In 1846–47 the government of Prime Minister Lord John Russell refused to do this again (Ireland's Great Hunger Museum). Russell's government, in power from June 1846, kept the previous prime minister Robert Peel's policy of allowing grain exports and shifted the cost of relief onto Irish taxpayers (Wikipedia, "Great Famine (Ireland)"). Peel's government had bought £100,000 of American maize (corn) in November 1845 (same source).
- Charles Trevelyan, the senior Treasury official who ran famine relief: in December 1846 he wrote to Lord Monteagle that "the real evil with which we have to contend is not the physical evil of the Famine but the moral evil of the selfish, perverse and turbulent character of the people." In "The Irish Crisis," which he wrote anonymously for the Edinburgh Review in January 1848, he called the famine "a direct stroke of an all-wise and all-merciful Providence." (Source: Wikipedia, "Sir Charles Trevelyan, 1st Baronet," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_Charles_Trevelyan,_1st_Baronet; label: encyclopedia summary quoting his letters and article. The December 1846 letter is quoted widely but the original was not seen in this session.)
- The quarter-acre rule: a clause in the 1847 Irish Poor Law amendment, named after the member of Parliament William H. Gregory, barred anyone holding more than a quarter acre of land from getting relief. To eat, a family had to give up its land. (Source: Wikipedia, "Great Famine (Ireland).")
- Evictions (landlords forcing tenant families out, often pulling down the house): police records count almost 250,000 people officially evicted from 1849 to 1854. The historian James S. Donnelly Jr. judged that an undercount; adding forced "voluntary" surrenders from 1846 to 1854, the total "would almost certainly exceed half a million persons." Named landlords: Lord Palmerston (a British cabinet minister) cleared about 2,000 tenants from his Sligo estate and paid their passage overseas; Major Denis Mahon evicted thousands of tenants in County Roscommon. (Source: Wikipedia, "Great Famine (Ireland)," citing Donnelly.) Mahon was shot dead in November 1847; that detail is from general knowledge, not a source checked this session, so do not use it without checking.
- Deaths: about 1 million people died of starvation and famine diseases, the figure the Great Hunger Museum gives as "more than 1 million." Historians call the 1 million an informed estimate, because Ireland did not register deaths then (Mokyr 1985; Boyle and Ó Gráda 1986, as summarized on the search result for EH.net, "Ireland's Great Famine"; the page itself did not load). Counts range from about 1 million to 1.5 million depending on method. The population fell from 8,175,124 counted in 1841 to 6.55 million in 1851. Recent studies think the 1841 count was too low and the real 1846 population was about 8.7 million. (Source: RTÉ History, "Greatest ravages: the 1851 census and famine mortality," 2020, https://www.rte.ie/history/post-famine/2020/0915/1165372-greatest-ravages-the-1851-census-and-famine-mortality/.) Most died of disease, "fever," as well as hunger (same source).
- Leaving: at least 2.1 million people left Ireland from 1845 to 1855 (Wikipedia); "nearly 2 million" to America and Australia plus 750,000 to Britain (Great Hunger Museum). The bank's earlier figure of more than 1.5 million arriving at US ports 1845–1855 stands beside these.
- The ships: overcrowded, disease-ridden emigrant ships were called "coffin ships." The Toronto Globe reported in 1847 that the Virginius, carrying 496 passengers, "had lost 158 by death, nearly one third"; the Erin's Queen lost 78 with 104 sick. These two ships sailed to Canada (Grosse Île quarantine station), not the United States. (Source: Ireland's Great Hunger Museum.) A US-port death rate was not found in the sources checked.
- The genocide question: some writers call British policy genocide; many historians reject that label while blaming the government's choices. Wikipedia has a section on the debate but its text did not load. Do not state either side as settled.

## 7. 1850 to 1900 — the huge wave and the first closing door

### Where they landed
- **Castle Garden**, at Castle Clinton in Battery Park, New York, was America's first official immigrant depot, run jointly by New York State and City from August 1855 to April 1890. It processed about 8 million of the roughly 11 million immigrants who arrived through New York in that period. New York took over from the federal side amid corruption complaints; the federal government then took charge of immigration. (Sources: NPS Castle Clinton; New-York Historical Society.)
- **Ellis Island** opened January 1, 1892, as the federal immigration station in New York Harbor, replacing Castle Garden. (Peak-year numbers in section 8.)

### The West Coast: Chinese immigration
- Chinese immigration began with the California Gold Rush: about 325 came in 1849, some 20,000 in 1852; about 40,400 arrived between 1851 and 1860. By 1880 there were about 105,465 Chinese in the country. Most came from Guangdong (Canton) province in southern China. (Sources: 19th-century Chinese immigration references; PBS American Experience.)
- The Central Pacific Railroad hired about 10,000 to 12,000 Chinese workers between 1865 and 1869 to build the western part of the first transcontinental railroad; roughly 1,200 died. (Immigration angle = who came and the work that drew them. The railroad engineering and labor itself → Transportation Ch7; Economy Ch9.)

### The first restriction by nationality
- **Page Act of 1875** — the first restrictive federal immigration law. It barred "coolie" contract laborers and, in practice, most Chinese women (on the pretext of barring prostitution). (Sources: NPS San Francisco Maritime; Page Act references.)
- **Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882** — signed by President Chester A. Arthur on May 6, 1882; barred Chinese laborers from immigrating for 10 years. It was the first U.S. law to bar a group by nationality or ethnicity. It was renewed and widened and was not repealed until 1943. (Sources: National Archives; Britannica.)

### The welcoming symbol
- **The Statue of Liberty**, a gift from France, was dedicated October 28, 1886, in New York Harbor. (As a monument → Landmarks Ch8.)
- **Emma Lazarus** wrote the sonnet **"The New Colossus"** in 1883 to help raise money for the statue's pedestal — the lines "Give me your tired, your poor, / Your huddled masses yearning to breathe free." The poem was not read at the 1886 dedication and was largely forgotten. After Lazarus died, her friend Georgina Schuyler had it cast on a bronze plaque, mounted inside the pedestal in 1903, which is how the statue became tied to the idea of America as a refuge for immigrants. Lazarus (1849–1887) came from a Sephardic Jewish family in New York and had worked with Russian-Jewish refugees arriving in the 1880s. (Sources: Wikipedia "The New Colossus"; Museum of the City of New York; Smithsonian.)

### New sources: Southern and Eastern Europe
- New arrivals came increasingly from Southern and Eastern Europe: Italians, Poles, Hungarians, Greeks, and Jews.
- **Russian and Eastern European Jews** fled pogroms (violent anti-Jewish riots) that spread after Tsar Alexander II was assassinated in 1881, and the "May Laws" of 1882 that restricted where Jews could live and work in the Russian Empire's Pale of Settlement. More than 2 million Jews from the Russian Empire came to the United States between 1881 and 1914 (over 1.5 million by 1910, most from Russia). (Sources: LOC; Jewish Virtual Library; academic migration studies.)

### Featured person — Oluale Kossola (Cudjo Lewis), the *Clotilda*
> **Who:** one of the last survivors of the last known slave ship to reach the United States; a founder of Africatown, Alabama.
> **When and where:** born about 1841 in West Africa (in present-day Benin); brought to Mobile, Alabama, July 1860; died 1935.
> **Movie:** the documentary *Descendant* (2022) tells the *Clotilda* and Africatown story. (Confirm the exact title/year for the metadata line before use; a National Geographic documentary on the *Clotilda* also exists.)

- The schooner **Clotilda** illegally brought about 110 Africans to Mobile, Alabama, around July 8, 1860 — 52 years after the 1808 ban on importing enslaved people — financed by the businessman Timothy Meaher, reportedly on a bet that he could smuggle in captives. This is the last documented slave ship to arrive in the United States. (Sources: Smithsonian; Encyclopedia of Alabama; CBS News.)
- Kossola was captured in West Africa (accounts describe a raid by Dahomey forces), taken to the coast, and carried on the Middle Passage to Mobile. After emancipation the survivors could not afford passage home and founded **Africatown** near Mobile.
- In 1927 Kossola was interviewed by the writer and folklorist **Zora Neale Hurston**. Her book *Barracoon: The Story of the Last "Black Cargo"* was not published until **2018**. The wreck of the *Clotilda* was found in the Mobile River and confirmed in 2019. (Sources: Encyclopedia of Alabama; Smithsonian.)
- This story fits Ch2's arrival angle (capture → Middle Passage → arrival → a new community). The slavery-as-injustice angle belongs to Ch17.

**Rock Springs, Wyoming Territory, September 2, 1885**
- Who and why: the Union Pacific Railroad's coal department hired Chinese miners at lower pay. By 1885 about 600 Chinese and 300 white miners worked the Rock Springs mines. White miners, many in the Knights of Labor union, resented this. (Source: WyoHistory.org, Wyoming State Historical Society, "The Rock Springs Massacre," https://www.wyohistory.org/encyclopedia/rock-springs-massacre.)
- Who attacked: 100 to 150 armed white miners and railroad workers, most of them immigrants themselves from Britain, Ireland, Wales and Scandinavia (WyoHistory).
- What was done: the mob shot Chinese miners and struck them with picks, hatchets and clubs. It looted and burned all 79 shacks and houses of Chinatown. **Expulsions: the "Eureka method" and the "Tacoma method," 1885–1886**
- Eureka, California, February 6–7, 1885: City Councilman David Kendall was killed by a stray bullet in a shootout between two Chinese groups. That night more than 600 white residents gathered and put up a gallows at Fifth and F Streets. City leaders ordered all Chinese people to leave within 24 hours. A "Committee of Fifteen" put virtually all of Eureka's roughly 480 Chinese residents on two steamships to San Francisco. Families left behind furniture, goods, shrimp boats and vegetable farms. No one was killed; Chinatown was torn down. In 1886 the Chinese Six Companies sued (Wing Hing v. Eureka); the case was dropped. Exclusion rules stayed in Eureka's city charter until 1959. (Sources: Wikipedia, "1885 Chinese expulsion from Eureka"; KQED, 2017, labeled journalism; Clarke Historical Museum, Eureka.) Wikipedia calls it the first of more than 170 expulsions of Chinese communities from 1885 to 1887 (label: encyclopedia summary, count not independently checked).
- Tacoma, Washington Territory, November 3, 1885: Mayor Jacob Weisbach, who called the Chinese "a curse" and a "filthy horde," led a year-long campaign with Jack Comerford, editor of the Tacoma Ledger. Leaders ordered all Chinese residents out by November 3. About 150 left before the deadline. On the day, a mob forced about 200 more out of their homes in the rain and marched them to the Lake View train station. The stationmaster sold 77 tickets to Portland; around 3 a.m. a freight train took the rest. The mob then burned their houses. None returned. - Seattle, Washington Territory, February 7–8, 1886: mobs tied to the Knights of Labor went house to house in Chinatown and forced more than 300 of the city's 350–400 Chinese residents to the Ocean Dock. 196 were shipped out on the steamer Queen of the Pacific, and 110 more on the George W. Elder on February 14. When the mob tried to block the rest from returning, the Home Guard (a local militia) fired into it; Charles Stewart died the next morning, and four or five others were injured. Governor Watson Squire declared martial law, and President Cleveland sent 300 federal troops. (Source: HistoryLink.org, File 2745, https://www.historylink.org/file/2745.) What happened to the attackers: 13 men were tried in connection with the riot and none was convicted (Wikipedia, "Seattle riot of 1886"). Congress paid the Chinese government $276,619.15 for losses in the western attacks; Wikipedia says the money never reached the victims (same source; check before use).

The lot comprises about 20,000 pounds." The bones were being sent home to China, a practice paid for by Chinese benevolent (mutual aid) societies.

  - (Source for both quotes: Central Pacific Railroad Photographic History Museum discussion archive, https://discussion.cprr.net/2007/01/dead-chinese.html, citing newspaper microfilm at the Nevada Historical Society and California State Library. So the recorded range runs from about 50 to about 1,200, and some writers go higher ("hundreds to thousands"). No source checked gives a documented count.

(Source: PBS American Experience, https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/americanexperience/features/tcrr-tunneling-sierra-nevada/.) - What a pogrom is: a violent attack by a crowd on Jews, their homes, shops and synagogues. (Plain definition; the word came into wide use after the riots of 1881–82. Source: search summary of My Jewish Learning, "What Were Pogroms?")
- When and where: the first attack of the series was in Elisavetgrad (today Kropyvnytskyi, Ukraine), in Kherson province, on April 15, 1881, the week after Easter, six weeks after revolutionaries assassinated Tsar Alexander II on March 1 (Old Style). A serious three-day pogrom hit Kiev on April 26 (May 8 New Style), 1881, and spread to villages around it. Warsaw had a pogrom on Christmas Day 1881. Other attacks followed through 1884. (Sources: search summaries of YIVO Encyclopedia, "Pogroms," and Wikipedia, "Kiev pogrom (1881)"; the YIVO page did not render in this session.)
- How many: about 250 pogroms, 1881–1884 (John Klier, "Pogroms, Pre-Soviet Russia," *Encyclopedia of Genocide and Crimes Against Humanity*, via encyclopedia.com, https://www.encyclopedia.com/international/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/pogroms-pre-soviet-russia); 259 pogroms from 1881 to 1883 (Wikipedia, "Pogroms in the Russian Empire").
- Who attacked: crowds Klier calls "the so-called barefoot brigade," drifting laborers. Wikipedia lists railroad workers, local business rivals of Jews, and industrial workers, with peasants joining mostly to loot. (Same two sources.)
- What was done: looting and wrecking of Jewish homes and shops, beatings, rapes and killings (Klier; Wikipedia).
- Deaths, as each source counts them:
  - "At least 40 Jews were killed during pogroms between April and December 1881" (Wikipedia, "Pogroms in the Russian Empire").
  - "About 50 fatalities, of whom half were pogromshchiki [the rioters] killed during the suppression of the riots" (Klier), which is about 25 Jews and 25 rioters, the same split Wikipedia gives for 1881–1883.
  - Rapes: Klier writes there were rapes "but not in the massive numbers claimed by contemporary publicists." A search summary of another source gives "hundreds of women raped"; that source was not identified, so do not use the "hundreds" figure without finding it.
  - The death toll from the 1881–84 wave was far smaller than later waves (Kishinev 1903, 1905, 1918–21). Do not borrow those figures for this era.
- Who was behind it: historians once believed the tsar's government organized the pogroms. Hans Rogger, I. Michael Aronson and John Klier found no evidence of central planning (Wikipedia). Klier writes that the government "took extensive measures to anticipate, prevent, and repress anti-Jewish riots." Wikipedia adds that the government's anti-Jewish policies made the violence possible and that some local officials helped stir it up.
- What happened to the attackers, where sources differ: Klier: "over a thousand pogromshchiki suffered some form of punishment," and some officials were removed from office for failing to stop the riots. Wikipedia: "The perpetrators who were prosecuted usually received clemency by Tsar's decree." Both kept; not settled.
- The May Laws: minister of the interior Nikolai Pavlovich Ignatiev proposed them and Tsar Alexander III approved them on May 3, 1882 (May 15 New Style). Their official name was the "Temporary Regulations." Ignatiev blamed the pogroms on the Jews themselves, as a peasant protest against "Jewish exploitation." The rules: "Jews be forbidden to settle anew outside of towns and boroughs"; Jews could not buy or rent land in the countryside or receive mortgages on it; Jews could not trade on Sunday mornings or Christian holidays. Later rules added quotas limiting Jewish students in universities. They were never lifted until 1917. (Sources: Center for Online Judaic Studies, "May 3, 1882, The May Laws," https://cojs.org/may-3-1882-pogroms-may-laws/; Wikipedia, "May Laws"; Klier.) The 1917 end date is from the Wikipedia summary and should be checked before use.

## 8. 1900 to 1950 — the wave peaks, then the gates shut

### The peak at Ellis Island
- The great wave peaked in the early 1900s. **Ellis Island's busiest year was 1907**, with 1,004,756 immigrants admitted (often rounded to "about a million"; the outline's earlier "1.25 million" figure is too high). The single busiest day was April 17, 1907, with 11,747 people. About **12 million immigrants passed through Ellis Island from 1892 to 1954.** (Sources: Statue of Liberty–Ellis Island Foundation; American Immigration Council; History.com. Older claims of ~17 million are overstated.)

### The West Coast: Angel Island
- **Angel Island Immigration Station** opened in 1910 in San Francisco Bay. Unlike Ellis Island, its main job was to enforce the Chinese Exclusion Act and other anti-Asian laws — it screened and often detained Asian arrivals, sometimes for days, sometimes up to two years. About 175,000 Chinese immigrants (and hundreds of thousands of people overall) were processed there between 1910 and 1940. Detained Chinese immigrants carved poems into the wooden barrack walls; the poems were nearly destroyed but were saved after a park ranger, Alexander Weiss, found them in 1970. (Sources: Angel Island Immigration Station Foundation; Smithsonian.)

### The door shuts
- **Emergency Quota Act of 1921** — the first numerical caps on immigration, set at 3 percent of each nationality's foreign-born residents counted in the 1910 census.
- **Immigration Act of 1924 (Johnson-Reed Act, the National Origins Act)** — tied each country's yearly quota to 2 percent of its foreign-born population in the **1890 census**, a year chosen because it came before the big Southern and Eastern European wave. This slashed those groups (Italy's yearly allotment fell from over 42,000 to fewer than 6,000) and barred nearly all immigration from Asia (by excluding those "ineligible for citizenship"). Total immigration was capped near 165,000. The great wave ended. (Sources: Wikipedia "Immigration Act of 1924"; History.com; Tenement Museum.)

### Refugees and reception
- **MS *St. Louis*, 1939.** In May 1939 the German liner *St. Louis* carried 937 passengers, almost all Jewish refugees, from Hamburg toward Havana. Cuba, then the United States, then Canada refused to let them land; the ship returned to Europe. Passengers were taken in by Britain, France, Belgium, and the Netherlands; 254 of them were later killed in the Holocaust. (Handle carefully. Sources: U.S. Holocaust Memorial Museum; Britannica.)
- **Displaced Persons Act of 1948** — the first U.S. refugee law. It first authorized 200,000 displaced Europeans (within the quota system); a 1950 extension raised the total, and roughly 350,000 to 400,000 European refugees were resettled between 1948 and 1952 (one count: 393,542). (Sources: USHMM; Truman Library; USCIS.)

### Mexican immigration
- The Mexican Revolution (1910–1920) pushed people north; about 350,000 fled across the border in that decade, and the flow continued in the 1920s.
- **Mexican Repatriation (1929–1936):** during the Great Depression, roughly 500,000 people of Mexican descent were pressured or forced to leave for Mexico — including many who were U.S. citizens. (Note the injustice thread here can also be flagged for Ch17; the arrival-angle point is that arrivals reversed into a forced departure.)
- **Bracero Program (August 1942–1964):** a U.S.–Mexico agreement bringing Mexican farmworkers in temporarily; about 4.5 million contracts were issued over its life. This was temporary labor rather than immigration to stay, so the labor/economy angle → Economy (Ch9); noted here because it shaped later Mexican settlement. (Sources: Britannica; LOC; Bracero History Archive.)

**Angel Island Immigration Station, 1910–1940: what detention was**
- Detainees were locked in crowded dormitories and could not leave them without a guard (National Park Service, "U.S. Immigration Station, Angel Island," https://www.nps.gov/places/u-s-immigration-station-angel-island.htm). Officers read all incoming and outgoing letters, and detainees could have no visitors until their cases were decided (search summary of Angel Island Immigration Station Foundation material).
- The medical exam: Public Health Service doctors flipped immigrants' eyelids to look for trachoma (an eye infection that can cause blindness) and took blood, saliva, urine or stool samples. Asian arrivals were tested for parasites such as hookworm. When officials doubted a person's stated age, doctors made the person strip naked while they examined hair, skin, teeth, voice and genitals. (Source: Angel Island Immigration Station Foundation, "Vault #5: Keepers of the Gate," https://www.aiisf.org/vault/keepers.)
- The interrogation: a Board of Special Inquiry, two inspectors, a stenographer and a translator, questioned each Chinese applicant to catch "paper sons" and "paper daughters" (people claiming a false family tie to a US citizen, the only way most Chinese could get in under exclusion). Two of the three board members had to agree on the verdict. (NPS; AIISF.) One inspector said in 1934 that "well over ninety percent" of his time went to questioning arriving Chinese (AIISF). Interrogations ran from 200 to 1,000 questions about home villages and relatives (search summary; exact source not confirmed). The outline's Lee Puey You faced about 170 (Judy Yung, already in the outline).
- How long: from a few days to months; "in some instances, several years" (NPS). The longest recorded stay is 22 months (Wikipedia, "Angel Island Immigration Station"; the outline's Lee Puey You, about 20 months, is among the longest).
- Rejected: about 18 percent of arrivals were rejected at Angel Island, against 1 to 3 percent at Ellis Island (Wikipedia, "Angel Island Immigration Station"; label: encyclopedia summary).
- Suicides: Wikipedia states that some detainees killed themselves, before leaving or on the voyage back, and gives no names or count. No documented case was confirmed in the sources checked this session. Do not state a number.
- How many Chinese: the bank says about 175,000 Chinese passed through (Wikipedia agrees, with 117,000 Japanese, out of about 1 million people). The National Park Service says "up to one million Asian and other immigrants, including 250,000 Chinese and 150,000 Japanese." Both kept; not settled.
- A fire burned the administration building on August 12, 1940, and the station closed soon after (NPS).

**The 1924 Immigration Act: who made it and on what argument**
- Who: Representative Albert Johnson of Washington State, chairman of the House Committee on Immigration and Naturalization, and Senator David Reed of Pennsylvania ("Johnson-Reed Act"). President Calvin Coolidge signed it on May 26, 1924 (search summary of Time and Teaching American History; the State Department Office of the Historian page, https://history.state.gov/milestones/1921-1936/immigration-act, confirms Coolidge signed it but gives no date). The Office of the Historian states: "The most basic purpose of the 1924 Immigration Act was to preserve the ideal of U.S. homogeneity" (sameness).
- Coolidge's own words, in his first message to Congress (December 1923): "America must be kept American. For this purpose, it is necessary to continue a policy of restricted immigration." (Source: Miller Center, University of Virginia, "Harding, Coolidge, and immigration," https://millercenter.org/issues-policy/us-domestic-policy/harding-coolidge-and-immigration.)
- The eugenics argument (eugenics: the false belief that a nation could improve its people by controlling who had children and who came in, ranking groups as "better" or "worse" by birth):
  - Harry H. Laughlin, superintendent of the Eugenics Record Office at Cold Spring Harbor, New York, testified three times to Johnson's committee, 1920–1924. He claimed immigrants from southern and eastern Europe, mainly Italians and Jews, had higher rates of "feeblemindedness, insanity, criminality, and dependency." He said a disproportionate number of people in mental hospitals came from southern and eastern Europe, though his own data showed German and Irish rates were also high. Johnson appointed him the committee's "expert eugenics agent." (Sources: Cold Spring Harbor Laboratory Eugenics Archive, "Immigration," http://www.eugenicsarchive.org/html/eugenics/static/themes/10.html; Embryo Project Encyclopedia, Arizona State University, "Harry Hamilton Laughlin," https://embryo.asu.edu/pages/harry-hamilton-laughlin-1880-1943.) His November 1922 testimony was titled "Analysis of America's Modern Melting Pot" (Embryo Project). Laughlin also wrote a "Model Eugenical Sterilization Law" (1922) that shaped state laws forcing surgery on people to make them unable to have children, and Nazi Germany's 1933 sterilization law; the University of Heidelberg gave him an honorary degree in 1936 (Embryo Project). The sterilization story belongs to `health` and `rights-movements`.
  - Madison Grant, a New York lawyer, wrote *The Passing of the Great Race* (1916), which claimed a "Nordic race" from northwestern Europe was superior to all others. He was a member of the Immigration Restriction League and "helped Congressman Albert Johnson, a fellow member of the Galton Society, draft the Immigration Act of 1924." Adolf Hitler later wrote to Grant calling the book his "Bible." (Source: Embryo Project Encyclopedia, "Madison Grant (1865–1937)," https://embryo.asu.edu/pages/madison-grant-1865-1937.)
- What it did, in numbers: yearly immigration capped at about 165,000, down from about 355,000 under the 1921 law (Embryo Project, Grant). About 86 percent of the quota went to northern and western Europe and about 9 percent to southern and eastern Europe (Eugenics Archive). Asian immigration was barred entirely. The German-Austrian quota was 27,370 a year by 1939 (USHMM, below).

**Mexican Repatriation, 1929–1936 (some sources to 1944)**
- Who: US Secretary of Labor William N. Doak, under President Herbert Hoover, led a national campaign to remove Mexicans, claiming they took jobs during the Great Depression. In Los Angeles, Charles P. Visel, head of the city's unemployment relief committee, planned the publicity campaign, and the Los Angeles County welfare office ran the "repatriation" trains. (Source: California Migration Museum, "From Deportation to Repatriation," https://www.calmigration.org/learn-chapter/deportation-to-repatriation.)
- How: officials raided Mexican American neighborhoods and public places. County welfare officers told families their relief money would stop unless they took free train tickets to Mexico. Hospital staff carried patients on stretchers to trucks for the border. The first county train left March 23, 1931. (California Migration Museum.) The State of California found that "massive raids were conducted on Mexican-American communities" and that families lost their property, which "often was sold by local authorities as 'payment'" for the cost of removing them (California Government Code §8721, enacted by SB 670, the "Apology Act for the 1930s Mexican Repatriation Program," signed 2005, https://www.leginfo.ca.gov/pub/05-06/bill/sen/sb_0651-0700/sb_670_bill_20051007_chaptered.html).
- The La Placita raid, Los Angeles, February 26, 1931: immigration agents sealed the exits of La Placita park while about 400 people were inside (California Migration Museum). Accounts differ on the result: about a dozen arrested and a handful deported (California Migration Museum); "rounded up more than 400 people ... and deported them" (search summary of a History.com article). Both kept; the museum account is the more specific.
- How many, and how many were citizens, as each source counts:
  - About 1 million repatriated nationally; about 60 percent were US-born citizens and about 2 percent naturalized citizens (California Migration Museum, following the historians Francisco Balderrama and Raymond Rodríguez, *Decade of Betrayal*; the book itself not checked).
  - About 2 million people of Mexican ancestry, "approximately 1.2 million of whom had been born in the United States"; in California alone about 400,000 US citizens and legal residents (State of California, SB 670 findings, 2005).
  - About 35,000 from Los Angeles, about one-third of its Mexican population, 1929–1936 (California Migration Museum).
  - The bank's existing "roughly 500,000" is the low end of published estimates. It stands, but the prose must give the range (about 500,000 to 2 million) and the citizen share.
- Apologies: the State of California apologized by law in 2005 (SB 670). The Los Angeles County Board of Supervisors apologized in February 2012 and put up a plaque at LA Plaza (MALDEF press release, 2012, https://www.maldef.org/news/releases/mexican_american_repatriation_event_2012/). No federal apology was found in the sources checked.

President Franklin D. Roosevelt never answered the passengers' appeals. The State Department said they must "await their turns on the waiting list." The ship sailed back to Europe on June 6. ## 9. 1950 to 2000 — the doors reopen, the sources shift

### The 1965 turning point
- **Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965 (Hart-Celler Act)** — abolished the national-origins quota system that had shaped immigration since the 1920s. The new system was built on family ties and job skills. It removed the bars against Asians and Southern/Eastern Europeans, and over the following decades it shifted the main sources of immigration toward Asia, Latin America, Africa, and the Caribbean. It also set, for the first time, a numerical cap on immigration from the Western Hemisphere (including Mexico). (Sources: Wikipedia; Immigration History project; History.com; U.S. House History.)

### Refugee waves
- **Cubans:** after Fidel Castro's 1959 revolution, waves of Cubans came to the United States, especially to Florida, through the 1960s. The **Mariel boatlift** ran April to October 1980, bringing about **125,000** Cubans by boat from the port of Mariel to Florida after Castro briefly allowed departures. (Sources: Britannica; Immigration History project; History.com.)
- **Southeast Asians:** after the fall of Saigon in 1975, refugees came from Vietnam, Laos (including the Hmong), and Cambodia — many as "boat people." From 1981 to 1990 alone, nearly 281,000 Vietnamese refugees were resettled in the United States. (Sources: USCIS Refugee Timeline; EBSCO "Boat people.")
- **Refugee Act of 1980** — created a permanent, standardized system for admitting refugees and defined "refugee" along United Nations lines. It passed about one month before the Mariel boatlift began. (Sources: Immigration History project; USCIS.)

### Legalization and enforcement
- **Immigration Reform and Control Act of 1986 (IRCA)**, signed November 6, 1986: legalized about **2.7 million** long-term unauthorized residents (those who could show continuous residence since before 1982, plus certain farmworkers), and for the first time set penalties for employers who knowingly hired unauthorized workers ("employer sanctions"). The unauthorized population grew again afterward. (Sources: USCIS; Immigration History project; LOC.)

## 10. 2000 to Today

### How many, and from where
- The foreign-born share of the population has climbed back toward its early-1900s peak. ### PATCH 2026-09-26 (T-239): The 2018 family separations: the DHS officials who carried out the policy
*Gap from T-237c. Sources: the US government's own court filing in* C.M. v. United States *(court record); the Justice Department inspector general's January 2021 report (government); The Washington Post via The Texas Tribune (journalism, for the names of the three officials who signed). The Project on Government Oversight letter and the DHS inspector general's PDFs refused access.*
- The DHS Referral Memorandum: "In a memorandum dated April 23, 2018, titled 'Increasing Prosecutions of Immigration Violations,' the Commissioner of U.S. Customs and Border Protection ('CBP'), the Director of ICE, and the Director of U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services ('USCIS') proposed three prosecution referral policy options to the Secretary of Homeland Security." CBP drafted it "at the request of DHS Secretary Kirstjen Nielsen." The three officials recommended Option 3: "Refer all Amenable Adults, including those presenting as part of a FMUA [family unit]," to reach "100% immigration violation prosecution referral for all amenable adults, including those initially arriving or apprehended with minors." (United States' Statement of Facts in Support of Motion for Summary Judgment, facts 6 to 8, *C.M. v. United States*, No. 2:19-cv-05217-SRB, US District Court for the District of Arizona, Doc. 372, filed March 9, 2023, https://www.americanimmigrationcouncil.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/bce8b0a17413d7c54ebffb54e776df07-1.pdf.)
- The three who signed: "acting Director of Immigration and Customs Enforcement Thomas Homan, Director of Citizenship and Immigration Services L. Francis Cissna and Customs and Border Protection Commissioner Kevin K. McAleenan." (Maria Sacchetti, The Washington Post, republished by The Texas Tribune, April 27, 2018, https://www.texastribune.org/2018/04/27/top-homeland-security-officials-urge-criminal-prosecution-parents-cros/; journalism.)
- Nielsen's decision: "On May 4, 2018, Secretary Nielsen approved Option 3 in the DHS Referral Memorandum: to refer all amenable adults who unlawfully cross the Southwest border for criminal prosecution, including those who arrive as part of a family unit" (C.M. filing, fact 15). The Justice Department inspector general: "On May 4, then DHS Secretary Nielsen signed a policy memorandum citing 'recent presidential direction and guidance from the Attorney General' and directing DHS Border Patrol sectors to refer for prosecution all adults apprehended for crossing the border illegally. The document explicitly included family unit adults." The same report says this was done "with the urging of Sessions." (DOJ OIG, "Review of the Department of Justice's Planning and Implementation of Its Zero Tolerance Policy," report 21-028, January 2021, https://oig.justice.gov/sites/default/files/reports/21-028_0.pdf.)
- Carrying it out: on May 4, 2018, Border Patrol headquarters told the Southwest Border sector chiefs they were "authorized to implement increased Southwest Border Prosecutions ... effective May 5, 2018." McAleenan "directed that parents traveling with children under the age of five (5) were exempt" for a first illegal entry. No directive told Border Patrol sectors exactly when a separated child should be designated an "unaccompanied alien child" (fact 26). Once a child was so designated, Border Patrol agents asked the Office of Refugee Resettlement (in the Department of Health and Human Services) to take the child "as expeditiously as possible" (fact 27). ICE officers then held the parents under ICE's usual detention rules. The government's filing says "There was no prescribed specific course of action for reunification" of separated parents and children. (C.M. filing, facts 17, 23, 24, 27, 28.) The DOJ inspector general adds that on May 4, 2018, "Border Patrol instructed field personnel to use spreadsheets to track separations because [database] system changes were still pending" (DOJ OIG 21-028, quoting the DHS inspector general).
- The government's position in that filing: "It was not Secretary Nielsen's purpose in adopting the DHS Referral Policy to use separations of parents and children to inflict harm as a deterrent" (fact 18). This is the defendant's own statement of facts, not a finding by the court. Keep it attributed.
