## 1. Before 1500

### Population — state the range, it is genuinely unsettled
- Estimates of the Native population north of Mexico at contact run from about **1 million to about 18 million**. Douglas Ubelaker (1976) estimated ~1.85 million for the continental U.S.; Henry Dobyns (1983) estimated ~18 million north of Mesoamerica; textbook estimates from 1988–1990 alone ranged from 1 to 12 million. There is no settled number. (Sources: British Academy paper "The Demographic Collapse of Native Peoples of the Americas"; Southeastern Oklahoma State Univ. proceedings "Counting the Dead"; both summarizing Ubelaker and Dobyns.)

### Cities and towns (nations as builders — Cahokia/Chaco facts already verified in ch03 bank)
- **Cahokia** (near present-day St. Louis): occupied ~700–1400, peak ~1100 with roughly **10,000–20,000 people** in the city (up to ~50,000 in the surrounding area by some estimates) — comparable to London at the time; dispersed by ~1350; causes debated (drought, floods, resource strain — no single settled cause). (Sources: Live Science; Illinois state historic-site materials; Cambridge/Berkeley summaries — via research-ch03-migration-west.md.)
- **Chaco Canyon** (New Mexico): Ancestral Puebloan center of "great houses"; began breaking up ~1140 after severe droughts, largely left by the end of the 1100s; people regathered into the Pueblo communities of the Southwest. (Sources: History.com; Discover; National Geographic — via ch03 bank.)
- **The Calusa** (southwest Florida): a fishing kingdom, not a farming one — one of the most politically complex hunter-gatherer societies known, estimated ~20,000 people, collecting tribute from other groups from south of Tampa Bay to the Keys. Capital **Calos on Mound Key** (near Fort Myers), built on shell mounds; "watercourts" — shell-walled pens holding live fish — radiocarbon-dated to **1300–1400**. (Sources: NPS "The Calusa"; Smithsonian Magazine 2020; Florida Museum/Randell Research Center; Univ. of Georgia research news on the PNAS watercourt study.)
- **Trade routes** carried turquoise, seashells, copper, and obsidian hundreds of miles between nations; the continent was laced with Native trails later newcomers followed. (Source: ch03 bank, NMAI/Smithsonian materials.)
- **Athabascan (Dené) migration south** — ancestors of the Navajo (Diné) and Apache moved from present-day western Canada/eastern Alaska toward the Southwest; scholars disagree on arrival timing (roughly 1400s–1525 vs. as early as the 1200s). State the disagreement. (Source: ch03 bank — EBSCO Research Starters and others.)

### The Haudenosaunee Confederacy and the Great Law of Peace
- The **Great Law of Peace (Gayanesshagowa)** united five nations — **Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, Seneca** — under a Grand Council; the **Tuscarora joined later as the sixth nation** (1722, see era 4). Tradition credits the **Peacemaker** and **Hiawatha** with uniting the warring nations; the agreement was kept in oral law and recorded on wampum belts. (Sources: Haudenosaunee Confederacy official site "Confederacy's Creation"; Library of Congress law blog "The Haudenosaunee Confederacy and the Constitution," 2023.)
- **Founding date is disputed:** one line of scholarship (Barbara Mann & Jerry Fields, using a solar-eclipse record in the founding tradition) dates it to **1142**; most scholars place it between the **mid-1100s and the late 1400s**, many in the mid-to-late 1400s. State the range. (Sources: LOC law blog; World History Encyclopedia "Origins of the Haudenosaunee Confederacy"; AOL/AP piece on the eclipse dating.)
- Often described as among the world's oldest continuing participatory democracies; Canasatego's 1744 advice to the colonies and Franklin's printing of it connect it to later U.S. history (see era 4). (Source: LOC law blog; PBS Native America.)

- None before 1500 — documented individual lives are scarce this far back. Do not invent. (Chapter rule; also ch03 precedent.)

## 2. The 1500s

### The Great Dying
- European diseases moved ahead of and behind the first expeditions and killed large shares of Native communities through the century and after. The overall scale is contested because the starting population is contested (1–18 million north of Mexico, above); what is certain is the direction: by **1900 the U.S. census counted only about 250,000** Native people (see era 7). (Sources: British Academy demographic-collapse paper; USAFacts/Census history "Native Americans and the US Census.")
- Writer's note: state disease deaths plainly, but anchor to the honest range — do not pick a percentage and present it as settled.

### The entradas
- **Hernando de Soto, 1539–1543**: landed in Florida with over **620 men and 220 horses** (from Havana, May 1539); cut through the Southeast; de Soto died of fever in spring 1542; survivors reached Mexico September 10, 1543. His men were the first — and nearly the last — Europeans to see the big Mississippian chiefdoms; the expedition brought violence, forced labor, and disease, and the chiefdoms fragmented in the decades after. (Sources: World History Encyclopedia "Hernando de Soto's Expedition"; NCpedia "De Soto Expedition.")
- **Francisco Vázquez de Coronado, 1540–1542**: entrada through the Southwest and onto the southern Plains (his route and aims are `exploration`'s story; here only what it meant for the nations). (Source: ch03 bank — Smithsonian NMAI, History.com.)
- **Reorganization:** the Southeast's Mississippian world did not simply vanish — survivors regrouped, absorbed refugees, and over the following century formed new nations and confederacies. (Sources: World History Encyclopedia and NCpedia on post-entrada fragmentation; the specific successor-nation genealogies are complex — keep the outline statement general.)

### The horse returns
- Horses had died out in North America thousands of years earlier; Columbus carried horses on his second voyage (1493), Spanish colonists brought them to the mainland through the 1500s (Coronado 1540–42; Oñate's New Mexico colony 1598). The Plains transformation comes later (era 3). (Source: ch03 bank — Smithsonian NMAI "A Song for the Horse Nation.")

### Featured person — VERIFIED
**Paquiquineo (Don Luís de Velasco)** — a documented individual from Spanish records, read against the mission's fate.
> **Who:** a young man from the Chesapeake Bay area (the later Powhatan country) taken aboard a Spanish ship in 1561 — the records do not settle whether he went willingly or was taken; he appeared before King Philip II in Spain, was baptized in Mexico City with the viceroy's name, and spent nine years trying to get home.
> **When and where:** taken 1561; returned September 1570, landing on the James River as guide and translator for a small Jesuit mission (Father Juan Bautista de Segura); in February 1571 he led the attack that killed the missionaries (an altar boy was spared). Spain gave up on the Chesapeake; the English arrived at Jamestown 36 years later.
> **Movie:** none.
(Sources: Encyclopedia Virginia "Don Luís de Velasco / Paquiquineo (fl. 1561–1571)"; Virtual Jamestown; Jamestown-Yorktown Foundation.)

## 3. The 1600s

### The state of the land
- Nations still held nearly the whole continent; European settlements were a coastal fringe dependent on Native food, trade, and permission. Nations chose alliances among Dutch, French, English, and Spanish and played them against one another.

### Tsenacommacah — the Powhatan paramount chiefdom
- **Wahunsenacawh** ("the Powhatan" to the English), mamanatowick (paramount chief), inherited six tribes and built an organization of **more than 30 tributary peoples — about 15,000 people across some 10,000 square miles** of eastern Virginia (James River to the Potomac), each tribe under its own weroance paying tribute. The land was called **Tsenacommacah**. Jamestown (1607) was planted inside it. (Sources: Encyclopedia Virginia "Tsenacomoco (Powhatan Paramount Chiefdom)"; Historic Jamestowne "Chief Powhatan"; World History Encyclopedia.)

### Wampum diplomacy and the Covenant Chain
- **Two Row Wampum (Guswenta):** the Haudenosaunee record of their first agreement with the Dutch — two purple rows on white beads, two vessels traveling the same river side by side, neither steering the other. Haudenosaunee tradition dates it to 1613; the surviving paper "treaty" document of that date is disputed among scholars, the wampum tradition itself is the Haudenosaunee record — attribute it as such. (Sources: Onondaga Nation "Polishing the Silver Covenant Chain"; Onondaga Nation analysis of the Tawagonshi document; Wikipedia "Two Row Wampum Treaty" for the scholarly dispute.)
- **Covenant Chain:** the alliance system between the Haudenosaunee and the English colonies; the first surviving record of the "Silver Covenant Chain" is **1677**, formalized at Albany under New York governor Edmund Andros. Iron rusts; silver can be polished — the alliance was "polished" (renewed by council, speeches, and wampum) again and again. (Sources: Onondaga Nation; Wikipedia "Covenant Chain.")

### The coastal wars
- **Pequot War (1636–1638), Connecticut:** English colonists with Mohegan and Narragansett allies against the Pequot. **May 26, 1637 — the Mystic massacre:** an English-led force burned the fortified Pequot town at Mystic before dawn and killed hundreds, mostly elders, women, and children, in under an hour. The **Treaty of Hartford (1638)** declared the Pequot nation dissolved, outlawed even the name, and handed survivors to the victors or into slavery. The Mashantucket Pequot Tribal Nation exists in Connecticut today — the erasure failed. (Sources: Mashantucket Pequot Tribal Nation official history; World History Encyclopedia "Mystic Massacre"; Connecticut History (CTHumanities).)
- **King Philip's War (1675–1676):** Metacom (King Philip), sachem of the Wampanoag, led a coalition against the New England colonies. About **9,000 people died**; by share of population it is often called the bloodiest war in American history, and estimates put Native deaths at **roughly half the Native population of New England**. Metacom was shot dead at Mount Hope on **August 12, 1676** by a Native man serving with colonial forces; his wife and nine-year-old son were sold into slavery (sources name Bermuda / the West Indies); his head was displayed at Plymouth. Hundreds of captives were sold into slavery to pay the war's costs. (Sources: Britannica "King Philip's War" and "Metacom"; EBSCO "Metacom's War"; World History Encyclopedia; Connecticut History.)
- **The Beaver Wars** (Haudenosaunee campaigns west and north over the fur trade) remade the eastern interior through the mid-late 1600s. (Source: ch03 bank context; Canadian Encyclopedia via Great Peace of Montreal materials.)

### The Pueblo Revolt of 1680 — the era's centerpiece
- On **August 10, 1680**, the Pueblo nations of the Rio Grande, coordinated by **Po'pay (Popé)**, a Tewa religious leader from **Ohkay Owingeh**, rose together, besieged and took Santa Fe, and drove the Spanish out of New Mexico. **401 settlers and 21 Franciscan friars were killed**; most Spaniards were allowed to flee south. The Pueblos governed themselves for **12 years**; drought, famine, and raids marked the period; Diego de Vargas began the reconquest in **1692**, and armed resistance continued for years after. It is commonly called the most successful Indigenous uprising against a European colonial power in North America. (Sources: Britannica "Pueblo Rebellion"; EBSCO "Pueblo Revolt"; Indian Pueblo Cultural Center; Gilder Lehrman.)
- **Horses and the revolt — state both accounts:** the traditional account says horses spread across the West largely from herds (perhaps 1,500+) left behind in 1680, moving along Native trade routes to the Ute, Navajo, Apache, Comanche, Kiowa, Shoshone, and northern Plains nations. A **2023 study in *Science*** (William Taylor and Lakota, Comanche, Pawnee, and other Native partners) radiocarbon-dated horse remains showing many Plains and Rockies nations had horses by the **early 1600s**, decades before the revolt, matching Native oral histories — the horses were bridled and corn-fed, kept and cared for. Timing unsettled; say so. (Sources: *Science* 2023 "Early dispersal of domestic horses into the Great Plains and northern Rockies"; PBS NewsHour; Smithsonian NMAI — via ch03 bank.)

**Po'pay (Popé)** — Tewa religious leader from Ohkay Owingeh who organized the 1680 revolt across dozens of towns and two languages' worth of nations; knotted cords carried the countdown to the rising (knotted-cord detail widely told incl. Indian Pueblo Cultural Center — attribute to Pueblo accounts). A statue of Po'pay stands in the U.S. Capitol (one of New Mexico's two Statuary Hall figures — Indian Pueblo Cultural Center). **Movie: none.**

**Metacom (King Philip)** — Wampanoag sachem, son of Massasoit (the leader who had made the 1621 arrangement with Plymouth — attribute via Britannica); led the 1675–76 war; killed August 12, 1676; family sold into slavery. (Sources: Britannica "Metacom"; EBSCO; PBS.)

## 4. 1700 to 1750

### Play-off diplomacy
- **Great Peace of Montreal, August 4, 1701:** about **1,300 delegates from more than 30 nations** gathered at Montreal and concluded peace with New France; the Haudenosaunee agreed to stay **neutral** in French-British wars — neutrality they used for half a century to bargain with both sides. (Sources: Canadian Encyclopedia "Great Peace of Montreal"; Pointe-à-Callière museum.)

### The Tuscarora migration — the Confederacy grows
- **Tuscarora War, 1711–1713** (North Carolina): triggered by colonial settlement (New Bern, 1710) on Tuscarora land and by slave raids; coordinated Tuscarora attacks began September 22, 1711; South Carolina forces with Native allies defeated them. About **1,500 surviving Tuscarora moved north** and asked the Five Nations for sponsorship; they were taken in by the Oneida and in **1722 became the sixth nation of the Haudenosaunee Confederacy** — a nation that lost its homeland and was adopted whole by a confederacy 500 miles away. (Sources: North Carolina History Project; South Carolina Encyclopedia; Oklahoma Historical Society "Tuscarora.")

### Comanche power
- The Comanche moved onto the southern Plains by the early 1700s and built the dominant power there — **Comanchería** eventually covered about **240,000 square miles**. They bred, trained, and traded horses, controlled the horse trade from the southern to the northern Plains, and in the 1740s traded bison robes and horses to the French for guns better than Spanish equipment. Their power rested on horses, trade, raiding, and diplomacy — an economy and a foreign policy, not just war. (Sources: Bullock Texas State History Museum "Comanche Nation"; Pekka Hämäläinen, *The Comanche Empire* (Yale Univ. Press) — publisher summary; Wikipedia "Comanche history" cross-checked.)

### Treaty councils as working diplomacy
- **Treaty of Lancaster (Pennsylvania), June–July 1744** [VERIFY cleared — the treaty is real; the colonies bought out Iroquois claims to the Shenandoah Valley, see ch03 bank]. On **July 4, 1744**, the Onondaga speaker **Canasatego** closed the council by advising the colonies to unite as the Haudenosaunee had: "We heartily recommend Union and a good Agreement between you our Brethren... you, as well as we, will become the stronger." **Benjamin Franklin printed the treaty minutes**, including the speech. (Sources: EBSCO "Analysis: Canassatego's Speech at the Treaty of Lancaster, 1744"; PBS Native America; Wikipedia "Canassatego" cross-checked.)

### Featured person — VERIFIED
**Canasatego** — Onondaga leader and speaker for the Haudenosaunee at Lancaster, 1744.
> **Who:** the Confederacy's voice at one of the era's great treaty councils; his printed speeches are among the best-documented Native diplomacy of the century.
> **When and where:** Lancaster, Pennsylvania, June–July 1744; union speech July 4, 1744.
> **Movie:** none.
(Sources: EBSCO; PBS Native America; treaty minutes printed by Franklin, 1744.)

## 5. 1750 to 1800

### The French and Indian War and Pontiac's War
- In the French and Indian War (1754–1763) Native nations were allies both sides needed; the French defeat removed the counterweight the play-off system depended on.
- **Pontiac's War, 1763–1766:** a loose confederation of Great Lakes, Illinois Country, and Ohio Country nations under the Odawa leader **Pontiac** took or besieged British forts across the west; Pontiac lifted the siege of Detroit in October 1763; the war pushed Britain to mark a boundary. (Sources: Lumen US History I "Pontiac's War"; AmericanRevolution.org.)
- **Royal Proclamation of 1763 (issued October 7, 1763):** drew a line along the Appalachians — colonists were not to settle west of it; the land beyond was reserved to the nations. Colonists resented it and crossed it anyway, but the proclamation became a foundation of later treaty law. (Sources: Encyclopedia Virginia "The Proclamation of 1763"; Britannica Kids cross-check.)

### The Revolution splits the Haudenosaunee
- The war broke the Confederacy's unity for the first time in centuries: **Oneida and Tuscarora sided with the Americans; Mohawk, Seneca, Cayuga, and Onondaga fought with the British**, many under the Mohawk leader **Joseph Brant (Thayendanegea)** — at Oriskany (August 6, 1777) Haudenosaunee men fought on both sides. (Sources: NPS "The Six Nations Confederacy During the American Revolution"; Britannica "Haudenosaunee Confederacy"; New York Almanack.)
- **Sullivan Expedition, July 31–October 15, 1779:** on Washington's orders ("the total destruction and devastation of their settlements"), Sullivan's army burned **more than 40 Seneca and Cayuga towns** and their crops; thousands of refugees fled to British Fort Niagara, where many starved or froze that winter. Seneca chiefs told Washington in 1790: "When your army entered the country of the Six Nations, we called you the Town-destroyer; and to this day, when that name is heard, our women look behind them and turn pale." (The name Conotocaurius/"Town Destroyer" had been given to Washington in 1753, and earlier to his great-grandfather.) (Sources: American Battlefield Trust "Newtown"; Seneca County NY history chapter; Wikipedia "Town Destroyer" with the 1790 address.)

### The treaty system begins
- **First U.S.–Native treaty: the Treaty of Fort Pitt (Treaty with the Delawares), September 17, 1778** — signed by Lenape leaders White Eyes, John Kill Buck Jr., and Pipe; it recognized Delaware sovereignty, asked safe passage for American troops, and even floated a Native 14th state with representation in Congress (never acted on). (Sources: Smithsonian NMAI "A Brief Balance of Power — the 1778 Treaty"; Journal of the American Revolution; Heinz History Center.)
- **374 ratified treaties** between the United States and Native nations (with associated documents, series dated 1722–1869) are held at the National Archives, digitized in full. Treaty-making ended in 1871 (era 7). (Sources: National Archives "Viewing American Indian Treaties"; NARA Text Message blog.)

### The Northwest Confederacy — the biggest defeat the U.S. Army ever took from Native forces
- A confederacy of Ohio Country nations — **Little Turtle (Miami), Blue Jacket (Shawnee), Buckongahelas (Delaware)** — defended the country north of the Ohio River.
- **St. Clair's Defeat (Battle of the Wabash), November 4, 1791:** about 1,000 confederacy fighters destroyed Gen. Arthur St. Clair's army of about 1,000 — **over 650 U.S. soldiers killed, 270+ wounded** (casualty rate ~97%, the highest of any battle in U.S. Army history), against roughly 100 Native casualties. It remains the largest defeat of a U.S. army by Native forces, bigger than Little Bighorn. It triggered the first congressional investigation in U.S. history. (Sources: Britannica "Saint Clair's Defeat"; U.S. Army article "St. Clair's Campaign of 1791"; Levin Center "Congress' First Investigation.")
- **Fallen Timbers, August 20, 1794:** Anthony Wayne's 3,300-man legion defeated the confederacy near present-day Toledo; **Treaty of Greenville, August 3, 1795:** the confederacy ceded most of Ohio and part of Indiana. (Sources: Britannica "Battle of Fallen Timbers" and "Treaty of Greenville.")

### Featured person — VERIFIED
**Little Turtle (Mishikinaakwa)** — Miami war leader and, afterward, diplomat.
> **Who:** co-led the Northwest Confederacy that won the biggest Native victory over a U.S. army (1791); after Fallen Timbers he judged the fight unwinnable, signed at Greenville (1795), and spent his last years as an advocate of peace.
> **When and where:** the Wabash and Maumee country (present Indiana/Ohio), 1780s–1790s.
> **Movie:** none.
(Sources: Britannica "Saint Clair's Defeat"; Potawatomi.org "Little Turtle's War"; Britannica "Treaty of Greenville" — Wayne, Little Turtle at Greenville. Note: precise birth/death dates vary across sources; not needed for the outline.)

## 6. 1800 to 1850

### Tecumseh and Tenskwatawa
- **Tenskwatawa** ("the Prophet") began a religious movement of return to Native ways (~1805); with his brother **Tecumseh** (Shawnee) the movement built **Prophetstown** on the Wabash (near the Tippecanoe) as the center of a multi-nation confederacy. NOTE: sources differ on the town's founding — EBSCO's overview says 1805 (likely conflating the Greenville, Ohio beginning); most accounts put Prophetstown's establishment on the Wabash at 1808. State "the brothers built Prophetstown on the Wabash in 1808" or omit the year. (Sources: EBSCO "Founding of Prophetstown"; American Battlefield Trust "Tippecanoe"; History.com "Tecumseh.")
- **Battle of Tippecanoe, November 7, 1811:** William Henry Harrison marched ~1,000 men on Prophetstown while Tecumseh was away recruiting; after the battle Harrison burned the town (Nov 8). The confederacy was wounded but fought on in the War of 1812. (Source: American Battlefield Trust.)
- **Tecumseh died October 5, 1813** at the Battle of the Thames (Moraviantown, Upper Canada), abandoned mid-battle by his British ally Procter; the confederacy did not survive him. (Sources: Britannica/History.com; American Battlefield Trust "Tecumseh.")

### The Cherokee renaissance
- **Sequoyah** (born ~1770–1778, Taskigi, Tennessee — birth year uncertain, say "about 1770"; died 1843 in Mexico), a Cherokee silversmith who could not read or write any language, worked about 12 years and in **1821** demonstrated his **86-character syllabary** — each character one Cherokee syllable. He is the only person in recorded history known to have created a writing system alone without first being literate in another. Within a few years most of the Cherokee Nation could read and write it — History.com puts Cherokee literacy above the surrounding white settlers' by the 1830s (attribute this comparison, don't state it bare). (Sources: Britannica "Sequoyah"; Library of Congress name authority (1770?–1843); Smithsonian Magazine; History.com.)
- **Cherokee constitution, 1827** (capital New Echota, established 1825), modeled on the U.S. Constitution with three branches. Georgia answered by declaring the Cherokee government abolished. (Sources: New Georgia Encyclopedia "Cherokee Removal"; Georgia supreme-court-history site.)
- **Cherokee Phoenix, 1828** — first Native American newspaper, printed in Cherokee and English. (Sources: New York Public Library exhibit; Smithsonian Magazine.)

### Removal — told here from the nations' side (journey details are `migration`'s)
- **Indian Removal Act, 1830** — authorized the president to negotiate removal treaties. (Source: New Georgia Encyclopedia; ch03 bank has May 28, 1830 via NPS.)
- ***Worcester v. Georgia* (1832):** Chief Justice John Marshall ruled Georgia law had no force in the Cherokee Nation — the Nation was under federal protection, a foundation of tribal-sovereignty law to this day. Georgia ignored the ruling and removal proceeded anyway. (Sources: New Georgia Encyclopedia "Worcester v. Georgia"; Supreme Court Historical Society "The Cherokee Nation Cases.")
- **Trail of Tears, 1838–39:** more than **16,000 Cherokee** removed; **over 4,000 died** (traditional estimate; some scholars argue higher — state the dispute). The five southeastern nations — Cherokee, Muscogee (Creek), Choctaw, Chickasaw, Seminole — were removed to Indian Territory, where they **rebuilt their governments, courts, newspapers, and schools**. (Sources: NPS Trail of Tears NHT — via ch03 bank; New Georgia Encyclopedia.)

### The Seminole resistance
- **Second Seminole War, 1835–1842** — the longest and costliest of the U.S. Indian wars in the East. **Osceola** was seized on **October 21, 1837** when Gen. Thomas Jesup lured him in under a white flag of truce — a ploy publicly condemned at the time; he died a prisoner at **Fort Moultrie, South Carolina, January 30, 1838**. Fighting faded after 1842, but **a few hundred Seminoles stayed in the Everglades and never surrendered**; their descendants — the Seminole Tribe of Florida (federally recognized 1957) and the Miccosukee Tribe (federally recognized 1962) — note that the Seminole were never conquered. (Sources: Britannica "Osceola"; NPS Fort Sumter/Fort Moultrie "Osceola"; Fort King Heritage Foundation; kiddle/Miccosukee history cross-checked with FIU law review on the Seminole Tribe.)

## 7. 1850 to 1900

### The wars, from the nations' side (campaign detail is `war`'s)
- **U.S.–Dakota War, 1862 (Minnesota):** six weeks of war after the U.S. failed to deliver the food and payments its treaties promised. Military commissions tried 392 Dakota men — some trials under five minutes, no defense lawyers, no interpreters. Lincoln reviewed the transcripts and cut the death list to 39 (one reprieved). **38 Dakota men were hanged at Mankato on December 26, 1862 — the largest mass execution in U.S. history**; it later emerged two men were hanged by mistake (one had answered to another man's name; one had been acquitted). (Sources: Minnesota Historical Society "The Trials & Hanging"; Death Penalty Information Center; Wikipedia "1862 Mankato mass execution" cross-checked.)
- **Sand Creek massacre, November 29, 1864 (Colorado):** about 675 U.S. volunteer troops under Col. John Chivington attacked Black Kettle's Cheyenne and Arapaho camp — a camp flying a U.S. flag and a white flag after being told it was under military protection; about **230 people killed** over eight hours, mostly women, children, and elders. (Sources: Britannica; EBSCO; History Colorado; NPS Sand Creek Massacre NHS materials.)
- **Red Cloud's War → Treaty of Fort Laramie, 1868:** the treaty closed the Bozeman Trail, and established the **Great Sioux Reservation** — all of present South Dakota west of the Missouri including the Black Hills — "for the absolute and undisturbed use" of the Lakota. (Sources: World History Encyclopedia "Red Cloud's War"; National Archives 1868 treaty exhibit.)
- **1874:** Custer's expedition announced gold in the Black Hills; the government ignored the 1868 treaty; December 1875 ultimatum ordered the winter camps onto the reservation by January 31, 1876, then sent the army. (Sources: NPS "Fighting for the Black Hills"; History.com.)
- **Little Bighorn (Greasy Grass), June 25, 1876:** the allied Lakota, Cheyenne, and Arapaho camp — Sitting Bull its center, Crazy Horse a battle leader — destroyed Custer's immediate command; about 200 died with Custer within an hour, five companies of the 7th Cavalry. (Sources: Britannica; History.com; EBSCO.)
- **After:** Crazy Horse surrendered May 6, 1877 and was bayoneted to death at Fort Robinson **September 5, 1877** while being arrested; Sitting Bull took his people to Canada in 1877 and, starving after the bison collapse, surrendered at Fort Buford in **July 1881**; the Nez Perce under Chief Joseph and other chiefs fled about **1,170 miles** (NPS trail figure; some accounts give up to ~1,700 — state the trail figure) toward Canada in 1877 and surrendered October 5, 1877, 40 miles short; **Geronimo** (Chiricahua Apache) surrendered **September 4, 1886** — the last Native leader to formally surrender to the United States; the Chiricahua were held as prisoners of war for 27 years. (Sources: History.com "Crazy Horse killed"; Britannica "Sitting Bull"; U.S. Army "The Nez Perce War of 1877"; LOC Today in History Oct 5; History.com "Geronimo surrenders"; ICT.)
- **The Long Walk of the Navajo, 1863–64** — about 8,500 people marched 300+ miles to Bosque Redondo and held until 1868 (VERIFIED in ch03 bank; the confinement/injustice side parked in ch17 bank → rights-movements). Here: the Navajo negotiated the 1868 treaty that took them HOME — nearly unique among removed nations. (Sources: Smithsonian NMAI "The Long Walk" via ch03/ch17 banks.)

### The bison collapse and the end of treaty-making
- Roughly **30 million bison** on the Plains in the early 1800s; **fewer than 1,000 by 1900** (one 1884 count: 325). Railroads, industrial hide-hunting, disease, drought — and army encouragement, because the herds were the Plains nations' food supply, economy, and religion. (Sources: Environment & Society Portal "Near Extinction of the Great Plains Bison"; Smithsonian "Plains Bison"; NPS "Where the Buffalo Roamed.")
- **1871:** a rider on the Indian Appropriations Act ended U.S. treaty-making with Native nations (existing treaties stayed in force — agreements after 1871 came as acts and executive orders). (Sources: Colorado Encyclopedia "Indian Appropriations Act (1871)"; National Archives Records of Rights.)

### Allotment — the Dawes Act
- **Dawes (General Allotment) Act, 1887:** broke communal tribal land into individual parcels and sold the "surplus." Native landholdings fell from about **138 million acres in 1887 to 48 million by 1934** — about **90 million acres, two-thirds of the land base, lost**. Ended by the Indian Reorganization Act, 1934. (Sources: Indian Land Tenure Foundation; ICT "The 1887 Dawes Act"; Meriam Report's own figures — 137M→47M — via Britannica "Indian Reorganization Act"; use "about 90 million acres lost" and note the two acre-counts differ by a million or so between sources.)

### The boarding schools — THIS CHAPTER LEADS (director ruling 4/7)
- **Carlisle Indian Industrial School** opened **November 1, 1879** in Carlisle, Pennsylvania, founded by Army officer **Richard Henry Pratt**, who had run an education program for Native prisoners. First federal off-reservation boarding school; model for the system. (Sources: Carlisle Indian School Digital Resource Center (Dickinson College); NABS; OU "Exiled to Indian Country.")
- **The phrase, exactly:** Pratt, 1892, in a speech printed in the *Official Report of the Nineteenth Annual Conference of Charities and Correction* (the 1892 national conference, held in Denver): "A great general has said that the only good Indian is a dead one... In a sense, I agree with the sentiment, but only in this: that all the Indian there is in the race should be dead. **Kill the Indian in him, and save the man.**" It is often misquoted as "kill the Indian, save the man." (Sources: Carlisle Indian School Digital Resource Center full-text PDF; History Matters (GMU); NABS.)
- **How the taking worked:** in **1891** Congress authorized the Commissioner of Indian Affairs to make and enforce rules to compel school attendance of Native children; in **1893** Congress authorized withholding **rations, clothing, and annuities** from parents who refused to send children. Some agents used reservation police to seize children. Some families consented, hoping schooling would arm their children; many had no real choice. (Sources: Native Philanthropy timeline "Compulsory attendance law"; LegalClarity "U.S. Indian Boarding School Policy"; PBS "Legacy of Trauma.")
- **On arrival:** hair cut, clothing replaced with uniforms, an English name assigned, home language forbidden. Punishments for speaking one's language are documented in survivor accounts and the federal investigation (see 2000–today): beatings, solitary confinement, withholding food, mouths washed with soap (Chester Nez's own account — era 8). (Sources: DOI Federal Indian Boarding School Initiative Investigative Report Vol. 1, 2022; Nez memoir.)
- **The "outing system":** Pratt's program placing students with white families to work — summers and school terms — as a deliberate assimilation tool. (Source: Carlisle Indian School Digital Resource Center.)
- **Half-day labor:** school days split between classroom and unpaid work that ran the institutions (documented in the Meriam Report, 1928 — era 8, and DOI report).
- **Scale (from the federal investigation, 2022/2024):** the U.S. operated or supported **417 federal Indian boarding schools** (Vol. 2 count; Vol. 1 had counted 408) across **37 states or then-territories**, 1819–1969, plus over 1,000 other institutions with similar aims outside the strict criteria. At least **973 children died** at the schools (documented deaths; the report says the true number is certainly higher); at least **74 marked and unmarked burial sites at 65 schools**. (Sources: DOI Federal Indian Boarding School Initiative Investigative Report Vols. 1 (May 2022) & 2 (July 30, 2024); BIA; NABS.)
- **The other half of the truth:** children kept languages alive in secret, formed friendships across nations (one root of 20th-century pan-Indian organizing), and some — Zitkala-Ša, Jim Thorpe's generation, the Society of American Indians founders — turned the schooling against the policy. (Sources: Zitkala-Ša's published essays; SAI history via EBSCO.)

### Wounded Knee and the Ghost Dance
- **Wovoka**, a Northern Paiute man from the Walker Lake country of Nevada, had a vision during the solar eclipse of **January 1, 1889**: live ethically, dance the round dance, and the dead would return and the world be made whole. The **Ghost Dance** spread across the exhausted, hungry reservations; frightened agents and sensational newspapers called it an uprising. (Sources: Britannica "Wovoka"; EBSCO; PBS American Experience "The Lakota Ghost Dance.")
- **Sitting Bull was killed December 15, 1890** at Standing Rock when Indian police came to arrest him amid the Ghost Dance panic. (Sources: Britannica; History.com.)
- **Wounded Knee, December 29, 1890:** the 7th Cavalry, disarming Spotted Elk (Big Foot)'s band on Wounded Knee Creek, opened fire with carbines and Hotchkiss guns; **between 250 and 300 Lakota were killed**, most of them women and children; 25 soldiers died, mostly from their own crossfire (crossfire point per Britannica/History accounts — attribute). (Sources: Equal Justice Initiative calendar; Britannica "Wounded Knee Massacre"; LOC classroom materials.)
- **The nadir, and the pivot the chapter must make:** the 1900 census counted about **250,000** Native people in the U.S. — the population low point. The chapter does NOT end here; era 8 carries straight through. (Sources: USAFacts census history; Wikipedia "Native Americans in the United States" census table cross-checked.)

## 8. 1900 to 1950

### The frontier "closes" — the nations do not
- This is the era the rest of the book most easily drops Native people. This chapter carries them straight through: organizing, the Osage boom, citizenship, the Indian New Deal, two world wars, and the schools still running.

### Organizing — the first national Native-run movements
- **Society of American Indians, founded October 12, 1911, Columbus, Ohio** — the first national American Indian rights organization developed and run by Native people themselves; its leaders ("Red Progressives" — doctors, lawyers, writers, many of them boarding-school alumni) included Dr. Charles Eastman (Santee Dakota) and Dr. Carlos Montezuma (Yavapai-Apache); Zitkala-Ša edited its journal. Pioneered 20th-century pan-Indianism. Active 1911–1923. (Sources: EBSCO "Society of American Indians"; Encyclopedia.com; OSU SAI centennial.)
- **Alaska Native Brotherhood, 1912 (Sitka)** — founded by twelve men and one woman to win citizenship for Alaska Natives; with the Alaska Native Sisterhood (1915), the only civil-rights organizations for Alaska Natives for half a century. (Sources: EBSCO; Alaska Public Media.) — Elizabeth Peratrovich's 1945 anti-discrimination fight PARKED in rights-movements.
- **National Congress of American Indians, founded November 16, 1944, Denver** — 80 delegates from more than 50 tribes and associations; still the major intertribal body today. (Sources: NCAI official history; Smithsonian NMAI archives.)

### The Osage and the Reign of Terror
- Under the **Osage Allotment Act of 1906** the Osage kept their mineral rights collectively — oil royalties paid per-capita "headrights" — and in the 1920s the Osage were among the wealthiest people per capita in the world. Congress's 1921 guardianship law put many Osage adults' money under white "guardians" appointed by local courts; guardians and husbands stole, and **from about 1920 to 1925 more than 60 Osage headright holders died in murders and suspicious deaths** — the **Reign of Terror**. The new Bureau of Investigation's case on the Kyle family murders (Mollie Burkhart's family) became one of the FBI's first big cases. Our angle: a nation's wealth, and the legal machinery built to take it. Investigation/FBI angle parked in crime-justice. (Sources: Osage Nation official "Did You Know? Osage Murders"; History.com "How Marriage and Murder Were Used to Steal Osage Oil Riches"; Voices of Oklahoma.) **Film:** *Killers of the Flower Moon* (2023, dir. Martin Scorsese) tells the Burkhart story; Lily Gladstone played Mollie Burkhart (see era 10).

### Citizenship and the Indian New Deal
- **Indian Citizenship Act, June 2, 1924** — citizenship for all Native people born in the U.S.; but voting was state law, and some states barred Native voters **until 1957** (LOC's figure — attribute). (Source: Library of Congress Today in History, June 2.)
- **Meriam Report, 1928** ("The Problem of Indian Administration") — a federal survey that documented the failure of allotment (tribal land down from 137 to 47 million acres) and the poverty, disease, and grim school conditions of the era; it drove the reform demands of the 1930s. (Sources: Britannica "Indian Reorganization Act"; National Archives Prologue "Indian New Deal.")
- **Indian Reorganization Act, June 18, 1934** (Commissioner John Collier, 1933–45): **ended allotment**, extended trust protections, funded land recovery, and let nations adopt constitutions and charter governments — the pivot from breaking tribes up to recognizing them. (Sources: Britannica; EBSCO; National Archives Prologue.)

### The schools continue
- The system did not stop in 1900 — the federal count runs to 1969, and enrollment kept growing for decades (it peaked around **60,000 in 1973** — era 9). The Meriam Report condemned the schools in detail in 1928; under Collier in the 1930s policy shifted toward day schools and some teaching in Native languages, and some boarding schools closed — but tens of thousands of children were still taken from home each year. (Sources: DOI investigative reports; PWNA "The 1960–1970s: Native American Education History" (60,000/1973); Britannica/NARA on Collier reforms.)
- **The documented irony, by name:** **Chester Nez** — punished at the Fort Defiance boarding school for speaking Navajo (his mouth washed with soap), then recruited in 1942 to build a military code out of Navajo. His memoir: *Code Talker* (2011, with Judith Schiess Avila). (Sources: Library of Congress Veterans History Project profile "Chester Nez"; America Comes Alive; the memoir itself.)

### The code talkers
- **First code talkers — Choctaw, World War I:** in the Meuse-Argonne campaign, with Germans tapping phone lines and capturing one in four runners, Choctaw soldiers of the 36th Division began transmitting in Choctaw — first secure messages **October 26, 1918**; devised code words for military terms ("little gun shoot fast" = machine gun); credited in the St. Etienne and Forest Ferme fighting. (Sources: Oklahoma Historical Society "Code Talkers"; National WWI Museum "America's First Code Talkers"; Choctaw Nation.)
- **Navajo code talkers, World War II:** the original **29 recruits** arrived at San Diego **May 5, 1942** and built the code; **350–420** Navajo served as code talkers across all six Marine divisions in the Pacific. At Iwo Jima the 5th Division signal officer, Maj. Howard Connor, said: "Were it not for the Navajos, the Marines would never have taken Iwo Jima." The code was never broken; it stayed classified until **1968**; Congressional Gold Medals to the original 29 (silver to the rest) were presented **July 26, 2001**. (Sources: Naval History and Heritage Command fact sheet; National WWII Museum; U.S. Mint; National Archives Prologue "Semper Fidelis, Code Talkers.")

### Featured people — VERIFIED

(Sources: ESPN July 2022; World Athletics; U.S. Olympic & Paralympic Hall of Fame; Oklahoma Historical Society. ## 9. 1950 to 2000

### Termination and Relocation
- **House Concurrent Resolution 108, August 1, 1953** — declared the policy of ending federal recognition and services ("termination"). From 1953 to 1970 Congress ran about **60 termination proceedings** affecting **more than 100 tribes and bands**; over **3 million acres** left trust status. (Sources: EBSCO "Termination Resolution"; National Archives BIA termination records; American Archive of Public Broadcasting.)
- **Menominee (Wisconsin):** termination act 1954 (effective 1961) brought the prosperous timber nation to poverty; the DRUMS movement led by Ada Deer won the **Menominee Restoration Act, signed December 22, 1973** — the turning of the tide. (Menominee/DRUMS/Ada Deer detail: Milwaukee Public Museum "Menominee Termination and Restoration"; restoration act date: BIA press release, Wikipedia cross-checked. Ada Deer's name appears in MPM's account — she belongs to any prose telling.)
- **Relocation:** BIA program (begun ~1948–1952, backed by the Indian Relocation Act of 1956) moved Native people to cities with one-way tickets and short-term help — about **30,000 people in the 1950s**, **more than 100,000 by the program's end in the 1970s**. Poverty followed many; so did **urban Indian communities** — Minneapolis, Chicago, Oakland/San Francisco, Los Angeles, Denver — where the next movement was born. Movement-of-people detail parked in migration. (Sources: Britannica "Urban Indian Relocation Program"; APM Reports "Uprooted"; National Archives "Urban Relocation.")

### Red Power
- **American Indian Movement (AIM), founded July 1968, Minneapolis** — George Mitchell, Dennis Banks, Clyde Bellecourt and about 200 others; began with patrols against police abuse in the city relocation built. (Sources: Britannica; MNopedia; History.com.)
- **Occupation of Alcatraz, November 20, 1969 – June 11, 1971** (19 months) — "Indians of All Tribes," Richard Oakes (Mohawk) among the leaders; the proclamation offered to buy the island for "$24 in glass beads and red cloth." Widely counted the spark of the Red Power era; Nixon ended termination policy in its wake. (Sources: NPS "Alcatraz Occupation"; Zinn Education Project; American Yawp reader.)
- **Blue Lake returned to Taos Pueblo, December 15, 1970** — 48,000 acres of sacred land returned when Nixon signed H.R. 471, the signal that "forced termination" was over; **ANCSA, December 18, 1971** — Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act: **44 million acres and $962.5 million**, settled through 12 regional (later 13) and over 200 village corporations — Alaska Natives as landowners and shareholders, a different model of nationhood (territories thread). (Sources: NARA Text Message "Righting a Wrong"; Taos Pueblo; BLM "ANCSA at 50"; ANCSA Regional Association.)
- **Wounded Knee occupation, February 27 – May 8, 1973 (71 days)** — about 200 Oglala Lakota and AIM members held the village of Wounded Knee against marshals and the FBI, protesting the Pine Ridge tribal chairman and broken treaties; two occupiers died. Say plainly: the site was chosen because of 1890. (Sources: SDPB; NLM Native Voices timeline; U.S. Marshals history.)

### The fish-ins and the Boldt decision
- Nisqually, Puyallup, and other Northwest nations held **"fish-ins"** through the 1960s–70s, fishing in open defiance of state arrests to force the 1850s treaties into court; **Frank's Landing** on the Nisqually was the center. **United States v. Washington (the Boldt decision), February 12, 1974:** Judge George Boldt ruled the treaties entitle the tribes to **half the harvestable salmon** and made the tribes co-managers of the fishery — affirmed by the Supreme Court in 1979 (HistoryLink). (Sources: HistoryLink "Boldt Decision"; Northwest Public Broadcasting 50th-anniversary coverage; Cascade PBS.)

### The self-determination laws (the turn, in statute)
- **Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act (P.L. 93-638), signed January 1975** — nations contract to run the federal programs themselves — schools, clinics, police; "Education" is in the title because the schools were the heart of it. (Sources: Wikipedia/UAF Tribal Governance; EBSCO; signed by President Ford.)
- **Indian Child Welfare Act, 1978** — after decades in which agencies removed a large share of Native children to non-Native homes, ICWA set federal standards keeping Native children with Native families and gave tribes jurisdiction. (Sources: NICWA "What is ICWA"; Cornell LII.)
- **Indian Gaming Regulatory Act, October 17, 1988** — the legal frame for tribal gaming (three classes, tribal-state compacts, National Indian Gaming Commission); gaming became a major tribal revenue source (scale varies enormously nation to nation — say so). (Sources: NIGC; Congress.gov S.555.)
- **NAGPRA, November 16, 1990** — Native remains and sacred objects in museums and federal collections belong to their descendants and nations; repatriation becomes law. (Sources: NPS NAGPRA; BIA.)

### Nations take the schools back
- **Navajo Community College, 1968** — the first tribally controlled college, founded by the Navajo Nation (Tsaile, Arizona); today **Diné College**; dozens of tribal colleges followed. (Sources: fiveable/UAF summaries citing the founding; Diné College history.)
- **Kennedy Report, November 1969** — Senate Report 91-501, *Indian Education: A National Tragedy — A National Challenge* (Special Subcommittee on Indian Education, begun under Robert F. Kennedy, finished under Edward Kennedy): found federal Indian policy was "coercive assimilation" with "disastrous effects," and recommended Native control of Native education. (Sources: ERIC ED034625; NABS resource database; NARF NILL.)
- Boarding-school enrollment **peaked about 60,000 in 1973**, then fell as nations took schools over under P.L. 93-638 or closed them; a handful still operate today under tribal/BIE control. Survivors began publishing their accounts (Fortunate Eagle below; many oral-history projects since). (Sources: PWNA education history; DOI report Vol. 1 (system winds down after 1969).)

## 10. 2000 to Today (current through 2026)

### The state of the nations
- **574 federally recognized tribal nations** (BIA annual list; CRS report R47414), plus state-recognized nations, nations still seeking recognition, more than 200 Alaska Native villages and their corporations, and Native Hawaiians (no federally recognized government; territories thread — shared with america-world).
- **9.7 million people** identified as American Indian or Alaska Native, alone or in combination, in the **2020 census** (2.9% of the U.S.) — from the ~250,000 counted in 1900. (Sources: USAFacts; Census 2020 figures via Wikipedia cross-check.)
- **Navajo Nation:** largest reservation — more than **27,000 square miles** across Arizona, New Mexico, and Utah. **Cherokee Nation:** most citizens — passed **450,000 enrolled citizens** (announced 2023; 475,000+ by some 2024–25 counts). (Sources: New Mexico Tourism/Britannica Kids for Navajo; Cherokee Nation announcements via ICT/Native News Online.)
- **National Museum of the American Indian** opened on the National Mall **September 21, 2004**; the opening procession of some 25,000 Native people from across the hemisphere was the largest such gathering in D.C. history. (Sources: Smithsonian Institution Archives; AHA Perspectives.)

### Sovereignty in court and in office
- ***McGirt v. Oklahoma*, July 9, 2020** (5–4, opinion by Justice Gorsuch, which opens: **"On the far end of the Trail of Tears was a promise."**): the Muscogee (Creek) reservation in eastern Oklahoma was never disestablished — for federal criminal law, some 3.25 million acres including much of Tulsa remain reservation land; follow-on rulings extended the logic to the other Five Tribes' reservations. (Sources: Supreme Court slip opinion 18-9526; History.com; Oklahoma Bar Association primer.)
- ***Haaland v. Brackeen*, June 15, 2023** (7–2): the Supreme Court upheld ICWA against constitutional challenge. (Sources: Supreme Court 21-376; NARF; ABA.)
- **Deb Haaland (Laguna Pueblo):** 2018 — she and **Sharice Davids (Ho-Chunk)** became the **first Native American women elected to Congress**; **March 15, 2021** — confirmed Secretary of the Interior, the **first Native American cabinet secretary**, overseeing the very department that ran the boarding schools. (Sources: CNN Nov 2018; NBC/19th News March 2021.)

### Standing Rock and land back
- **Standing Rock, 2016–17:** the Standing Rock Sioux opposed the Dakota Access Pipeline's crossing under Lake Oahe, their water supply, upstream of the reservation. Camps from April 2016 (Oceti Sakowin); **youth carried the message on foot — a 500-mile relay to the Army Corps in Omaha and a 2,000-mile run to Washington, D.C.**; thousands came, the largest gathering of Native nations in more than a century (widely stated — attribute to coverage); **December 4, 2016** the Army Corps halted the easement; more than **2,000 U.S. military veterans** arrived that week to stand with the camps; the next administration approved the easement in early 2017 and oil moved later that year — the tribe's suits continued. Both truths stated plainly: the pipeline runs, and the nations showed what standing together now looks like. (Sources: ICT "Dakota Access Pipeline: Timeline"; EBSCO; NPR/CNN veterans coverage; Native News Online youth-run coverage.) **Film:** *Awake, a Dream from Standing Rock* (2017, dirs. Josh Fox, James Spione, Myron Dewey). (Sources: Wikipedia/Letterboxd/Netflix.)
- **Land back, concrete cases:** the **National Bison Range** (18,800 acres, Flathead Reservation, Montana) — taken for a federal refuge in 1908 — was restored to the **Confederated Salish and Kootenai Tribes** by law in December 2020, with full management assumed 2022. (Sources: DOI press release; Flathead Beacon; Native News Online.)

### The boarding-school reckoning
- **Federal Indian Boarding School Initiative** (announced by Secretary Haaland, June 2021). **Vol. 1 (May 2022):** 408 federal schools identified, marked/unmarked burial sites at 53 schools. **Vol. 2 (July 30, 2024, final):** **417 schools in 37 states or then-territories; at least 973 documented child deaths; at least 74 burial sites at 65 schools; 1,025 additional institutions** outside the four federal criteria. The report says the death count is certainly an undercount. (Sources: DOI/BIA report pages; NICWA; EJI summary.)
- **October 25, 2024:** President Biden, at the **Gila River Indian Community, Arizona** (Ira Hayes's home community), issued the first formal presidential apology for the boarding-school system, calling it "a sin on our soul." (Sources: CBS/PBS/Washington Post coverage of the apology.)
- **The children coming home:** the Army has been disinterring and returning children buried at the Carlisle Barracks cemetery — **2017:** Little Chief and Horse returned to the Northern Arapaho (Little Plume's grave, when opened, held two other children — he was found and returned the next year); **July 2021:** nine Rosebud Sioux children returned home to South Dakota, escorted by Rosebud youth. (Sources: Philadelphia Inquirer coverage 2017 & 2021; NABS "Carlisle Repatriation.")

### Languages come home — the direct answer to the boarding schools; END THE SCHOOL THREAD HERE
- **Wôpanâak (Wampanoag), Massachusetts:** no native speakers since the mid-1800s; **Jessie Little Doe Baird** co-founded the **Wôpanâak Language Reclamation Project (1993)**, reconstructed the language from documents (including the 1663 Eliot Bible printed in Wôpanâak), earned an MIT linguistics degree, raised her daughter in the language — the first time an American language with no living speakers has been brought back to living use. MacArthur Fellowship, 2010. (Sources: MacArthur Foundation fellow page; MIT SHASS; PBS Independent Lens.)
- **Hawaiian:** **Pūnana Leo** immersion preschools from **1984** (Kekaha, Kaua'i) — the first Indigenous-language immersion preschools in the U.S.; the 1896-era ban on teaching in Hawaiian was lifted in **1986**; first public immersion classes 1987; Hawaiian-medium education now runs preschool through graduate school (attribute the arc to ʻAha Pūnana Leo). (Sources: ʻAha Pūnana Leo official history; Ka Wai Ola; Cultural Survival.)
- Cherokee, Lakota, Diné, and many other nations run immersion schools; tie to the code talkers: the languages the schools punished helped win a world war and are now taught to children on purpose. (General framing; specific programs above are the verified anchors.)

## Acoma Pueblo, December 1598 – February 1599 (VERIFIED 2026-09-07; the `[VERIFY]` tag from the 2026-08-07 `migration` park is resolved — what remains disputed is stated below and named to its disputants)

**Status of the earlier park.** `migration` era 02 features Oñate's 1598 colony march up the Camino Real (sourced in `research/research-migration.md`: NPS Camino Real materials; Texas State Historical Association) and hands the Acoma story here. That park carried `[VERIFY]` on the death toll, the trial and the sentences, and the chapter writer therefore left the whole episode out of `manuscript/native-nations/part1-before-1800.md`. This section replaces the park. **Nothing below is settled by picking a figure.** Where the sources disagree, the disagreement is written down with the names attached.

### 0. What kind of record this is — read before using any number

- The detailed narrative of the fighting comes from **one Spanish participant's epic poem**: Gaspar Pérez de Villagrá, *Historia de la Nueva México* (Alcalá, 1610), 34 cantos modeled on Virgil's *Aeneid*, written within about eleven years of the events by a captain who was there. It is the account historians of the Southwest have leaned on, and it is a poem written to praise the expedition. (Sources: University of California, Berkeley, English Dept., on Genaro Padilla, *The Daring Flight of My Pen: Cultural Politics and Gaspar Pérez de Villagrá's Historia de la Nueva Mexico, 1610*; Columbia University "World Epics" project entry on *Historia de la Nueva México*; National Humanities Center, "American Beginnings" primary-source set.)
- The legal record — the testimony, the charges and the sentence — survives in Spanish colonial documents translated in **George P. Hammond and Agapito Rey, eds. and trans., *Don Juan de Oñate: Colonizer of New Mexico, 1595–1628*, 2 vols. (University of New Mexico Press, 1953)**, in the documents titled "Trial of the Indians of Acoma" and "Conviction of Oñate and his Captains, 1614." **I could not open these volumes.** Every quotation of the sentence below is quoted here as a secondary source quotes them, and is labeled as such.
- **The Acoma kept their own account by telling it.** The *New York Times* reported in 1998 that since 1599 the Acoma had passed from generation to generation the account of Oñate ordering the right feet of 24 captive men cut off (James Brooke, "Conquistador Statue Stirs Hispanic Pride and Indian Rage," Feb. 9, 1998 — quoted via the citation trail in Wikipedia's "1599 Acoma massacre"; nytimes.com could not be fetched from this machine). Acoma's account of what started it is in §1.
- **No count of the Acoma dead was made by anyone at the time.** The Spanish record counts prisoners, not bodies. That is why §2 is a spread and not a number.

### 1. The sequence — December 1598 and January 1599

- **Spring 1598:** Juan de Oñate leads a colonizing expedition north from Mexico into what the Spanish called New Mexico. (NPS, "Acoma Pueblo: Ancient City in the Sky.")
- **Before the killing, Acoma had already been made a Spanish subject on paper.** Oñate had gathered the Acoma elders in his ceremony of submission — the "Act of Obedience and Vassalage by the Indians of Acoma." The Spanish afterward treated Acoma as bound by Spanish law, and used that submission to argue the later war was legal. (Juan Velarde, "Act of Obedience and Vassalage by the Indians of Acoma," in Hammond & Rey 1953, p. 356, cited in Rebecca Carlson, "Don Juan de Oñate's Prosecution for 'Crimes and Excesses' in the Provinces of New Mexico, 1614," senior thesis, Western Oregon University, 2008, pp. 17–18.)
- **Early December 1598:** Oñate's nephew **Juan de Zaldívar** arrives at Acoma with about thirty soldiers and takes some of them up the mesa to demand food. (EBSCO Research Starters, "Battle of Acoma"; NPS, "Acoma Pueblo: Ancient City in the Sky.")
  - **The date:** "In December 1598" (NPS); **December 4, 1598** (EBSCO/Wikipedia chronology); **December 1, 1598** (EBSCO Research Starters, "Ácoma Pueblo"). Sources give December 1 and December 4 for the arrival; the fighting is placed on or within days of the arrival.
  - **What the Spanish were doing there, in the Pueblos' own account:** the soldiers "had demanded food and supplies, assaulted an Acoma woman, and forced allegiance to the Spanish crown." (All Pueblo Council of Governors, press release, Oct. 6, 2023, hosted on puebloofacoma.org.) The Rio Grande Sun reported the same Acoma account — that the soldiers forcefully demanded supplies and raped a woman ("Fiesta Historians Downplay Acoma Massacre," 2019; the article would not load directly and is quoted here from a search result). **This detail is in the Pueblos' account and is not in the Spanish-derived summaries;** the director should decide how an 8–15 book states it.
- **The Acoma kill Zaldívar and part of his party.** The number of Spanish dead differs by source: **eleven** of his men plus Zaldívar (Andrew Knaut, *The Pueblo Revolt of 1680*, University of Oklahoma Press, 1995, p. 69, as cited in Wikipedia; and Texas State Historical Association, "Oñate, Juan de," which says "eleven colonists"); **twelve** soldiers dead including Zaldívar (NPR, John Burnett, July 13, 2020; All Pueblo Council of Governors, Oct. 6, 2023; Carlson 2008, p. 19); **twelve of his men plus Zaldívar, thirteen in all** (EBSCO, "Battle of Acoma"; PBS POV, *The Last Conquistador*, background page). **NPS says "him and fourteen of his men."** So: **between eleven and fourteen Spaniards, plus Zaldívar in most tellings.** State the range.
- **Oñate holds a formal inquiry first.** On **December 28, 1598** he opened judicial proceedings that ran more than two weeks, took testimony from survivors, and asked the Franciscan friars to rule on whether a war against Acoma would be just. The friars answered that he "possessed both the authority and sufficient cause." (Carlson 2008, p. 19, citing Marc Simmons, *The Last Conquistador: Juan de Oñate and the Settling of the Far Southwest*, University of Oklahoma Press, 1991, p. 138.)
- **January 1599: the punitive force.** Oñate sends Juan de Zaldívar's brother **Vicente de Zaldívar** with about **seventy** soldiers. (John L. Kessell, *Pueblos, Spaniards, and the Kingdom of New Mexico*, University of Oklahoma Press, 2008 — publisher's description; NPS, "Acoma Pueblo," gives "a force of seventy Spanish soldiers.")
  - **Dates differ.** Force dispatched **January 12, 1599** and reaching Acoma **January 21** (compilation at usdeadlyevents.com, citing its listed works); Zaldívar **reached Acoma January 21, 1599** (EBSCO, "Battle of Acoma"; Intermountain Histories, Northern Arizona University, entry by Edgar I. Bernal Sevilla, citing Matthew Liebmann, *Revolt: An Archaeological History of Pueblo Resistance and Revitalization in 17th Century New Mexico*, University of Arizona Press, 2012). But the **New Mexico Office of the State Historian** page "Pueblo of Acoma" says Zaldívar **arrived at Acoma on January 12, 1599**, asked three times for surrender, got arrows each time, and attacked the next morning (quoted from a search result; newmexicohistory.org did not resolve on 2026-09-07).
  - **Length of the fighting:** **three days** (NM Office of the State Historian; EBSCO/Wikipedia chronology, Jan. 22–24); **two days** (EBSCO, "Battle of Acoma": "after two days of bitter fighting"); "fighting raged all of the next day" with the deaths dated January 22 (Intermountain Histories).
- **The pueblo is stormed and burned.** The Spanish scaled the mesa, sacked and burned much of the pueblo. (NPS, "Acoma Pueblo: Ancient City in the Sky"; NPR, July 13, 2020, which reports Oñate declared a war "by fire and blood.")
- **Prisoners taken:** "some five hundred men, women, and children were captured" (EBSCO, "Battle of Acoma"); **507** put on trial (National Library of Medicine, "Native Voices" timeline entry, which however dates the trial to 1598); "500 captives" (Sherry Robinson column, Oct. 14, 2023).

### 3. The trial and the sentences, February 1599

- **Where.** The captives were taken to **Santo Domingo Pueblo (Kewa)** and tried there before Oñate (Simmons, *The Last Conquistador*, 1991, p. 145, cited in Carlson 2008, p. 19). **Wikipedia says the trial was held at San Juan Pueblo**, and the National Library of Medicine's "Native Voices" entry names **Ohkay Owingeh** as where enslaved Acoma women were held. **The location is not settled across sources.**
- **When.** The trial "began the very day the Acoma arrived, **February 9, 1599**," and "lasted three days" (Carlson 2008, p. 19, citing Simmons). Other sources put the decree on **February 12, 1599** (EBSCO, "Battle of Acoma"; Intermountain Histories). Those are compatible if the sentence was pronounced at the end of a trial that opened February 9. The NLM entry dates the whole thing to **1598**, which conflicts with every other source.
- **Oñate appointed a defense attorney**, who asked for clemency on the ground that the accused were "uncivilized" and so lacked sufficient reason (Carlson 2008, p. 19, citing Simmons p. 145). Captain **Alonso Gómez Montesinos** is named as the Acoma defender in some accounts (Wikipedia, uncited at that sentence).
- **The Acoma who testified** were asked why they attacked and why they refused the peace offered afterward. Many said they had been working in their fields during the first fight; on the refusal, "some wanted to surrender but some refused, and so they fought." (Carlson 2008, pp. 19–20, quoting Juan Gutiérrez Bocanegra and Juan de Oñate, "Trial of the Indians of Acoma," in Hammond & Rey 1953, pp. 464–467.)

**The sentence Oñate pronounced.** There were no death sentences. Reported wording, as secondary sources quote the trial record: **"The males who are over 25 years of age, I sentence to have one foot cut off"** — and to twenty years of personal servitude. (Quoted from a document signed by Oñate in the myText CNM open textbook and in Intermountain Histories; the underlying document is in Hammond & Rey 1953, which I could not open. Carlson 2008, p. 20, gives it as "one foot cut off and … twenty years of servitude." AP, NPR, the NYT in 1998 and the All Pueblo Council of Governors all say **right** foot.)

| Who | Sentence |
|---|---|
| **Men over 25** | one foot cut off, **and** twenty years of personal servitude (Carlson 2008 p. 20, citing the trial record; EBSCO; NLM Native Voices; Intermountain Histories) |
| **Males 12–25** | twenty years of servitude (Carlson; EBSCO; NLM) |
| **Females over 12** | twenty years of servitude (Carlson; EBSCO; NLM) |
| **Children under 12** | declared innocent and taken from their families, given to the friars (Carlson 2008 p. 20). **NPS: "Some 70 Acoma girls under the age of 12 were sent down El Camino Real and placed in convents across Mexico," and "in all probability, none ever returned home"** (NPS, "Traditional Groups along El Camino Real de Tierra Adentro"). **The All Pueblo Council of Governors says 60 Acoma children were removed and taken to Mexico, never to return** (Oct. 6, 2023). PBS POV adds that children were sent to Mexico to be raised by missionaries, "but some scholars believe they were eventually sold on the slave market." **The number is 60 or 70 depending on the source; both say none came home.** |
| **The elderly** | enslaved to the Plains Apache (EBSCO, "Battle of Acoma") |
| **Two Hopi (Moqui) men captured in the fighting** | right hand cut off, then released to carry word of the punishment to their own people (Carlson 2008, p. 20, citing Hammond & Rey 1953, p. 477; EBSCO) |

- **How many men the mutilation was applied to: 24.** This figure is everywhere — AP (Russell Contreras, June 27, 2020: Oñate's "order to have the right feet cut off of 24 captive tribal warriors"); NPR (July 13, 2020: "cut off a foot from 24 young men as a warning to other pueblos"); the NYT in 1998 reporting the Acoma oral tradition; Sherry Robinson's 2023 column. **Wikipedia states "Twenty-four men suffered amputation" with no footnote on that sentence, and I could not trace the number 24 to a document in any source I could reach.** It may come from the trial record via Hammond & Rey; I could not confirm that. **State it as the number the record is reported to give, and say who reports it.**

### 4. Was the mutilation carried out? — the scholarly dispute, stated

**That Oñate ordered it is not in dispute.** The order is in the trial record and it was one of the counts he was later convicted on (§5). What is disputed is what was then done to the men.

- **Marc Simmons** wrote in *The Last Conquistador* (University of Oklahoma Press, 1991) that the amputations took place. **He later came to doubt it.** **John L. Kessell** doubts it too. Both had seen a document describing Oñate planning to order severe punishments and then let the friars argue him down, so the friars would look merciful. Kessell's words as quoted: **"the historical record makes no mention of a one-footed Acoma slave. Cutting off a foot, after all, rendered a potential worker all but useless."** (Reported by Sherry Robinson, "Juan de Oñate is with us once again," column, Oct. 14, 2023, Carlsbad Current-Argus / New Mexico News Services — journalism quoting the two historians. I could not open Simmons's or Kessell's books.) Robinson's own conclusion: **"Nobody knows. The surviving records are thin."**
- **A separate claim: that the sentence meant the front of the foot, not the whole foot.** **Donald A. Chavez y Gilbert** argued in an **opinion piece** in the *Albuquerque Journal* ("An accurate accounting of the history of Oñate," 2017) that sixteenth-century Spanish "las puntas del pie" means the points or ends of the foot — the toes — and that Acoma oral tradition exaggerated over generations. **This is an op-ed by an advocate, not peer-reviewed history.** Wikipedia's footnotes for the toes reading are two letters to the editor (2002 and 2017) and a 2006 book by the same author. **Record who is arguing it and on what standing; do not present it as a scholarly finding.**
- **And at the far end, outright denial.** At the July 13, 2019 Santa Fe Fiesta history conference, **Al Borrego** said the massacre never happened and that there were only a few hundred Acoma in the area to begin with. **Matthew Martinez** (Ohkay Owingeh, then Lieutenant Governor) answered that revisionism of this kind can erase identity. (Rio Grande Sun, "Fiesta Historians Downplay Acoma Massacre," 2019 — the article would not load directly and is quoted from a search result. Label as journalism.)
- **What is solid:** the order, the ages, the twenty-year terms, the taking of the children, and Oñate's conviction for it. **What is not solid:** how many men were actually mutilated, whether the sentence was carried out in full, and whether the cut was the whole foot or the front of it. Two of the historians who worked longest in these documents say the record does not tell us.

### 5. Oñate's own fate

- He was relieved of the governorship and **left New Mexico in 1609** (Carlson 2008, p. 25; Oklahoma Historical Society, entry by Carl N. Tyson: "In 1609 he witnessed the founding of Santa Fe").
- **Called back to New Spain and arraigned on thirty charges.** Dates differ: arraigned **1612** and convicted **1614** (Carlson 2008, pp. 24–25); "in **1613** he finally faced charges" (Texas State Historical Association, "Oñate, Juan de," entry by Gerald F. Kozlowski, revised July 28, 2017); "traveled to Mexico City in **1613** to defend himself" (Oklahoma Historical Society).
- **Convicted on 12 charges, acquitted on 18.** The convictions included: **using excessive force against the Acoma**; unjustly hanging two Indians; murdering Captains Pablo de Aguilar and Alonso de Sosa de Albornoz; ordering his captains to kill two deserters; and lying to the viceroys and the king about the wealth of the land. He was acquitted of, among others, living immorally with married and unmarried women and ill treatment of the clergy. (Carlson 2008, pp. 24–25, tabulating Juan Benítez Camacho, "Conviction of Oñate and his Captains, 1614," in Hammond & Rey 1953, pp. 1109–1113.)
- **Sentence: banished from New Mexico for life, exiled from Mexico City, and fined 6,000 Castilian ducats.** The Mexico City term is given as **four years** by Carlson, TSHA and the Oklahoma Historical Society, and as **five years** by PBS POV. He also lost his titles as governor and captain general.
- **The fine did not hurt him.** He had rebuilt a fortune in the family mines at Zacatecas; Carlson calls the fine "inconsequential" (2008, p. 25).
- **What happened after is not settled.** Carlson says he was **pardoned in 1623** (2008, p. 27). TSHA says he "spent much of the rest of his life trying to clear his name, with some evident success," and that the king made him a **royal mining inspector**; other summaries say evidence of a pardon, **likely between 1622 and 1624, is inconclusive**. **State it as: he was convicted, banished and fined; he spent years working to have it undone; whether he was formally pardoned is not settled.**
- **Note for the writer:** the punishment came from Spain, for breaking Spanish law. Nobody at Acoma was asked, and nothing was returned.

### 6. The afterlife of this history — the statues, and Acoma's own words

**Journalism except where marked. Labeled as required.**

- **The statue.** A twelve-foot bronze equestrian Oñate by sculptor **Reynaldo "Sonny" Rivera** was put up at **Alcalde, New Mexico in 1994**, at what is now the Northern Rio Grande National Heritage Center; State Senator **Emilio Naranjo** raised **$2.4 million** in public funds for the project. (World Heritage USA, Monuments Toolkit, "Don Juan de Oñate Statues — Albuquerque and Alcalde, New Mexico.")
- **1998 — the foot.** The statue's **right foot** was cut off with an electric saw, "boot, stirrup, star-shaped spur and all," on the eve of the **cuartocentenario**, New Mexico's 400th-anniversary observance of Oñate's 1598 entrada. World Heritage USA dates the cutting to **December 29, 1997**, with a note left at the site reading **"Fair is fair."** On **January 7, 1998** an envelope reached the *Albuquerque Journal* containing typewritten pages and a Polaroid of the bronze boot. The senders called themselves **"Friends of Acoma."** Their statement, as quoted by 99% Invisible: **"We took the liberty of removing Oñate's right foot on behalf of our brothers and sisters at Acoma Pueblo. We will be melting this foot down and casting small medallions to be sold to those who are historically ignorant."** (The *New York Times*, Feb. 9, 1998, quotes it as "in behalf of our brothers and sisters of Acoma Pueblo" — the wording varies slightly across retellings.) **No one was ever identified, no medallions ever appeared, and the sculptor cast a replacement foot from the surviving left one.** (Sources: 99% Invisible, "Oñate's Foot," podcast/article — journalism; World Heritage USA Monuments Toolkit; James Brooke, NYT, Feb. 9, 1998, via citation trail.) The academic treatment of this episode is **Douglas Seefeldt, "Oñate's Foot: Histories, Landscapes, and Contested Memories in the Southwest," in *Across the Continent: Jefferson, Lewis and Clark, and the Making of America* (University of Virginia Press, 2005), pp. 169–209** — **the full text is behind a 403 and I could not read it;** cited here for the record, not quoted.
- **2017 — the paint.** On **September 8, 2017**, the Alcalde statue's **left boot was covered in red paint** and **"Remember 1680"** — the year of the Pueblo Revolt — was written at the Rio Arriba County Oñate Monument Resource and Visitors Center. It happened the same day as protests against Santa Fe's **Entrada** pageant, at which eight people were arrested. (*Albuquerque Journal*, "Rio Arriba county statue vandalized on day of the Entrada," Sept. 15, 2017 — the article would not load directly; quoted from a search result and corroborated for the year and the paint by the World Heritage USA Monuments Toolkit.)
- **2018 — the Entrada ends.** The Santa Fe Entrada reenactment was ended by **negotiated agreement** after the All Pueblo Council of Governors led "a series of diplomatic sessions among the stakeholders," with the parties agreeing "to cease reenactment of the Entrada and instead work toward reconciliation." (All Pueblo Council of Governors, Oct. 6, 2023 — tribal source, describing its own work.)
- **June 2020 — both statues come down.** In Albuquerque, demonstrators tried to pull down Rivera's **"La Jornada"** Oñate at the Albuquerque Museum with chains and a pickax; **a man was shot** during the confrontation on **June 15, 2020** (World Heritage USA names the wounded protester **Scott Williams** and the shooter **Steven Ray Baca**), and the city removed the statue to storage on June 16. Rio Arriba County removed the Alcalde statue ahead of a planned protest. (AP, Russell Contreras, June 27, 2020; NPR, John Burnett, July 13, 2020; World Heritage USA Monuments Toolkit.)
- **September 28, 2023 — Española.** At a prayer gathering over Rio Arriba County's plan to reinstall the Alcalde statue, **Ryan Martinez shot Jacob Johns**, an Indigenous man, in the abdomen. Martinez later pleaded no contest to aggravated battery with a deadly weapon and to aggravated assault for threatening **Malaya Peixinho**, and is serving a four-year sentence; Johns and Peixinho sued Rio Arriba County officials. (Rio Grande Sun; *Albuquerque Journal*; Searchlight New Mexico / Source New Mexico, 2025 — journalism.)

**Acoma and Pueblo voices, on the record:**

We survived some of the most violent acts committed at the hands of a historic figure who should not be idolized. ## Parked from `marketplace` research (2026-08-08) — The Dalles / Celilo Falls as a trading place
- The ten-mile reach of the Columbia between Celilo Falls and The Dalles was the largest trading gathering in the Northwest, drawing traders from British Columbia to California and, by some accounts, from as far as the Great Lakes. Goods recorded: coiled baskets, skins, and berries from the north; obsidian and bows from the south; horses, camas, and buffalo hides from the east; wapato, whale oil, and dentalium shells from the coast. Dentalium passed widely enough to serve as a common measure of value.
- Archaeological occupation is commonly given as **at least 10,000 years**, with 15,000 appearing in journalism and museum copy — state the range.
- Clark's journal of October 1805 calls the reach the "Great Mart of all this Country"; the trading was far older than the record.
- **Celilo Falls was drowned by The Dalles Dam in 1957.** (Oregon Historical Society, *The Columbia River Trade Network* and *Dalles of the Columbia, 1805*; lewis-clark.org.) `marketplace` uses this as exchange before there were stores; the nations who held the site, and what the dam took, are yours.

## Parked from `marketplace` research (2026-08-08) — Native buyers as choosy customers, 1500s
- Copper and brass kettles were among the most sought European trade goods in the Northeast and Great Lakes in the later 1500s. Excavated examples were frequently **cut up and reworked into beads, pendants, tubes, and projectile points** — forms long made from native copper — rather than used as pots. Basque kettle fragments are found from the Maritimes to the lower Great Lakes, showing Native traders redistributing the metal far inland. (Ehrhardt, *International Journal of Historical Archaeology*, 2011; Fitzgerald et al., *Historical Archaeology*, on Basque banded copper kettles; Museum of Ontario Archaeology.)
- The Hudson's Bay Company (from 1670) sent gunsmiths to its posts to assess quality and repair firearms in response to Native customers' demands. (Bill of Rights Institute; EH.net, "The Economic History of the Fur Trade.")
