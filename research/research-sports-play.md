## Era 01: Before 1500 (T-271a, 2026-09-28)

**What the evidence is.** Almost nothing about Native games before 1500 was written down at the time. The
direct evidence is objects dug up by archaeologists (chunkey stones, gaming reeds, kicking billets, clay
figurines). The descriptions of how games were played come from Europeans who watched them after contact
(1600s-1800s) and from Native nations' own traditions. Culin 1907 says his collection covers only games
played with objects: "Indian children have many amusements which they play without implements, such as tag,
etc." which he left out (Culin 1907, preface, p. 29-30).

**Two kinds of game.** Culin divides Native games into games of chance (dice-like objects thrown to get a
count) and games of dexterity (skill). He found "games of pure skill and calculation, such as chess" absent
(Culin 1907, introduction, p. 31).

### Chunkey at Cahokia
- Cahokia, the city near present-day St. Louis, "arose around A.D. 1050" (Archaeology 2009). Jefferson Patterson
  Park says chunkey "is believed to have originated around 600 A.D." in the Cahokia area and that the city
  declined around 1500 while "variations were played throughout North America" after (JPPM 2011). The two
  sources give different starting points: the game (about 600) and the city (about 1050). Keep both.
- How it was played: players rolled a stone disk and threw or slid poles after it, trying to land the pole
  closest to where the stone stopped (JPPM 2011). Stones have "convex edges and concave sides and were
  occasionally perforated in the center" (JPPM 2011).
- Archaeology 2009 is written by **Timothy R. Pauketat**, professor of anthropology, University of Illinois
  Urbana-Champaign. Captions: a "pre-Cahokian sandstone chunkey roller measures 2.5 inches in diameter"; "a
  12th-century stone of 3.5 inches was made of quartzite found at a single source on the Mississippi"; "ca. 1300
  chunkey stones from Georgia". A shell collar found 90 miles south of Cahokia shows a chunkey player holding a
  pole and a stone. Pauketat's argument (his words, hedged by him): "One of the primary vehicles for the growth
  of this new civilization may have been Cahokian envoys who carried chunkey stones in one hand and war clubs in
  the other as they ventured into the hinterlands with the purpose of making peace or political alliances."
  Attribute it to Pauketat as an argument. He also says chunkey "had a significant role in organizing social
  and political life at Cahokia." His opening scene of 1,000 people in the plaza is a reconstruction, not a
  record: do not use it as fact.
- Cahokia's Grand Plaza: city-building bank gives about 50 acres, levelled and filled by hand for ceremonies
  and chunkey (research-city-building.md line 13, Cahokia Mounds State Historic Site). A search summary gives 47
  acres for chunkey arenas (unconfirmed: search summary only). Use "about 50 acres" with the city-building source.
- Maryland: chunkey stones turn up in Maryland collections only at sites in Frederick and Montgomery counties
  (JPPM 2011).
- Betting: JPPM 2011 says spectators "frequently bet on the game, with some wagering everything they owned",
  including "their wives, children or their own freedom", and "some losers were even known to commit suicide."
  JPPM gives no date or observer for this sentence. Treat as a later-observer claim, source JPPM; do not put
  it in the before-1500 cell as a fact about Cahokia. The dated eyewitness on stakes is Adair (era 05).
- Later eyewitnesses who saw chunkey: the journal of the French frigate at the Mississippi mouth, 1698-99
  (Culin 1907, p. 485, citing Margry v. 4 p. 261); James Adair 1775 (see era 05); George Catlin 1832 (JPPM).

### Lacrosse and stickball (racket games)
- Names: the Choctaw call their game kapucha toli (stick + play) or ishtaboli (Choctaw Nation; Wikipedia "Choctaw
  stickball" for the translation "little brother of war", which the Choctaw Nation article does not use). The
  Mohawk name tewaarathon is given as "little brother of war" by the Canadian Encyclopedia (unconfirmed: search
  summary only). Do not put "little brother of war" in the prose without a better source.
- Age: "Stickball has been the Choctaw national sport for unknown centuries" (Choctaw Nation). No object or
  record dates a racket game before 1500. The earliest written description of a Choctaw match is 1729, by a
  Jesuit missionary (Wikipedia "Choctaw stickball", citing its refs 4, 6, 10). Huron lacrosse described by the
  Jesuit Jean de Brebeuf in 1636 (Culin 1907, citing *Jesuit Relations* v. 10 p. 185).
- Size: fields ran "100 yards to perhaps several miles in length"; George Catlin saw an 1834 game with "several
  hundred players each" side; other games had "twenty or fewer players on each side" (Choctaw Nation).
- Meaning: the Choctaw game "represents a detailed microcosm of Choctaw family structure and gender roles and
  has been used as a basic teaching aid for the youth"; women played alongside men; "Betting was traditionally
  a major part of the game, especially for the women"; "Fatalities were common" in the old games (Choctaw Nation).
- Healing: Brebeuf (1636) wrote that among the Huron "the games of crosse, dish, and straw, the first two are,
  they say, most healing"; a sick man or a healer could call for a lacrosse game for the sick man's health, and
  "village contending against village" bet "beaver robes and porcelain collars" (Culin 1907, p. 589).
  Among the Huron, "The women have only this kind of game [a dice game with fruit stones]. Children play at
  cross, never or rarely at platter" (Bacqueville de la Potherie, early 1700s, quoted in Culin 1907 p. 109,
  under Huron dice games). So children played lacrosse, and women played dice games.
- **Settling disputes: the claim checked.** The Choctaw Nation writes that game outcomes "are said to have
  sometimes been used to settle disputes between Choctaw communities", and names two documented games, both
  around the 1790s, between the Choctaw and the Muscogee (Creek). Both "broiled into actual battles" (Choctaw
  Nation). Both are after 1500 and are filed under era 05. **No source found dates this use before 1500.** The
  seed sentence "sometimes played between nations to settle disputes instead of fighting" is not supported for
  this era and came out of the outline (see SEARCHED, NOT FOUND below).
- Haudenosaunee (Iroquois): Lewis Henry Morgan (*League of the Iroquois*, 1851, p. 294, quoted in Culin 1907
  p. 592) called the ball game, o-ta-da-jish'-qua-age, "the favorite" Iroquois game, played "usually from six to
  eight on a side", with gates about 80 rods apart, each gate two poles about 10 feet high set about 3 rods
  apart; 5 or 7 goals won; managers on each side enforced the rules, and one rule "forbade the players to touch
  the ball with the hand or foot." Players trained and dieted "as in a preparation for a foot-race." The people
  "gathered from the whole surrounding country." (80 rods = 440 yards, 1,320 feet. 3 rods = about 50 feet.)
  This is an 1851 description.
- 1797 Grand River (Ontario) match between Mohawk and Seneca: "about six hundred upon a side", goals 30 rods
  apart (William L. Stone, *Life of Brant*, 1844, quoted in Culin 1907 p. 591). Era 05 material.

### Games of chance
- Massachusett (coast of present Massachusetts), described by William Wood, *New England's Prospect* (London,
  1634): two games, puim and hubbub, "not much unlike cards and dice." Hubbub was "five small bones in a small
  smooth tray", black on one side and white on the other; the player thumped the tray on the ground so the bones
  jumped, and scored by colors; players shouted "Hub, Hub, Hub" and "may be heard play at this game a quarter of
  a mile off" (Culin 1907 about p. 73-74, quoting Wood). This is a 1634 description (era 03).
- Southwest, archaeological: split gaming reeds excavated by J. Walter Fewkes at the Chevlon ruin, near where
  Chevelon Fork meets the Little Colorado River, about 15 miles east of Winslow, Arizona, which Culin takes as
  evidence of the age of the Hopi and Zuni cane game (Culin 1907 p. 165). Culin gives no date for the reeds.
- Kicking billets (wooden pieces kicked along a race course) from cliff dwellings in Mancos Canyon, Colorado,
  held at the University of Pennsylvania museum: one 4 3/4 inches long, marked with incised lines (Culin 1907 p.
  667). Culin calls them proof of the kick-ball race "at an early period." No date given.

### Children's play and toys (archaeology)
- Kathryn Kamp (Grinnell College) measured fingerprint ridge widths on clay animal figurines, miniature pots and
  full-size vessels from Sinagua sites in northern Arizona. Children's prints are narrower than adults'. She
  found that very young Sinagua children made animal figurines and probably miniature dishes as playthings, and
  learned how clay behaves while doing it (Kamp 2001, abstract). Discover (Alex 2020) dates the sites "roughly
  800-year-old" (so about 1200).
- Split-twig figurines, animals made of split twigs, from sites in the Southwest roughly 2,000 years old, were
  thought to be for hunting rituals; three of them, two deer and a duck, were found in a child's burial, and
  archaeologists now consider that some may have been toys (Alex 2020).
- The seed asked for "dolls, tops, miniature bows" in museum collections dated before 1500: see SEARCHED, NOT FOUND.

## Era 02: The 1500s (T-271a, 2026-09-28)

**Thin, and the record says why.** The Europeans here in the 1500s were Spanish and French soldiers, sailors
and missionaries. What they wrote about games is almost entirely what they saw Native people play.

### What the French saw in Florida, 1562-1565
- René de Laudonnière (French, on the 1562 voyage and leader of the 1564-65 Fort Caroline colony near present
  Jacksonville) wrote of the Native people there (Timucua): "They exercise their young men to runne well, and
  they make a game among themselves, which he winneth that has the longest breath. They also exercise themselves
  much in shooting." And: "They play at ball in this manner: they set up a tree in the midst of a place which is 8
  or 9 fathoms high, in the top whereof there is set a square mat made of reeds or bulrushes, which whosoever
  hitteth in playing thereat, winneth the game." (Culin 1907, about p. 603 (ball) and p. 803 (running), quoting Hakluyt's *Voyages*
  v. 13 p. 413, Edinburgh 1889. Culin files it under "Muskogee, Georgia"; the people Laudonnière met were Timucua
  on the Florida coast.) 8 or 9 fathoms = 48 to 54 feet.
- Jacques Le Moyne de Morgues, artist and mapmaker on the 1564 Laudonnière expedition, painted Timucua life.
  Theodor de Bry, a Flemish engraver who never went to Florida, bought the paintings from Le Moyne's widow and
  published engravings from them. Plate XXXVI, "The Youth at Their Exercises", shows young men running, shooting
  arrows and throwing a ball at a target on top of a tall pole (earlyfloridalit.net, caption credited to Florida
  Memory, State Library and Archives of Florida; search summary of Florida Memory item 254323).
- **Date dispute:** most sources give de Bry's publication as 1591 (earlyfloridalit.net; Jacksonville Public
  Library). The NPS page (nps.gov/timu/learn/historyculture/foca_lemoyne.htm) says 1587, "the year after Le
  Moyne's death." Record both.
- **Reliability:** NPS: de Bry "combined cultures, environments, and artifacts from across North and South
  America, filtered them through his European perspective, and created images specifically meant to sensationalize
  and sell copies." So the written Laudonnière account is the firmer source for the pole-target ball game.

### The Spanish in New Mexico, 1598
- Juan de Oñate's colonists reached the Rio Grande in 1598. Gaspar Pérez de Villagrá, *Historia de la Nueva
  México* (Alcalá, 1610), describes a play written by Captain Marcos Farfán for the first camp on the Rio Grande
  in May 1598, about the wars between Christians and Moors in Spain. The 1598 celebrations included "tilts with
  cane spears, running bulls and tilts at the ring" (El Palacio, "Proud Pageantry", Feb 2020, Museum of New
  Mexico). A tilt with cane spears (juego de cañas) is a mock battle on horseback in which riders throw light
  cane spears at each other and block them with shields (Wikipedia "Game of Cañas": "rows of men mounted on
  horseback (usually nobles) throwing reeds at each other like lances or darts and stopping them with shields";
  popular in Spain 16th-18th centuries). Tilting at the ring (sortija): a rider at a gallop tries to take a
  hanging ring on the point of a lance (general definition; no page opened for it: (unconfirmed: search summary only)).
- Date of the bull-running and cane tilts: sources connect them with the dedication of the first church at San
  Juan de los Caballeros, 8 September 1598 (search summary, several NM history sites) (unconfirmed: search
  summary only). El Palacio itself says only "the 1598 pageantry." Say "in 1598."
- `holidays` tells the thanksgiving on the Rio Grande (30 April 1598) and `storytelling-evolution` tells Farfán's
  play as theatre. This chapter keeps the horseback games.

### SEARCHED, NOT FOUND 2026-09-28 (T-271a): Did Spanish missionaries ban Native ball games in the 1500s?
Sources checked: Paiva's *Ball Game Manuscript* (1676) and Bushnell, "That Demonic Game" (*The Americas* 35,
1978), whose title dates the campaign 1675-1684; Wikipedia "Missions in Spanish Florida" (missions spread in
the 1590s). No ban on games dated to the 1500s was found. The documented campaign is in the 1670s (era 03).
How the prose can say it: "Spanish friars started missions in Florida and Georgia in the 1590s. Their campaign
to stop the Native ball game came eighty years later."

### Jamestown, 1611
- Sir Thomas Dale arrived in May 1611 with about 300 soldiers. "On Dale's first day, the colonist Ralph Hamor
  later wrote, the governor 'hastened' to Jamestown only to find his charges at 'their daily and usuall works,
  bowling in the streetes.'" (Encyclopedia Virginia). Historians William Kelso and Karen Ordahl Kupperman argue
  that malnutrition and disease produced "behavior that could be mistaken for idleness" (Encyclopedia Virginia).
- Dale then enforced the *Lawes Divine, Morall and Martiall*. Under them "one man" convicted of stealing oatmeal
  "suffered a needle through his tongue, after which he was lashed to a tree until he starved" (Encyclopedia
  Virginia). This belongs to `crime-justice`; this chapter uses only the bowling scene.

### New England
- Plymouth, Christmas 1621: William Bradford took away the stool-ball and bar-pitching equipment of newcomers
  playing in the street. **Told in `holidays` (outlines/holidays.md line 100). Cross-reference only.**
- Massachusetts Bay laws (Mass. Laws 1660, text and Whitmore's notes): a law of 26 May 1647 against
  shovel-board (shuffleboard) in public houses; 21 June 1650 extended it to "bowling or any other play or game in
  public houses"; a law of 4 Nov 1646 against gaming for money; 7 May 1651 against dancing in ordinaries (taverns).
  The text of the Gaming title: because of "the use of the Games of Shuffle-board and Bowling, in and about Houses
  of Common entertainment, whereby much precious time is spent unprofitably, and much waste of Wine and Beer
  occasioned", no one may play them in or about such a house. The house keeper forfeits **20 shillings** per
  offence, every player **5 shillings**. Anyone who plays or games for money forfeits "treble the Value thereof".
  Dancing in an ordinary: 5 shillings per person. In the same code, the Christmas law (1659) adds that anyone
  "playing either at cards or at dice" anywhere in the colony pays 5 shillings per offence. (Mass. Laws 1660;
  `holidays` tells the Christmas ban itself.)
- Breen 1977, note 9: in 17th-century Massachusetts Bay "heavy betting did not develop"; he found "a small
  amount of gambling ... among the poor, especially among servants" but no gambling among the colony's leaders.
- **Plymouth Colony fines for cards (named people, Plymouth Records):**
  - 1663 (court held before 5 Oct 1663): "John Shilley, for playing att cards on the Lords day, fined 20s."
    (vol. 4, around p. 42.)
  - Same volume, a 1663-64 court: "Richard Berry, and Wiliam Griffin and his wife, and Richard Michell and his
    wife, for playing att cards, fined each of them forty shillings, according to the law."
  - Court of June 1679 (vol. 6): Joseph Thorne of Scituate, cards twice, fined £1; Joseph Peirse, once, 10s;
    James Benitt, "seuerall times", £1; John Holbrooke, "for playing att cards once in the woods, himselfe
    haueing comand there", £1, and "His man, Patricke", 10s; Isacke Woodworth, twice; Mathew Gannet, "for playing
    att cards seuerall times, and suffering others to play att cards in his house", £2. Patrick is a servant
    fined beside his master.
- William Wood, *New England's Prospect* (London, 1634), on the Massachusett: "For their sports of activity they
  have commonly but three or four, as football, shooting, running, and swimming: when they play county against
  county there are rich goals, all behung with wompompeage [wampum], mowhackies, beaver skins and black otter
  skins." "Their goals be a mile long, placed on the sands, which are even as a board; their ball is no bigger
  than a handball, which sometimes they mount in the air with their naked feet". "Sometimes also it is two days
  before they get a goal; then they mark the ground they win and begin there the next day." Before playing
  "they paint themselves, even as when they go to war" so that nobody would know who hurt him and seek revenge.
  "While the men play, the boys pipe, and the women dance and sing". Wood also claims "one English being able to
  beat ten Indians at football" (his boast: attribute it, do not state it). (Culin 1907 about p. 697-698.)
- Wood 1634 on hubbub (dice game with five bones in a tray): see Era 01 bank.

### New Netherland (New York), 1650s
- **Sunday ordinance, 26 Oct 1656**, Director General Peter Stuyvesant and Council: forbade on Sunday "any
  ordinary labor", fine one pound Flemish per person, "much less any lower or unlawful exercise and Amusement,
  Drunkenness, frequenting Taverns or Tippling houses, Dancing, playing Ball, Cards, Tricktrack, Tennis, Cricket or
  Ninepins, going on pleasure parties in a Boat, Car or Wagon before, between or during Divine Service, on pain of
  a double fine" (NN Laws p. 258-259).
- **Pulling the goose.** O'Callaghan's note: at Shrovetide farmers of Gelderland "Pull the Goose": "a goose whose
  neck and head had been previously smeared with Oil or Soap, was fastened by a rope between two poles. Horsemen
  then entered the lists, and driving at full gallop, made an attempt to seize the prize." The winner was "King
  of the festival". The custom "seems to have been first introduced into New Netherland in 1654"; Stuyvesant
  called it "an unprofitable, heathenish and Popish festival, and a pernicious custom" and banned it. "Some
  Farmers ... celebrated the feast notwithstanding, and although the Burgomasters and Schepens of New Amsterdam
  remonstrated, the Farmers were fined, and some of them imprisoned" (NN Laws p. 333; the year of the fines is
  not clear from the note). On 26 Feb 1658 farmers and their servants on Manhattan petitioned to Pull the Goose
  and were refused (NN Laws p. 334). Old Taverns of New York (Bayles, 1915, Gutenberg 44240) dates Stuyvesant's
  ban on riding the goose to February 1658 and quotes his reply. **Whether the goose was alive:** O'Callaghan does
  not say. Accounts of gander pulling elsewhere describe a live bird (see era 04-05). Do not state it for New
  Netherland.
- **Golf (kolf) in Fort Orange (Albany), 10 Dec 1659:** the commissaries of Fort Orange and Beverwyck, after
  complaints "against playing at Golf along the streets, which causes great damage to the windows of the Houses,
  and exposes people to the danger of being wounded", forbade it on pain of 25 guilders per person (NN Laws p. 367).
- Christmas and New Year in New Amsterdam: records name "firing of guns, beating of drums, dancing, playing of
  tick-tack, bowling, playing of ninepins, sleighing parties or wagon rides" (Bayles, *Old Taverns of New York*,
  1915, ch. 1). `holidays` owns the festival.

### Horse racing
- **Newmarket, Long Island, 1665.** After the English took New Amsterdam in 1664, Col. Richard Nicolls, commander of
  the British troops and first English governor of New York, laid out "a 2-mile (3.2-km) course on the plains of
  Long Island (called Newmarket after the British racecourse)" and offered "a silver cup to the best horses in the
  spring and fall seasons" (Britannica). Wikipedia "Newmarket Course" gives the Hempstead Plains, present Garden
  City, and a prize of a silver crown or a bushel of wheat (unconfirmed: search summary only). Land: which nation
  lived on the plains: see the PATCH at the end of this era.
- **Virginia quarter racing.** Breen 1977: "The contest that generated the greatest interest among the gentry was
  the quarter-horse race, an all-out sprint by two horses over a quarter-mile dirt track." County court records
  name planter families Randolph, Eppes, Jefferson, Swan, Kenner, Hardiman, Parker, Cocke, Batte, Hardidge, Yowell
  and Washington. The biggest recorded stake: John Baker and John Haynie, Northumberland County, 1693, wagered
  4,000 pounds of tobacco and 40 shillings sterling on their horses Prince and Smoaker. Riders agreed to "fair
  Rideing" and appointed a starter, judges and a stakeholder. Earlier races allowed a rider to "jostle, whip, or
  knee an opponent, or to attempt to unseat him" (Breen).
- Durand of Dauphiné, a French Huguenot, saw burgesses at Jamestown in the fall of 1686 start gambling after dinner
  and still playing the next morning (Breen, quoting Durand).
- Stanard 1894: no horses were kept especially for racing until the 1700s, "perhaps about 1730". An Oct 1678
  Henrico deposition by Bartholomew Roberts describes a race at Bermuda Hundred between Abraham Womock and Richard
  Ligon, started by Abraham Childers.
- **James Bullocke, 1674 (story).** York County court order, 10 September 1674, as printed by Stanard 1894 p. 294:
  "James Bullocke, a Taylor, haveing made a race for his mare to runn w'th a horse belonging to Mr. Mathew Slader
  for twoe thousand pounds of tobacco and caske, it being contrary to Law for a Labourer to make a race, being a
  sport only for Gentlemen, is fined for the same one hundred pounds of tobacco and caske." And: "Whereas Mr.
  Mathew Slader & James Bullocke, by condition under the hand and seale of the said Slader, that his horse should
  runn out of the way that Bullock's mare might win, w'ch is an apparent cheate, is ord'ed to be putt in the stocks
  & there sitt the space of one houre." **Figure dispute:** Breen 1977 (citing York Co. Deeds, Orders, Wills
  1672-94, p. 85) gives the fine as "two hundred pounds of tobacco and cask"; Stanard's printed transcription says
  one hundred. Record both. Stanard calls it "the earliest notice of racing" in the Virginia records. Whose law
  the court meant is not stated: no Virginia statute limiting racing to gentlemen has been found (see SEARCHED,
  NOT FOUND). Slader, a "Mr." (a gentleman), agreed in writing to pull his horse; he was the one sent to the stocks.
  Stocks: a wooden frame with holes that locked a person's ankles while they sat in public.

### Spanish Florida: the ball game banned, 1676
- Fray Juan de Paiva, friar of the mission of San Luis de Talimali (present Tallahassee), wrote his account of the
  Apalachee and Yustaga ball game on 23 Sept 1676, with the royal interpreter Diego Salvador and the parish
  interpreter and Apalachee leader Juan Mendoza (earlyfloridalit.net intro).
- The game: a town sent a courier to challenge another, naming the day and the number of players, "forty or
  fifty more or less". Played in summer at midday. Players kicked a ball "the size of a musket-ball, [or] a little
  larger" at a single tall pole; eleven hits won. Players wore only a deerskin breechcloth. (Paiva.)
- Harm, in Paiva's words: players stepped on "faces, heads, or bellies", pulled "at arms or legs with no concern
  as to whether they may be dislocated", and filled mouths with dirt; when a player swallowed the ball "they are
  made to vomit it up by squeezing their windpipe or by kicks to the stomach". He listed "many lamed, broken legs,
  persons without the use of one or both hands, blinded in one eye, broken ribs", and "people who have been killed
  in the said game. I can give testimony about two that I am aware of in the place of San Luis." In 1676 "In five
  successive games not one concluded without becoming a live war", and soldiers were present at the games.
- Paiva's other charges: houses left open to theft while everyone watched; fields left unplanted, so that "the
  lieutenant of this province" had to send soldiers to order villages to plant; heavy betting. (Paiva.) These are
  a missionary's charges against a game he wanted banned: attribute them to him.
- The ban: Governor **Pablo de Hita Salazar** and the Franciscan provincial **Fray Francisco Perete** supported
  Paiva. The Apalachee chiefs "knocked down the poles and put the holy cross in their place", and Captain Juan
  Fernández de Florencia thanked them for it on the governor's behalf. Some people wrote to the governor asking him
  not to abolish the game (Paiva). Amy Bushnell, "'That Demonic Game': The Campaign to Stop Indian Pelota Playing
  in Spanish Florida, 1675-1684," *The Americas* 35 (1978), dates the campaign 1675-1684 (title only read).
- The same text calls chunkey "quicio ... which all these nations play, which is [played] with two long poles about
  three yardsticks in length and a flat and round stone" (Paiva).
- The Talimali Band of the Apalachee Indians lives in Louisiana today (earlyfloridalit.net editor's note).

### Enslaved people, 1680
- The Virginia General Assembly, 8 June 1680, "An act for preventing Negroes Insurrections": its preamble called
  "the frequent meeting of considerable numbers of negroe slaves under pretence of feasts and burialls ... of
  dangerous consequence." An enslaved person who left the owner's land without a certificate got "twenty lashes on
  his bare back well layd on"; one who raised a hand against "any Christian" got "thirty lashes on his bare back
  well laid on"; one "lying out and resisting" capture could lawfully be killed (Encyclopedia Virginia primary
  document; NPS Westmoreland). In November 1682 the Assembly barred enslaved people from gathering on plantations
  not their owners' for more than four hours at a time (NPS, which writes "for fours at a time").
- Lashes: blows with a whip on the bare back. "Well laid on" meant struck hard.

### Horse racing
- Robert Beverley, *History of Virginia* (London, 1705): "There is yet another kind of sport, which the young
  people take great delight in, and that is, the hunting of wild horses", horses "foaled in the woods of the
  uplands, that never were in hand"; unmarked, they "belong to him that first takes them" (Stanard 1894 p. 298).
- Rev. Hugh Jones, *The Present State of Virginia* (London, 1724), p. 48: "The common Planters leading easy
  lives don't much admire Labour, or any manly Exercise except Horse racing"; "almost every ordinary Person
  keeps a Horse; and I have known some spend the morning in ranging several miles in the Woods to find and catch
  their Horses only to ride two or three miles to Church, to the Court House, or to a Horse-Race" (Stanard; Breen).
- Mount Vernon: quarter races, about a quarter mile, "dominated" early 18th-century racing; "Two horses would race
  along a straight, narrow path while being ridden by their owners, rather than trained jockeys"; the horses
  were farm horses, "which allowed common farmers to participate"; Henrico County paths at Richmond, Bermuda
  Hundred, Malvern Hill and Varina. **Conflict:** Breen (era 03) says the gentry "actively enforced its exclusive
  control" and cites Bullocke 1674; Mount Vernon says common farmers could take part. And Stanard's 1678 Henrico
  deposition has "Mr. Chamberlaine's boy upon Abraham Womeck's horse", so not every rider was an owner. Record
  all three; do not resolve.
- Mount Vernon (article by Gabrielle McCoy, PhD; its note 4: "Different sources list John Pinkerton's name as
  'Pinkerton' or 'Brickerton.' The correct spelling is uncertain."; note 5 cites *Virginia Gazette* 30 Sept 1737):
  "John Pinkerton hosted the first documented long-distance race in Virginia at Old Field track in
  November 1737", three days, a three-mile race each day; with "a fiddling contest, a boys' running race, a beauty
  contest for local unmarried women, and a dinner with singing and dancing." The *Virginia Gazette* notice said
  "such Meetings and Entertainments are somewhat New, in these Parts" and aimed at "Friendship, and innocent Mirth."
- Hanover County, St. Andrew's Day (30 Nov) 1736, *Virginia Gazette* 26 Nov 1736: "some merry disposed gentleman
  of the said County" set up prizes: "A neat Hunting Saddle ... to be run for (the Quarter) by any number of
  Horses and Mares: A fine Cremona Fiddle to be plaid for, by any number of Country Fiddlers ... With divers
  other considerable Prizes, for Dancing, Singing, Foot-ball play, jumping, wrestling &c particularly a fine paid
  of silk stockings to be given to the handsometh Maid upon the Green, to be judged of by the Company, At Page's
  Warehouse commonly call'd Crutchfields" (blog transcription; see sources). Breen: a later notice asked "all
  Persons resorting there ... to behave themselves with Decency and Sobriety"; the aim was "cultivating
  Friendship" among the county's "considerable Number of Gentlemen, Merchants, and credible Planters"; "Less
  affluent persons apparently were welcome to watch" (Breen, citing *Va. Gazette* 19-26 Nov 1736, 30 Sept-7 Oct 1737).
  A Cremona fiddle is a violin made in Cremona, Italy. `music` may want the fiddle contest (see TO PARK).
- *Virginia Gazette* 1 July 1737: "We hear there is to be horse-racing every Saturday till October, at the Race
  Ground near this City" (Williamsburg) (Stanard p. 300).
- Williamsburg fair, December 1739 (*Va. Gazette* 14 Dec 1739, in Stanard p. 300): three days of races "round
  the Mile Course"; day one, eight horses for a saddle worth 40 shillings, won by Col. Chiswell's horse Edgcomb;
  second place won a bridle, third a whip.
- Gloucester County, April 1740 (ad of 11 Jan 1739/40): a purse of thirty pistoles at Joseph Seawell's; horses
  carry 140 lb (Stanard p. 301).
- 1714: Richmond County court fined several men for selling liquor "by Retaile in the Race Ground" (Breen).
- Bulle Rock: "thought to be the first English Thoroughbred stallion brought to the American Colonies", imported
  in 1730 by James Patton, at age 21 (Wikipedia, "Bulle Rock"). Stanard: 1740-1775 at least fifty stallions and
  thirty mares imported into Virginia.

### Cockfighting and gander pulling
- Gale/encyclopedia.com: trainers following English manuals (Gervase Markham, 1614; Charles Cotton, 1674) fed
  the birds special diets and fitted them with "'spurs' made of silver, steel, or bone attached to their legs.
  Fights were traditionally to the death." A fight at the "Town House" in Salem, Massachusetts, in 1744; the
  *South Carolina Gazette* of 1732 advertised a fight at "the House of Mrs. Eldridge on the Green". *Virginia
  Gazette* ads for cockfights in 1752, 1755 and 1770 (era 05). William Byrd II, Robert Carter and George
  Washington are named as enthusiasts. Critics complained of "genteel people ... with the vulgar and debased",
  and "both blacks and whites attended the fights and wagered on the outcome" (Dulles pp. 34-35).
- Plain definition for the writer: a cockfight set two roosters to fight in a ring; the steel or bone spurs
  strapped to their legs cut the other bird; men bet on which bird would kill the other.
- Gander pulling (SC Encyclopedia): "a live gander was tied upside down by its feet to a tree branch. Farmers then
  greased the gander's neck, and riders on horseback would pass the tree at a full gallop, attempting to decapitate
  the bird"; riders sometimes fell and some "lost fingers"; "in Virginia they often took place on the Monday
  following Easter"; spectators bet. The entry covers "the colonial and antebellum periods" and says gander
  pulling "appears to have been beneath lowcountry men of property". No dated 1700-1750 event is given.

### Native games
- **James Adair, *The History of the American Indians* (London, 1775), p. 401, on chungke (chunkey) among the
  Choctaw** (quoted in Culin 1907 p. 485-486): "The warriors have another favorite game called chungke; which, with
  propriety of language, may be called 'running hard labor.'" Near the townhouse "a square piece of ground well
  cleaned, and fine sand is carefully strewed over it". "Only one or two on a side play". The stone is "about 2
  fingers broad at the edge, and 2 spans round"; each player has "a pole of about 8 feet long". Players run "most
  part of the day, at half speed, under the violent heat of the sun, staking their silver ornaments, their nose,
  finger, and ear rings; their breast, arm, and wrist plates, and even all their wearing apparel, except that which
  barely covers their middle." The stones "are kept with the strictest religious care from one generation to
  another, and are exempted from being buried with the dead. They belong to the town where they are used". Adair
  also calls the game "a task of stupid drudgery" (his judgment: attribute, or leave out).
- **Creek-Choctaw ball game on the Noxubee River, about 1790** (Cushman 1899, quoting H. S. Halbert, who heard it
  from Stonie Hadjo, a Creek-born warrior adopted by the Choctaw, who died in Noxubee County, Mississippi, in
  1836): the Creek and Choctaw both claimed a large beaver pond on the Noxubee, valuable for the fur trade at Mobile
  and Pensacola. "The two Nations finally agreed to settle the matter by a ball-play." Preparations took nearly
  three months. Stonie Hadjo said ten thousand Creek and Choctaw people camped around the ball ground. The Creek
  team won the deciding game after four hours. A Choctaw player insulted a Creek player (Choctaw accounts say the
  Creek player threw a petticoat on him first), the two fought, and "a general fight with sticks, knives, guns,
  tomahawks and bows and arrows" spread to the warriors. The fight lasted from an hour before sunset until two
  hours after sunrise, when the chiefs arrived and stopped it. The ball players "almost to a man perished". The
  dead were buried with money and silver ornaments. The beaver pond was then left to the Choctaw. **No number of
  dead is given in the account.** A figure of "more than 500" appears only in a search summary (unconfirmed:
  search summary only). In 1832 a settler named Charles Dobbs took the ground and dug up about $500 in silver and
  $250 in silver ornaments from the graves (Cushman).
- **Black Warrior River game, about 1800** (Cushman, from an aged Choctaw via Halbert): the Choctaw and Creek
  disputed the land between the Tombigbee and Black Warrior rivers, agreed to a ball-play a mile below present
  Tuscaloosa, both claimed victory, and a battle followed "in which many were killed and wounded on both sides";
  more battles followed that fall. Creek chief Tuskeegee; Choctaw chief Luee. Era 06 (1800); left for T-271b.
- The Choctaw Nation confirms: two documented games "around the 1790s", one "over a productive beaver pond located
  near the Nuxubee River" and one over land between the Tombigbee and Black Warrior, and "both of these stickball
  games were highly contested and broiled into actual battles!"
- **Mohawk and Seneca at Grand River, Ontario, 1797:** a match of "about six hundred upon a side", goals 30 rods
  (about 165 yards) apart (William L. Stone, *Life of Brant*, 1844, quoted in Culin p. 591). Grand River is the
  Six Nations reserve in Canada, where many Haudenosaunee moved after the Revolution (location from Culin's
  heading; the move is general knowledge: check `native-nations` before stating it).
- **Hawaii, 1779.** Lt. James King, finishing Cook's journal at Kealakekua Bay: "Whenever, from stormy weather,
  or any extraordinary swell at sea, the impetuosity of the surf is increased to its utmost heights, they choose
  that time for their amusement ... Twenty or thirty of the natives, taking each a long narrow board, rounded at
  the ends, set out together from the shore." They dove under the waves, swam out beyond the surf, lay on their
  boards and rode the largest wave in (King 1779, published 1784). The surgeon's mate David Samwell described
  surf-riding at Kealakekua Bay on 22 January 1779 (surfresearch.com.au list of excerpts). Hawaii was an
  independent kingdom then: territories thread.

### Virginia: horses, cocks, fights (Fithian and Mount Vernon)
- **Jockeys:** "While the owners of race horses increasingly came from the upper echelons of society, those who rode
  in these races often came from the bottom of the social hierarchy: enslaved men and boys." **Austin Curtis**,
  enslaved by Willie (William) Jones of Halifax, North Carolina, raced in Virginia and North Carolina in the 1770s.
  "In 1791, Jones freed Curtis." When Curtis died in 1809 the *Raleigh Minerva* called him "well known for many
  years past, as keeper of race horses; in the management of which useful animal, he particularly excelled."
  (Mount Vernon/McCoy.) A search summary adds that Jones petitioned the North Carolina General Assembly on 5 Dec
  1791, which granted it and gave Curtis the surname Jones, and that Curtis hid Jones's horses from Cornwallis's
  army (unconfirmed: search summary only).
- By 1772 races were "established annually, almost at every town and considerable place in Virginia" (J. F. D.
  Smyth, *A Tour in the United States*, 1784, quoted by Mount Vernon and Stanard). Williamsburg races each spring
  and fall, purses of £100 the first day and £50 after, races "commonly continueing for a week" (Smyth, in Stanard).
- Jockey clubs in Dumfries, Fredericksburg, Petersburg, Portsmouth, Richmond and Warwick by the late 1700s held
  subscription races for members (Mount Vernon). George Washington was co-manager of a race at Alexandria in May
  1761 (Mount Vernon, citing Robertson 1964).
- During the war, Congress proposed that the states pass laws to suppress "theatrical entertainments, horse
  racing, gaming, and such other diversions as are productive of idleness, dissipation and a general depravity of
  principles and manners." Virginia passed a law in 1793 banning bets of more than $7 on horse racing and "any other
  sport or pastime" (Mount Vernon).
- Cockfights advertised in the *Virginia Gazette* in 1752, 1755 and 1770 (Gale/encyclopedia.com, era 04 sources).
- **Fithian, Easter Monday, 4 April 1774:** "Easter Monday; a general holiday; Negroes are now all disbanded till
  Wednesday morning & are at Cock Fights through the County". The next day his students complained that "As the
  Negroes have this Day for a Holiday our Schollars thinks it hard that they should be compell'd to attend to
  Business."
- **Fithian, Sunday 10 April 1774:** "Before Breakfast I saw a Ring of Negroes at the Stable, fighting Cocks, and in
  several parts of the plantation they are digging up their small Lots of ground allow'd by their Master for
  Potatoes, peas, &c; All such work for themselves they constantly do on Sundays, as they are otherwise employed on
  every other Day."
- **Fithian, 29 Jan and 3 Feb 1774:** enslaved people gathered in the schoolroom in the evening "& began to play the
  Fiddle, & dance"; the Carter boys Ben and Harry joined, "Harry was dancing with his Coat off", and Fithian
  "dispersed them however immediately" (29 Jan). On 3 Feb "several Negroes & Ben, & Harry are playing on a Banjo &
  dancing." `music` may want these (see TO PARK).
- **Fithian, 18 June 1774:** "Bob teaz'd me for leave to go to a Cock-Fight & Horse-Race about two Miles off, I gave
  him leave with his promising to be home by Sun Set." Bob is one of the Carter sons Fithian taught; his age is not
  given in the passages read.
- **Fithian, Monday 29 August 1774:** "By appointment is to be fought this Day near Mr. Lanes two fist Battles
  between four young Fellows." He lists what was allowed in such fights: "Bruising, Kicking, Scratching, Pinching,
  Biting, Butting, Tripping, Throtling, Gouging, Cursing, Dismembring, Howling, &c." and says the fights drew "a
  crowd of People".
- **Gouging (NCpedia, "Gouging", Jim L. Sumner, 2006, citing Elliott Gorn, *American Historical Review* 90, Feb
  1985):** "no-holds-barred" or "rough-and-tumble" fighting, "A mixture of boxing, wrestling, and brawling", which
  "prohibited only the use of weapons"; a match ended when one fighter gave up or could not go on. In 1746 North
  Carolina Governor Gabriel Johnston criticized the "barbarous and inhuman manner of boxing which so much prevails
  among the lower sort of people", and legislators made it illegal "to cut out tongues, pull out eyes, or bite off
  noses or fingers". An 1807 witness, Charles Janson, saw two men with "their thumbs endeavoring to force a passage
  into each other's eyes, while several of the bystanders were betting upon the first eye to be turned out of its
  socket", and the winner stood up "with his antagonist's eye in his hand" (era 06). NCpedia: "most historians
  think that the loss of an eye or other body part was rare" but fights risked "broken bones, scarring, maiming, or
  even death." Search summary (Gorn): Virginia made it a felony in 1752 to put out an eye, slit or bite off a nose or
  lip (unconfirmed: search summary only).
- **Fithian, December 1773, barring out:** "Mr. Goodlet was barred out of his School last Monday by his Scholars,
  for Christmas Holidays, which are to continue til twelfth-day" (6 January). Barring out: the pupils locked the
  schoolmaster out of the schoolhouse until he agreed to give them a holiday. Fithian's own pupils "consented to have
  four or five Days now, and to have their full Holiday in May next."
- Fithian's school holidays in 1774 included Shrove Tuesday (afternoon off) and Good Friday, 1 April 1774 ("A
  general Holiday here").
- Fithian's advice letter to his successor, John Peck (Aug 1774): whenever you go "to a Ball; or to a Horse-Race, or
  to a Cock-Fight, or to a Fish-Feast ... if you bett at all" be careful.

### The Revolution and play
- **Continental Association, 20 Oct 1774, article 8:** the delegates agreed to "discountenance and discourage every
  species of extravagance and dissipation, especially all horse-racing, and all kinds of games, cock fighting,
  exhibitions of shews, plays, and other expensive diversions and entertainments" (Avalon). Gale: jockey clubs in
  Annapolis and New York canceled their meetings in 1774 out of respect for the Association.
- **Valley Forge, spring 1778.** Ensign George Ewing, 3rd New Jersey Regiment, journal: on a day in April 1778
  (the entry follows his 7 April entry on Steuben's new drill; the day heading is lost in the printed text):
  "exercisd in the afternoon in the intervals playd at base this evening". Protoball dates it 7 April 1778
  (unconfirmed: search summary only). On 2 May 1778: "in the afternoon playd a game at Wicket with a number of
  Gent of the Arty [artillery]". On 4 May 1778: "This day His Excellency dined with G[eneral] Knox and after dinner
  did us the honor to play at Wicket with us" (Ewing). "His Excellency" is General George Washington. Base was a
  running game with bases; wicket was a bat-and-ball game like cricket.
- **Pittsfield, Massachusetts, 1791:** a town bylaw "for the Preservation of the Windows in the New Meeting House"
  barred playing "Wicket, Cricket, Baseball, Batball, Football, Cats, Fives or any other Games played with Ball"
  within 80 yards of the meeting house, fine five shillings (wording and fine from search summaries: unconfirmed:
  search summary only). SABR confirms the bylaw "prohibit[ed] anyone from playing baseball within 80 yards of the
  building" to protect the meeting house's windows. John Thorn found the reference in an 1869 history of Pittsfield;
  a librarian found the document at the Berkshire Athenaeum; announced May 2004 (search summary of ESPN; SABR
  opened). Wikipedia calls it "The first known American reference to baseball".

### Children
- **George Washington's toy orders.** When he married Martha Custis in January 1759 her children John Parke
  ("Jacky") and Martha Parke ("Patsy") Custis were about six and four. In his first invoice of goods from London
  after the marriage he ordered "10 shillings worth of Toys," "6 little books for children beginning to read," and
  "1 fashionable-dressed baby to cost 10 shillings." When that doll broke or was lost, he ordered "1 fashionable
  dress Doll to cost a guinea," and "A box of Gingerbread Toys & Sugar Images or Comfits." (Ford 1896.) "Baby" here
  means a doll. A guinea was 21 shillings.
- **Mount Vernon marble:** a limestone marble about 17.7 mm across, found at the House for Families, the quarter
  where most enslaved people at the Mansion House Farm lived; "It is possible a toy such as this would have
  belonged to one of the many enslaved children living and working at the Mansion Farm during George Washington's
  lifetime" (Mount Vernon "Marble"). The quarter was used from the 1760s to 1792 (search summary of Mount Vernon
  "Housing" page) (unconfirmed: search summary only).
- **Newbery in America:** Isaiah Thomas printed *A Little Pretty Pocket-Book* at Worcester, Massachusetts, in 1787,
  with the "Base-Ball" rhyme and woodcut (Wikisource transcription of the 1787 edition; search summary says the
  Library of Congress holds it). The same book has rhymes on swimming and trap-ball.

## Parked from `rights-movements` era 10 (2026-09-09) — the 2026 school-sports ruling

`rights-movements` tells this as a rights fight. **School and college sport is yours.** Primary
source, downloaded and read locally on 2026-09-09: the slip opinion, **West Virginia v. B.P.J.,
No. 24-43**, 609 U.S. ___ (2026), supremecourt.gov/opinions/25pdf/24-43_2b35.pdf.

- **Argued 13 January 2026. Decided 30 June 2026.** Consolidated with **No. 24-38, Little, Governor
  of Idaho v. Hecox.**
- **Kavanaugh** wrote for the Court, joined by Roberts, Thomas, Alito, Gorsuch and Barrett. Thomas
  and Gorsuch concurred separately. **Sotomayor** filed an opinion concurring in the judgment in
  part and dissenting in part, joined by **Kagan and Jackson**; **Jackson** filed one too.
- **The question:** **"whether, under Title IX and the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth
  Amendment, schools may maintain women's and girls' sports teams for biological females."**
- **Held:** **"Title IX allows schools to provide separate women's and men's sports teams defined by
  biological sex,"** and the West Virginia and Idaho laws do not violate equal protection. The Court
  also held that **"Title VII and Bostock are not relevant in this very different statutory and
  factual context of sports."**
- **The two laws:** Idaho's **Fairness in Women's Sports Act (2020)**, Idaho Code Ann. §33-6203;
  West Virginia's **Save Women's Sports Act (2021)**, W. Va. Code Ann. §§18-2-25d.
- **Scale, from the syllabus:** **"In the past six years, 27 States have enacted laws that maintain
  female sports for biological females."**
- **The Title IX history the opinion sets out, which is useful to this chapter on its own:** Title IX
  became law in **1972**; the **Javits Amendment** of **1974** told HEW to write regulations
  including, for **"athletic activities reasonable provisions considering the nature of particular
  sports"**; in **1975** HEW issued regulations requiring **"equal athletic opportunity for members
  of both sexes"** and authorising **"separate teams for members of each sex where selection for such
  teams is based upon competitive skill or the activity involved is a contact sport"** (34 CFR
  §§106.41(b), (c)).
- **The students.** The West Virginia student is identified in the record only as **B.P.J.**; the
  caption names her mother, **Heather Jackson**. She wanted the girls' cross-country and
  track-and-field teams and, per Sotomayor's opinion, was **11 years old** when she first sought to
  play. **"In the five years since the ban has been in place, B. P. J. is the only transgender girl
  publicly identified in the State who has sought to play sports with other girls."** The Idaho
  student is named in the record as **Hecox**, who competed for a women's club soccer team and tried
  out for the women's Division I track and cross-country teams.
