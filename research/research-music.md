## Era 01: Before 1500 (T-268a, 2026-09-28)

### Broken Flute Cave flutes
- Arizona State Museum, "Flutes, Broken Flute Cave" online exhibit (https://statemuseum.arizona.edu/online-exhibit/flutes-broken-flute-cave, opened): found 1931 by archaeologist Earl Halstead Morris (1889-1956); Prayer Rock district of the Navajo Nation, northeastern Arizona; Broken Flute Cave "the largest of the 15" caves explored; four wooden flutes (catalog A-13994-A, A-13994-B, A-14550, A-14551); dated "620-670 CE"; "the oldest known wooden flutes yet discovered in North America"; exhibit titles them Ancestral Pueblo flutes; transferred to the museum in Tucson by the Carnegie Institution in 1957. Sizes: two about 72.5 to 73.8 cm long, 2.3 to 2.6 cm across; two 68.5 cm long, 2.8 cm across.
- *Arizona Highways*, "A Second Wind" (https://www.arizonahighways.com/article/second-wind, opened): "long tubes crafted from polished boxelder wood," "smooth, even interiors and nearly identical finger holes," "an intentional scale"; two "scattered in the trash above a grave," two "beneath the dirt floor ... intact, tied together"; "red, blue and black feathers" fastened to one end; dated 620 to 670. Musician Michael Graham Allen made a replica about 2000 and recorded it on his 2004 album *Rainbird*.
- Six finger holes, end-blown: Wikipedia "Anasazi flute" (search summary; consistent with the replica makers' pages) (unconfirmed: search summary only for the hole count).
- Land: the cave is in the Navajo Nation today. The makers were Basketmaker III people, ancestors of the Pueblo peoples (ASM exhibit title).

### Hopewell panpipes
- NPS, Hopewell Culture National Historical Park, History and Culture (https://www.nps.gov/hocu/learn/historyculture/index.htm, opened): Hopewell culture "flourished in the woodlands of eastern North America from 1 to 400 CE"; park in Ross County, near Chillicothe, Ohio. No mention of instruments.
- Gina M. Turff and Christopher Carr, "Hopewellian Panpipes from Eastern North America: Their Social, Ritual, and Symbolic Significance," ch. 18 in Carr and Case, eds., *Gathering Hopewell* (2005) (PDF at christophercarrarchaeology.com, read with pypdf): 105 panpipes from 55 sites documented; sites range "from northern Wisconsin and south-central Ontario to the Gulf Coast"; all Middle Woodland; panpipes = "multiple tubes made of cane, reed, or bone that were held together by a jacket made of copper, silver, iron, or a combination"; all with a countable number have three or four tubes, three most common; of 102 jackets with known metal, 81 copper, 11 silver, 10 copper overlaid with silver, one iron (Turner site, Ohio), one iron and copper (Hopewell site, Ohio); recovered "almost entirely from mortuary contexts rather than in habitations."
- Which present-day nations descend from Hopewell peoples: not stated in either source. The outline names no nation for them.

### Haudenosaunee instruments and song kinds
- Onondaga Nation, "Song" (https://www.onondaganation.org/culture/song/, opened): water drum (Gana'jyo:wih) "usually made of cedar trees with deer hide stretched over the top ... Songs are usually sung with one drum"; horn rattle "an animal horn, usually a cow ... dried and hollowed out ... seeds are placed inside"; "The turtle rattle is very sacred to the Haudenosaunee and is only used in a few select ceremonies"; "Ceremonial songs are only performed in the longhouse and are not for public viewing. While social songs can be performed anywhere." Note: the cow horn is a material that came after European cattle arrived. The page does not say what the rattle was made of before that, so the outline says "animal horn."

### Tlingit song ownership
- Christina Mills and Mary Beth Moss, "At.óowu: Tlingit Homeland," NPS Glacier Bay (https://www.nps.gov/articles/aps-v13-i1-c7.htm, updated Aug 29, 2022, opened): "The Tlingit concept of at.óowu, meaning 'something owned or purchased'"; "the stories, songs, clan crests, and regalia commemorating these losses are the Tlingit 'deeds' to place"; names the Chookaneidi clan.
- Search summaries (Thornton et al., *Journal of Ethnobiology* 39:3, 2019, on Mary Sheakley's song): ceremonial songs "are typically owned as property and performed by particular Tlingit matrilineal groups, known as clans, or their house groups"; a song raised to "clan song" becomes at.óow (unconfirmed: search summary only). Useful for era 8 (Sheakley composed near 1900).

## Era 02: The 1500s (T-268a, 2026-09-28)

### Fort Caroline, 1564 (Timucua land)
- Laudonnière, *L'histoire notable de la Floride* (1586), as quoted by the National Huguenot Society via Heidelblog (https://heidelblog.net/2021/11/the-first-huguenot-thanksgiving-in-1564-at-ft-caroline-florida/, opened): "On that day on the beach, I commanded us to sing aloud (like a trumpet), to ends that we assembled render thanks to God, of our arrival felicitous and happy." The day was 30 June 1564 (search summaries; `holidays` bank line 64 holds the same). Holidays tells the thanksgiving. Our angle is only the singing.
- Francis Parkman, *Pioneers of France in the New World* (1865), Project Gutenberg #3721 (read): "They were roused at daybreak by sound of trumpet, and after singing a psalm they set themselves to their task. It was the building of a fort." Also Parkman: Jean Ribault, before he was killed at Matanzas, "recited the Psalm, 'Domine, memento mei.'" Parkman is a secondary source written 300 years later.

### St. Augustine, 1565 (Seloy, a Timucua town)
- Francisco López de Mendoza Grajales, "The Founding of St. Augustine, 1565" (English translation, Saylor PDF, https://resources.saylor.org/wwwresources/archived/site/wp-content/uploads/2011/08/HIST321-9.1.3-The-Founding-of-St.-Augustine.pdf, read with pypdf): "On Saturday, the 8th, the general landed with many banners spread, to the sound of trumpets and salutes of artillery. As I had gone ashore the evening before, I took a cross and went to meet him, singing the hymn Te Deum laudamus ... A large number of Indians watched these proceedings and imitated all they saw done." After the capture of Fort Caroline: "My companions and I walked ahead in a procession, singing the Te Deum laudamus."
- Killings, counts: NPS Fort Matanzas, "The Massacre of the French": 111 killed at the first killing, 134 on 12 October 1565 (as recorded in `research/research-holidays.md` line 71). 111 + 134 = 245. At Fort Caroline on 20 September "most of the men" were killed. Menéndez's own letter (15 Oct 1565) says 132 at Fort Caroline had "their throats cut" and gives his reason, "they spread the Lutheran sect" (holidays bank line 221). `war` owns the telling.

### Drake and the Coast Miwok, June 1579
- Robert F. Heizer, *Francis Drake and the California Indians, 1579* (University of California Press, 1947; Project Gutenberg #36201, read): *The World Encompassed by Sir Francis Drake* (London, 1628), "of uncertain authorship but usually attributed to Francis Fletcher"; sources "the Fletcher manuscript" and others; Drake entered the harbor June 17, 1579 (Old Style) and "remained there for five weeks repairing his ship," probably at Drake's Bay. Quoted text: "In the time of such prayers, singing of Psalmes, and reading of certaine Chapters in the Bible, they sate very attentiuely: and obseruing the end at euery pause, with one voice still cryed, Oh, greatly reioycing in our exercises. Yea they tooke such pleasure in our singing of Psalmes, that whensoeuer they resorted to vs, their first request was Gnaah, by which they intreated that we would sing." Heizer: the words "Hioh and Gnaah seem to be Coast Miwok words"; "Sing" is given as Gnaah.
- Land: Coast Miwok (Heizer).

### Oñate, 1598
- "La Toma," 30 April 1598: "In a makeshift church, the Te Deum was sung and Franciscan priests celebrated a solemn high Mass"; Oñate with about 500 colonists; Villagrá's *Historia de la Nueva México* (1610) records it (Wikipedia and Texas State Historical Association search summaries) (unconfirmed: search summary only).
- John Koegel, "Music and Christianization on the Northern Frontier of New Spain," in *Conversion to Christianity from Late Antiquity to the Modern Age* (UMN Libraries Publishing, https://umnlibraries.manifoldapp.org/read/conversion-to-christianity/section/6eb76c45-30e7-4133-b2a0-207b97d9e57d, opened): the Pueblos "began to hear Spanish military music and perform Catholic plainsong and polyphony (part-singing) on a regular basis" "during the Oñate expedition and its aftermath." (More from Koegel under era 03.)

## Era 03: The 1600s (T-268a, 2026-09-28)

### The Bay Psalm Book
- `research/research-religion.md` line 368 and `research/research-technology.md` line 34 (Library of Congress exhibition "First Among Many"; Britannica): first book published in British North America; Richard Mather and two other ministers; eleven copies survive; *The Whole Booke of Psalmes Faithfully Translated into English Metre*, Cambridge: Stephen Daye, 1640.
- Library of Congress research guide, "Early American Sacred Music" (https://guides.loc.gov/early-american-sacred-music, opened): "the first book published in the colonies"; "The printed music in the Bay Psalm Book, which did not appear until the ninth edition (1698), was of European origin."
- Wikipedia, "Bay Psalm Book" (opened): about 1,700 copies of the first edition; tunes named only, from existing psalters; ninth edition (1698) "the first to contain notated music," 13 tunes from John Playford's music instruction book; a copy sold in 2013 for a hammer price of $14.165 million. (Wikipedia; the LOC guide confirms the 1698 date.) Note on translators: Wikipedia says "thirty pious and learned Ministers" including Richard Mather, Thomas Mayhew and John Eliot. `religion` bank says Mather "and two other ministers." The two accounts differ. The outline says "Richard Mather and other New England ministers."

### Lining out
- *The Directory for the Publick Worship of God* (Westminster Assembly, 1644), "Of Singing of Psalms" (https://thewestminsterstandard.org/directory-for-the-publick-worship-of-god/, opened): "every one that can read is to have a psalm book ... But for the present, where many in the congregation cannot read, it is convenient that the minister, or some other fit person appointed by him and the other ruling officers, do read the psalm, line by line, before the singing thereof."
- Wikipedia, "Lining out" (opened): "taken to American colonies by English and Scottish emigrants"; "a slow, drawn-out heterophonic and often profusely ornamented melody"; turned against in England and New England "in the first quarter of the 18th century" in favor of "regular singing"; today "predominates among the Old Regular Baptist churches" in the US; cites William T. Dargan, *Lining Out the Word* (2006), on Black congregations' "Dr. Watts" hymn singing (lead for eras 6-10).
- The seed's claim that the call-and-response habit "stayed in American church singing for centuries" is supported only as far as the Old Regular Baptist and Dargan leads. The outline no longer makes the broader claim.

### New Mexico mission music and the bans
- John Koegel, "Music and Christianization on the Northern Frontier of New Spain" (UMN Libraries Publishing, opened): Fray Cristóbal de Quiñones (d. 1609), quoted from Vetancurt's *Menologio Franciscano*: "Learning the language of the Queres ... He built the church and convent at San Felipe Pueblo, where he also established a pharmacy and infirmary. He installed an organ to enrich the Divine Service and encouraged music-making by the skilled singers [whom he trained]." Koegel: "perhaps the first trained teacher of European music residing in what is now the U.S. Southwest." Fray Bernardo de Marta "arrived in New Mexico about 1605 ... remembered as a 'great musician and organist of the heavens' who taught Native Americans in many pueblos to sing and play European musical instruments" (d. 1635). Fray Roque de Figueredo (Koegel's text as returned spells "Figuredo") taught "part-singing [canto de órgano] to the boys with the best voices" among the Zuni by 1634. Benavides's supply records (1625-1634): choir-books, "a set of chirimías and a bajón" (shawms and a bassoon). Revolt of 1680: "Some 400 of the Spanish settlers, including twenty-one Franciscans, were killed by the Pueblos."
- The "first" for Quiñones is Koegel's hedged "perhaps the first." The outline quotes it as his.
- Bans: Dennis Zotigh, NMAI, Smithsonian Magazine blog, 2 September 2026, as recorded in `research/research-religion.md` lines 794-805 and `research/research-holidays.md` line 126: "From 1656 to 1665, Franciscan missionary leader Alonso de Posada forbid traditional ceremonies by the Pueblo people and ordered the Spanish missionaries to invade the sacred Pueblo kivas ..., burn their masks and confiscate or destroy their sacred objects." "In 1675 Juan Francisco Treviño ... ordered the arrest of 47 medicine men for practicing sorcery. Four were sentenced to death by hanging; in the end, three were hanged and one committed suicide. The remaining spiritual leaders were humiliated and publicly whipped."
- Correction (same day): the outline no longer says the banned ceremonies were sung and danced. A museum source for katsina songs (Peabody Museum, Harvard, "Rainmakers From the Gods") returned 403, and the only other results were a shop blog. The outline states only what Zotigh states: ceremonies forbidden, kivas entered, masks burned, 47 leaders arrested.

### Africans, 1619, and the silence of the records
- `research/research-slavery-freedom.md` line 30 (NPS; National Geographic; Hampton History Museum): late August 1619, the English privateer *White Lion* landed "20. and odd Negroes" (John Rolfe's words) at Point Comfort, Virginia, people taken from a Portuguese slave ship, from the Ndongo/Kongo region of West Central Africa; the exact day is disputed (August 20 to about August 25).
- OUP blog, "10 fun facts about the banjo" (July 2014, https://blog.oup.com/2014/07/10-fun-facts-about-the-banjo/, opened): "The first definitive description of an early banjo is from a 1687 journal entry by Sir Hans Sloane, an English physician visiting Jamaica, who called this Afro-Caribbean instrument a 'strum strump'"; "The earliest reference to the banjo in North America appeared in John Peter Zenger's The New-York Weekly Journal in 1736"; the banjo's likely forerunners include "more than 60 plucked lute instruments" of West Africa.

Not used as a fact in the outline beyond the plain statement that the records say almost nothing.

## Era 04: 1700 to 1750 (T-268a, 2026-09-28)

### Regular singing and singing schools
- Henry Wilder Foote, "Musical Life in Boston in the Eighteenth Century," *Proceedings of the American Antiquarian Society* (October 1939) (PDF https://www.americanantiquarian.org/proceedings/44807002.pdf, read with pypdf):
  - "By the end of the seventeenth century singing had sunk to a low level in all the colonies."
  - Symmes, of Bradford, Harvard 1689, "The Reasonableness of Regular Singing: or Singing by Note," 1720: "Singing schools and singing books being laid aside, there was no way to learn." Symmes says there had been singing schools "in the early days of the colony."
  - "The originator of the movement was Rev. John Tufts of Newbury, who, about 1712, published 'An Introduction to the Singing of Psalm Tunes in a Plain and Easy Method; with a Collection of Tunes in Three Parts.' The early editions contain twenty-eight, the later ones thirty-eight tunes." "the first book of instruction in music to be printed in the North American Colonies." Footnote: "Neither the title nor the exact date of the first edition is known." "Tufts reinforced his pamphlet by travelling about to lecture and to establish singing-schools." *New England Courant*, 30 Sept 1723: a "Singing Lecture" in Boston at which Tufts preached.
  - DATE DISPUTE: Foote "about 1712"; Wikipedia "John Tufts (music educator)" and several search summaries "c. 1721" (unconfirmed: search summary only for 1721). Both are recorded. The outline states the dispute.
  - Sewall's diary, 20 March 1720/21: "At night Dr. Mather preached in the school house to the young musicians, from Rev. 14:3 ... House was full, and the singing extraordinarily excellent, such as hardly been heard before in Boston. Sang four times out of Tate and Brady."
  - Walter, 1721, "at the age of twenty-five ... The music was printed in modern notation with bar lines for the first time in the colonies." "The vogue for 'regular' singing was, in truth, a youth movement, eagerly taken up by the young people."
  - Symmes after introducing regular singing: "a great part of the town has for near half a year, been in a mere flame about it." Rev. Nathaniel Chauncey of Connecticut, discourse at Hartford, 1728, answering objections: "this practice leads to the Church of England and will bring in organs quickly"; "the very original of this way was from the Papists"; "It looks very unlikely to be the right way, because the young people fall in with it."
  - First advertised Boston concert: *Boston News-Letter*, 16-23 December 1731, "Concert of Music" on sundry instruments at Mr. Pelham's great room, 30 December, tickets "Five Shillings," from Sonneck's *Early Concert Life in America*. Further concerts 1732, 1733, 1736, 1744 (Faneuil Hall), 1747. After the 1744 concert the selectmen received £205.5.0 "for the use of the poor of the town."
- Matt B. Jones, "Bibliographical Notes on Thomas Walter's 'Grounds and Rules of Musick Explained,'" *Proceedings of the AAS* (1932) (PDF https://www.americanantiquarian.org/proceedings/44817363.pdf, read): Walter born 1696, died 10 January 1724/5; Cotton Mather's nephew; title page "BOSTON: Printed by J. Franklin, for S. Gerrish ... 1721"; preface dated 18 April 1721, signed by Increase and Cotton Mather and others; "24 psalm tunes in bars. This was the first engraved music printed in the United States, although the New England Psalms Books had previously printed music in wood-cut"; "continued to be a favorite for more than forty years, during which time it passed through six editions."
- "First" claims: Foote's "first book of instruction" (Tufts) and "bar lines for the first time" (Walter); Jones's "first engraved music." The outline uses Foote's two.

### Stono, 1739, and the drums
- "An Account of the Negroe Insurrection in South Carolina" (1739), text at https://www.latinamericanstudies.org/slavery/stono.htm (opened): "Some Angola Negroes assembled, to the number of Twenty; and one who was called Jemmy was their Captain"; "Several Negroes joyned them, they calling out Liberty, marched on with Colours displayed, and two Drums beating, pursuing all the white people they met with, and killing Man Woman and Child when they could come up to them"; "they were above Sixty, some say a hundred, on which they halted in a field, and set to dancing, Singing and beating Drums, to draw more Negroes to them"; the militia "charged them on foot"; "many ran back to their Plantations ... but they were there taken and Shot, Such as were taken in the field also, were after being examined, shot on the Spot"; "In the whole action about 40 Negroes and 20 whites were killed."
- Attribution: enclosed by James Oglethorpe in a letter to Harman Verelst, 9 October 1739, and printed in the London *Daily Post and General Advertiser*, 17 March 1740 (search summary) (unconfirmed: search summary only).
- Other counts: about 25 white colonists killed and 35 to 50 rebels (`research/research-slavery-freedom.md` line 64: LOC Today in History, Sept. 9; Britannica; BlackPast). The account's own count and the later counts differ. Both are in the outline.

### Negro Act of 1740, section 36
- US History Scene, "Excerpts From South Carolina Slave Code Of 1740 No. 670" (opened through WebFetch, which paraphrased): section XXXVI; passed 10 May 1740, assented by Lieutenant Governor William Bull; masters or overseers who allowed drums, horns or other loud instruments, or public gatherings, forfeited "ten pounds, current money, for every such offence"; masters, overseers and other persons could apprehend any enslaved person off the plantation without a ticket and without a white person, especially Saturday nights, Sundays and holidays; those found with weapons could be "disarm[ed], take[n] up and whip[ped]."
- Exact wording of the drum clause, from search summary (matches the page's excerpt as far as WebFetch returned it): "And for that as it is absolutely necessary to the safety of this Province, that all due care be taken to restrain the wanderings and meetings of negroes and other slaves, at all times, and more especially on Saturday nights, Sundays and other holidays, and their using and carrying wooden swords, and other mischievous and dangerous weapons, or using or keeping of drums, horns, or other loud instruments, which may call together or give sign or notice to one another of their wicked designs and purposes" (unconfirmed: search summary only for the exact words). Direct fetch of the US History Scene page by curl was forbidden (403), and the UNB Laws of Enslavement and Freedom page returned nothing.
- Wikipedia, "Negro Act of 1740" (opened): passed 10 May 1740 "in response to the Stono Rebellion in 1739."
- UNC College of Arts and Sciences, "Sounding the Alarm" (Emily Davis, June 2020, opened): Petal Samuel (UNC) says "the colonial authorities and planters were fearing that certain kinds of sounds could either signal or induce slave rebellions."

## Era 05: 1750 to 1800 (T-268a, 2026-09-28)

### William Billings (story, verified)
- Library of Congress research guide, "Early American Sacred Music" (opened): *The New England Psalm Singer* (1770) "the first publication consisting of sacred music composed entirely by a native-born American."
- David W. Stowe, "America's musical founding father: 'Liberty songs' by a self-taught singer and tanner helped fuel the Revolution," The Conversation, 15 May 2026 (https://theconversation.com/americas-musical-founding-father-liberty-songs-by-a-self-taught-singer-and-tanner-helped-fuel-the-revolution-278382, opened): apprenticed as a tanner at 14; later a tannery near the Liberty Tree; Nathaniel Gould (1853): "one eye blind, one leg shorter than the other, one arm somewhat withered"; frontispiece of the 1770 book engraved by Paul Revere; six tune books, about 340 choral works; soldiers "learned the words by heart" and "carried them to whatever part of the country duty called them"; copyright: the Massachusetts legislature passed a bill in his favor but "the Tory governor refused to sign it" (as returned by WebFetch); 1790s worked as a street cleaner and hog wrangler; wife Lucy died 1795, six children; died 1800, buried in an unmarked grave on Boston Common.

### Newport Gardner (story, verified)
- Sophie Genevieve Lowe, "America's First Published Black Composer?," Early Music America, 19 June 2023 (https://www.earlymusicamerica.org/web-articles/americas-first-published-black-composer/, opened): Occramer (or Occramar) Marycoo; "around 14 years old when brought to America"; enslaver Captain Caleb Gardner, who "helped enslave some 3,912 human beings"; Andrew Law is "the most prominent" theory for his teacher, but "the first known time that Law went to Rhode Island was in 1783, when Marycoo was already in his late thirties"; Josiah Flagg suggested as earlier teacher; "Marycoo probably had a variety of musical teachers in America"; 1791 lottery winnings bought his freedom; home ownership 1807; singing school in Newport; "Crooked Shanks" in *A Number of Original Airs, Duetto's, and Trio's* (1803) credited to him by Eileen Southern, but "new research seems to indicate that the music had been composed prior to the 1803 publication date" (London 1768 and 1770s printings under other titles); anthem words "Hear the words of the LORD, O ye African race.
