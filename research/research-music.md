## Era 01: Before 1500 (T-268a, 2026-09-28)

### Broken Flute Cave flutes
- Arizona State Museum, "Flutes, Broken Flute Cave" online exhibit (https://statemuseum.arizona.edu/online-exhibit/flutes-broken-flute-cave, opened): found 1931 by archaeologist Earl Halstead Morris (1889-1956); Prayer Rock district of the Navajo Nation, northeastern Arizona; Broken Flute Cave "the largest of the 15" caves explored; four wooden flutes (catalog A-13994-A, A-13994-B, A-14550, A-14551); dated "620-670 CE"; "the oldest known wooden flutes yet discovered in North America"; exhibit titles them Ancestral Pueblo flutes; transferred to the museum in Tucson by the Carnegie Institution in 1957. Sizes: two about 72.5 to 73.8 cm long, 2.3 to 2.6 cm across; two 68.5 cm long, 2.8 cm across.
- *Arizona Highways*, "A Second Wind" (https://www.arizonahighways.com/article/second-wind, opened): "long tubes crafted from polished boxelder wood," "smooth, even interiors and nearly identical finger holes," "an intentional scale"; two "scattered in the trash above a grave," two "beneath the dirt floor ... intact, tied together"; "red, blue and black feathers" fastened to one end; dated 620 to 670. Musician Michael Graham Allen made a replica about 2000 and recorded it on his 2004 album *Rainbird*.
- Six finger holes, end-blown: Wikipedia "Anasazi flute" (search summary; consistent with the replica makers' pages) (unconfirmed: search summary only for the hole count).
- Land: the cave is in the Navajo Nation today. The makers were Basketmaker III people, ancestors of the Pueblo peoples (ASM exhibit title).

### Hopewell panpipes
- NPS, Hopewell Culture National Historical Park, History and Culture (https://www.nps.gov/hocu/learn/historyculture/index.htm, opened): Hopewell culture "flourished in the woodlands of eastern North America from 1 to 400 CE"; park in Ross County, near Chillicothe, Ohio. No mention of instruments.
- Gina M. Turff and Christopher Carr, "Hopewellian Panpipes from Eastern North America: Their Social, Ritual, and Symbolic Significance," ch. 18 in Carr and Case, eds., *Gathering Hopewell* (2005) (PDF at christophercarrarchaeology.com, read with pypdf): 105 panpipes from 55 sites documented; sites range "from northern Wisconsin and south-central Ontario to the Gulf Coast"; all Middle Woodland; panpipes = "multiple tubes made of cane, reed, or bone that were held together by a jacket made of copper, silver, iron, or a combination"; all with a countable number have three or four tubes, three most common; of 102 jackets with known metal, 81 copper, 11 silver, 10 copper overlaid with silver, one iron (Turner site, Ohio), one iron and copper (Hopewell site, Ohio); recovered "almost entirely from mortuary contexts rather than in habitations."
- Which present-day nations descend from Hopewell peoples: not stated in either source. The outline names no nation for them.

### Haudenosaunee instruments and song kinds
- Onondaga Nation, "Song" (https://www.onondaganation.org/culture/song/, opened): water drum (Gana'jyo:wih) "usually made of cedar trees with deer hide stretched over the top ... Songs are usually sung with one drum"; horn rattle "an animal horn, usually a cow ... dried and hollowed out ... seeds are placed inside"; "The turtle rattle is very sacred to the Haudenosaunee and is only used in a few select ceremonies"; "Ceremonial songs are only performed in the longhouse and are not for public viewing. While social songs can be performed anywhere." Note: the cow horn is a material that came after European cattle arrived. The page does not say what the rattle was made of before that, so the outline says "animal horn."

### Tlingit song ownership
- Christina Mills and Mary Beth Moss, "At.óowu: Tlingit Homeland," NPS Glacier Bay (https://www.nps.gov/articles/aps-v13-i1-c7.htm, updated Aug 29, 2022, opened): "The Tlingit concept of at.óowu, meaning 'something owned or purchased'"; "the stories, songs, clan crests, and regalia commemorating these losses are the Tlingit 'deeds' to place"; names the Chookaneidi clan.
- Search summaries (Thornton et al., *Journal of Ethnobiology* 39:3, 2019, on Mary Sheakley's song): ceremonial songs "are typically owned as property and performed by particular Tlingit matrilineal groups, known as clans, or their house groups"; a song raised to "clan song" becomes at.óow (unconfirmed: search summary only). Useful for era 8 (Sheakley composed near 1900).

## Era 02: The 1500s (T-268a, 2026-09-28)

### Fort Caroline, 1564 (Timucua land)
- Laudonnière, *L'histoire notable de la Floride* (1586), as quoted by the National Huguenot Society via Heidelblog (https://heidelblog.net/2021/11/the-first-huguenot-thanksgiving-in-1564-at-ft-caroline-florida/, opened): "On that day on the beach, I commanded us to sing aloud (like a trumpet), to ends that we assembled render thanks to God, of our arrival felicitous and happy." ### St. Augustine, 1565 (Seloy, a Timucua town)

### Drake and the Coast Miwok, June 1579
- Robert F. ## Era 03: The 1600s (T-268a, 2026-09-28)

### The Bay Psalm Book
- `research/research-religion.md` line 368 and `research/research-technology.md` line 34 (Library of Congress exhibition "First Among Many"; Britannica): first book published in British North America; Richard Mather and two other ministers; eleven copies survive; *The Whole Booke of Psalmes Faithfully Translated into English Metre*, Cambridge: Stephen Daye, 1640.
- Library of Congress research guide, "Early American Sacred Music" (https://guides.loc.gov/early-american-sacred-music, opened): "the first book published in the colonies"; "The printed music in the Bay Psalm Book, which did not appear until the ninth edition (1698), was of European origin."

## Era 04: 1700 to 1750 (T-268a, 2026-09-28)

### Regular singing and singing schools
- Henry Wilder Foote, "Musical Life in Boston in the Eighteenth Century," *Proceedings of the American Antiquarian Society* (October 1939) (PDF https://www.americanantiquarian.org/proceedings/44807002.pdf, read with pypdf):
  - "By the end of the seventeenth century singing had sunk to a low level in all the colonies."
  - Symmes, of Bradford, Harvard 1689, "The Reasonableness of Regular Singing: or Singing by Note," 1720: "Singing schools and singing books being laid aside, there was no way to learn." Symmes says there had been singing schools "in the early days of the colony."
  - "The originator of the movement was Rev. John Tufts of Newbury, who, about 1712, published 'An Introduction to the Singing of Psalm Tunes in a Plain and Easy Method; with a Collection of Tunes in Three Parts.' The early editions contain twenty-eight, the later ones thirty-eight tunes." "the first book of instruction in music to be printed in the North American Colonies." Footnote: "Neither the title nor the exact date of the first edition is known." "Tufts reinforced his pamphlet by travelling about to lecture and to establish singing-schools." *New England Courant*, 30 Sept 1723: a "Singing Lecture" in Boston at which Tufts preached.
  - DATE DISPUTE: Foote "about 1712"; Wikipedia "John Tufts (music educator)" and several search summaries "c. 1721" (unconfirmed: search summary only for 1721). Both are recorded. The outline states the dispute.
  - Sewall's diary, 20 March 1720/21: "At night Dr. Mather preached in the school house to the young musicians, from Rev. 14:3 ... House was full, and the singing extraordinarily excellent, such as hardly been heard before in Boston. Sang four times out of Tate and Brady."
  - Walter, 1721, "at the age of twenty-five ... The music was printed in modern notation with bar lines for the first time in the colonies." "The vogue for 'regular' singing was, in truth, a youth movement, eagerly taken up by the young people."
  - Symmes after introducing regular singing: "a great part of the town has for near half a year, been in a mere flame about it." Rev. Nathaniel Chauncey of Connecticut, discourse at Hartford, 1728, answering objections: "this practice leads to the Church of England and will bring in organs quickly"; "the very original of this way was from the Papists"; "It looks very unlikely to be the right way, because the young people fall in with it."
  - First advertised Boston concert: *Boston News-Letter*, 16-23 December 1731, "Concert of Music" on sundry instruments at Mr. Pelham's great room, 30 December, tickets "Five Shillings," from Sonneck's *Early Concert Life in America*. Further concerts 1732, 1733, 1736, 1744 (Faneuil Hall), 1747. After the 1744 concert the selectmen received £205.5.0 "for the use of the poor of the town."
- Matt B. Jones, "Bibliographical Notes on Thomas Walter's 'Grounds and Rules of Musick Explained,'" *Proceedings of the AAS* (1932) (PDF https://www.americanantiquarian.org/proceedings/44817363.pdf, read): Walter born 1696, died 10 January 1724/5; Cotton Mather's nephew; title page "BOSTON: Printed by J. This was the first engraved music printed in the United States, although the New England Psalms Books had previously printed music in wood-cut"; "continued to be a favorite for more than forty years, during which time it passed through six editions."
- "First" claims: Foote's "first book of instruction" (Tufts) and "bar lines for the first time" (Walter); Jones's "first engraved music." The outline uses Foote's two.

## Era 06: 1800 to 1850 (T-268b, 2026-09-28)

### What an enslaved singer said the songs meant: Frederick Douglass
- Frederick Douglass, *Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass, an American Slave* (Boston, 1845), chapter 2 (Documenting the American South, UNC, https://docsouth.unc.edu/neh/douglass/douglass.html, opened 2026-09-28). On the enslaved people sent from the outlying farms of Colonel Edward Lloyd's plantation (Talbot County, Maryland) to the Great House Farm for the monthly allowance: "They would compose and sing as they went along, consulting neither time nor tune." Chorus quoted: "I am going away to the Great House Farm! O, yea! O, yea! O!" sung "to words which to many would seem unmeaning jargon, but which, nevertheless, were full of meaning to themselves."
- Same passage: "I did not, when a slave, understand the deep meaning of those rude and apparently incoherent songs. I was myself within the circle." "Every tone was a testimony against slavery, and a prayer to God for deliverance from chains." "I have often been utterly astonished, since I came to the north, to find persons who could speak of the singing, among slaves, as evidence of their contentment and happiness. It is impossible to conceive of a greater mistake. Slaves sing most when they are most unhappy. The songs of the slave represent the sorrows of his heart." "I have often sung to drown my sorrow, but seldom to express my happiness."
- Frederick Douglass, *My Bondage and My Freedom* (New York, 1855) (Documenting the American South, https://docsouth.unc.edu/neh/douglass55/douglass55.html, opened 2026-09-28), p. 98 area (allowance day): "Slaves are generally expected to sing as well as to work. A silent slave is not liked by masters or overseers. 'Make a noise,' 'make a noise,' and 'bear a hand,' are the words usually addressed to the slaves when there is silence amongst them." Teamsters sang as "one means of letting the overseer know where they were, and that they were moving on with the work."
- Same book, pp. 252-253 (Christmas holidays): fiddling, dancing and "jubilee beating" ("Juba" beating: slapping hands on the body as the beat, with improvised words). "Among a mass of nonsense and wild frolic, once in a while a sharp hit is given to the meanness of slaveholders." His example begins "We raise de wheat, / Dey gib us de corn; / We bake de bread, / Dey gib us de cruss; / We sif de meal, / Dey gib us de huss" and ends with a racial slur (do not print the slur; the verse lists work the enslaved did and the scraps they were given). Douglass: "This is not a bad summary of the palpable injustice and fraud of slavery." He calls the holidays "among the most effective means, in the hands of slaveholders, of keeping down the spirit of insurrection."
- Same book, pp. 278-279: in 1836 ("this first day of the year 1836") Douglass, enslaved on William Freeland's farm, planned an escape with five men he names: Henry Harris, John Harris, Sandy Jenkins, Charles Roberts and Henry Bailey. "A keen observer might have detected in our repeated singing of 'O Canaan, sweet Canaan, I am bound for the land of Canaan,' something more than a hope of reaching heaven. We meant to reach the north--and the north was our Canaan." Of "I don't expect to stay / Much longer here. / Run to Jesus--shun the danger": it "had a double meaning. In the lips of some, it meant the expectation of a speedy summons to a world of spirits; but, in the lips of our company, it simply meant, a speedy pilgrimage toward a free state." (The plan was betrayed and failed; Douglass escaped in 1838. `slavery-freedom` tells his escape.)
- This is the strongest documented case of a double meaning: one singer, in his own words, naming the song and the men.

### Songs as signals: Harriet Tubman, in her own words as Sarah Bradford wrote them down (1869)
- Sarah H. Bradford, *Scenes in the Life of Harriet Tubman* (Auburn, N.Y., 1869) (Documenting the American South, https://docsouth.unc.edu/neh/bradford/bradford.html, opened 2026-09-28). Bradford interviewed Tubman. When Tubman left Maryland, pp. 17-19: "Those communications were generally made by singing. They sang as they walked along the country roads, and the chorus was taken up by others, and the uninitiated knew not the hidden meaning of the words." Tubman sang to her relatives, and past her enslaver ("the Doctor"), "I'm sorry I'm gwine to lebe you, / Farewell, oh farewell; / But I'll meet you in the mornin'" and "I'm boun' for de promised land, / On the oder side of Jordan." Bradford: "These words meant something more than a journey to the Heavenly Canaan."
- pp. 26-27, when Tubman came back to lead others north: fugitives hidden in the woods listened for her hymn. Tubman, as Bradford quotes her: "De first time I go by singing dis hymn, dey don't come out to me, till I listen if de coast is clar; den when I go back and sing it again, dey come out. But if I sing: Moses go down in Egypt, Till ole Pharo' let me go ... den dey don't come out, for dere's danger in de way." The safe-signal hymn begins "Hail, oh hail ye happy spirits." Bradford: "I give these words exactly as Harriet sang them to me to a sweet and simple Methodist air."
- Caution for writers: this is Tubman's account through Bradford, a white writer who wrote it down to raise money for Tubman (Bradford's preface). It documents songs used as signals of safe and unsafe. It does not document songs carrying maps.

### The "coded map" claim: "Follow the Drinking Gourd" (who says what)
- Joel Bresler, "Follow the Drinking Gourd: A Cultural History" (https://www.followthedrinkinggourd.org/, pages "Introduction", "Collection Story", "The Song as History", "Teachers' Guide", opened 2026-09-28; copyright 2008-2012):
  - The song was "first published in 1928," by the Texas Folklore Society, in an article by H. B. (Harris Braley) Parks (1879-1958), "a Texas entomologist who was also an amateur folklorist." Parks claimed that an Underground Railroad worker called Peg Leg Joe taught the song on plantations north of Mobile, Alabama, and marked a route up the Tombigbee River to the Tennessee River and the Ohio River at Paducah, Kentucky. The "drinking gourd" is read as the Big Dipper, which points to the North Star.
  - Parks said he heard it three times (North Carolina 1912, Louisville about 1913, Texas 1918), all from Black informants, and had it explained by another person in Texas. Bresler: "It has never been found in its home territory of Alabama"; "No other map songs survive"; Parks's article is "the first instance I have found of 'drinking gourd' used as a coded synonym for the Big Dipper. It doesn't appear in any of the period newspapers or slave testimonies I've searched." Parks's great-uncle, whom Parks said confirmed Peg Leg Joe, Dr. Harris Cowdry, "died in 1875, four years before Parks was born."
  - Lee Hays, of the Almanac Singers and the Weavers, published a rearranged version in 1947 that changed the words to standard English and added the chorus line "For the old man is awaiting for to carry you to freedom." Bresler: escaping slaves could not have sung that line, "since that line was written 80 years after the end of the Civil War." The Hays version appears in about 200 recordings and several children's books.
  - Citing the historians John Hope Franklin and Loren Schweninger: most people who escaped from the Deep South did not head north. Citing J. Blaine Hudson: 600 to 800 people escaped through Kentucky each year. Bresler's own estimate (labelled as his): about two a year from Alabama crossing at Paducah.
- James Kelley, "Song, Story, or History: Resisting Claims of a Coded Message in the African American Spiritual 'Follow the Drinking Gourd'," *Journal of Popular Culture* (April 2008) (Mississippi State University repository and ResearchGate; PDF returned 403): the coded-message claims "rely on a single source—now dead—with no corroborating evidence" and have circulated in classrooms "particularly when Black History Month rolls around" (unconfirmed: search summary only).
- How the prose can state the dispute: Douglass and Tubman (above) documented songs with double meanings and songs used as signals. The claim that "Follow the Drinking Gourd" was a map comes from one 1928 article by Parks, and the best-known words were written by Lee Hays in 1947. Bresler and Kelley doubt it.

### Blackface minstrelsy: Rice, the Virginia Minstrels, Christy (music's angle; `storytelling-evolution` leads on the show and the makeup)
- Wikipedia, "Thomas D. Rice" (opened 2026-09-28): Thomas Dartmouth Rice, born May 20, 1808, New York City; died September 19, 1860, New York. A white actor. By 1827 a traveling actor on New York, Ohio valley and southern stages. "Rice had made the Jim Crow character his signature act by 1832." "Rice claimed to have been inspired by a Black stable groom who was crippled, who sang and danced as he did his work, and even claimed to have bought the man's clothes for 'authenticity.' The time, place and truth of this claim have been disputed." Also (Wikipedia lead): "Rice also adapted and popularized a traditional song of slaves called 'Jump Jim Crow'". Peak popularity about 1832-1844, "sold-out houses"; London 1836. Earliest dated newspaper notice cited: *American Sentinel* (Philadelphia), 11 September 1832, "Mr. Rice will appear and sing Jim Crow."
- David Pilgrim, "Who Was Jim Crow?", Jim Crow Museum, Ferris State University (2000, edited 2023; https://jimcrowmuseum.ferris.edu/who/index.htm, opened 2026-09-28): Rice "happened upon a black person singing the above song -- some accounts say it was an old black enslaved man who walked with difficulty, others say it was a ragged black stable boy ... we know that in 1828 Rice appeared on stage as 'Jim Crow'." "Rice, a white man, was one of the first performers to wear blackface makeup -- his skin was darkened with burnt cork." Route: "from Louisville to Cincinnati to Pittsburgh to Philadelphia and finally to New York in 1832." Path of the name: "By 1838, the term 'Jim Crow' was being used as a collective racial epithet for black people ... By the end of the 19th century ... the phrase Jim Crow was being used to describe laws and customs which oppressed black people." "In 1843, four white men from New York, billed as the Virginia Minstrels, darkened their faces and imitated the singing and dancing of black people. They used violins, castanets, banjos, bones, and tambourines." "In 1845, the Christy Minstrels (for whom Stephen Foster wrote some of his most popular songs) originated many features of the minstrel show": the semicircle, Mr. Tambo and Mr. Bones at the ends, Mr. Interlocutor in the middle. Pilgrim: Rice and his imitators "helped to popularize the belief that black people were lazy, stupid, inherently less human, and unworthy of integration." Rice "became rich and famous" and died in poverty in 1860.
- Date dispute, to state: Pilgrim says 1828 for Rice's first Jim Crow; Wikipedia says the act was his signature "by 1832"; the `storytelling-evolution` outline says sources place it between 1828 and 1832.
- Wikipedia, "Virginia Minstrels" (opened): Dan Emmett, Billy Whitlock, Dick Pelham and Frank Brower, all white. Try-out at the Branch Hotel on the Bowery, New York; first paying audience "probably on January 31, 1843" at the Chatham Theatre (New York Herald, 31 January 1843), then the Bowery Amphitheatre from 6 February 1843. "Unlike earlier blackface acts that featured solo singers or dancers, the Virginia Minstrels appeared as a group in blackface." Instruments: Emmett fiddle, banjo and bones, Brower banjo and bones, Whitlock fiddle, Pelham dance and several instruments.
- Who profited: the white performers and the publishers. Rice "had made a considerable fortune" (New York Times reminiscence cited by Wikipedia). No source read names a Black musician paid, or not paid, by Rice for "Jump Jim Crow." The seed's claim that "much of the music the shows used was taken from Black musicians who were paid nothing" is supported only as far as: Rice's own claim that he copied a disabled Black stable groom and bought his clothes (disputed); Wikipedia's statement that he adapted a slave song; and the "Dixie" dispute (era 07, the Snowden family). See the SEARCHED, NOT FOUND entry below.

### The banjo moves to white performers: Joel Walker Sweeney
- Wikipedia, "Joel Sweeney" (opened 2026-09-28): Joel Walker Sweeney (1810 - October 29, 1860), white, "Born to a farming family in Appomattox County, Virginia, he learned to play the banjo from local enslaved Africans." Performed at county court days in central Virginia in the early 1830s, then with a circus. "By 1839, Sweeney was performing in various blackface venues in New York. His earliest documented use of the banjo on stage was in April 1839. He is the earliest known person to have played the banjo on stage." Toured Britain 1843. The claim that he added the banjo's fifth string: "In fact, there is no proof that Sweeney introduced either innovation"; the short drone string appears on 18th-century four-string banjos and in earlier pictures.
- Pairs with the era 04-05 entries: the banjo's African origin (OUP blog; Jefferson's "brought hither from Africa"; *The Old Plantation*).

### Stephen Foster (story, era 06)
- University of Pittsburgh Library System, "The Life and Music of Stephen Collins Foster" (Center for American Music, https://library.pitt.edu/foster-biography, opened 2026-09-28):
  - Born July 4, 1826, Lawrenceville, Pennsylvania (now part of Pittsburgh); died January 13, 1864, New York. "one of the first American songwriters to earn a living through composition alone." "His songs depicting African Americans, however, have been controversial since they were written."
  - The Foster household used Black indentured servants, "enslavement in all but name." His father wrote on July 14, 1834: "Mrs. Collins made your ma a present of an excellent coloured girl a few days ago, who had upwards of three years to serve. So much saved for girls hire." The Pitt page names her as Kitty.
  - At nine (1835) he sang blackface songs ("Zip Coon," "Long-tailed Blue," "Coal-Black Rose," "Jim Crow") in a boys' theatre in a carriage house and was paid a small weekly sum (his brother Morrison's recollection). "Foster's understanding of African American music was predominantly shaped not through direct contact but rather through songs by white musicians that negatively caricatured blacks."
  - 1846-1847: bookkeeper for his brother Dunning's shipping firm in Cincinnati, which profited "from cotton picked by enslaved blacks in the South."
  - "Oh! Susanna" (1847): first record an advertisement of September 11, 1847, Pittsburgh; published within a year by William C. Peters as one of the "songs of the Sable Harmonists," a minstrel troupe. Pitt calls it one of his "comic songs that disparaged blacks."
  - Married Jane McDowell July 22, 1850; one child, Marion (1851-1935).
  - From 1850, Edwin P. Christy of the Christy Minstrels paid Foster $10 a song for the right to sing it first. For "Old Folks at Home" (1851) Christy paid $15 to premiere it and to be named as its composer.
  - Letter to Christy, May 25, 1852: his songs "have done a great deal to build up a taste for Ethiopian songs among refined people, by making the words suitable to their taste, instead of the trashy and really offensive words which belong to some songs of that order." The Pitt authors (citing Steven Saunders): "It seems more likely that Foster was concerned about writing songs with family-friendly language to maximize his sheet-music sales." Evidence for a turn to abolition is "inconclusive at best." His only openly political songs before the war supported anti-abolitionist Democrats. In 1856 he wrote "The Abolition Show," which attacked Republicans and abolitionists.
  - Frederick Douglass, lecture of March 19, 1855 (Rochester), quoted by Pitt: "Lucy Neal," "Old Kentucky Home," and "Uncle Ned" "awaken the sympathies for the slave, in which anti-slavery principles take root." The Hutchinson Family sang "Old Folks at Home" in their antislavery concerts.
  - About 1857 he sold his rights to future royalties on his published songs to Firth, Pond & Co. for one-time payments. Moved to New York in 1860. There he wrote more minstrel songs, including "A Soldier in de Colored Brigade" (1863), and "Beautiful Dreamer" (1862). In January 1864, ill, he "fell while using the bathroom in his hotel room, leaving serious wounds on his neck and face," was taken to Bellevue Hospital on January 10 and died January 13. Buried in Allegheny Cemetery, Pittsburgh.
- Wikipedia, "Stephen Foster" (opened): Foster "may also have sought to kill himself" (a possibility, not settled); his writing partner George Cooper found him "lying in a pool of blood"; died at 37; his wallet held "37 cents in Civil War scrip and three pennies" and the note "Dear friends and gentle hearts." Lifetime earnings of "just over fifteen thousand dollars in royalties" (unconfirmed: search summary only, attributed to American Music at Pitt).
- American Music at Pitt, "Old Folks at Home" (https://americanmusic.library.pitt.edu/content/old-folks-home, opened): copyright deposit by Firth, Pond & Co., 1851; title page "written and composed by E. P. Christy"; Morrison Foster says he drew up a written disclaimer that Christy signed; Christy refused Foster's 1852 request to restore his name and wrote on the back of the letter that Foster was "a vacillating skunk and plagiarist."
- Wikipedia, "Oh! Susanna" (opened): the original second verse used a racial slur and described an electric explosion that killed "five hundred" Black people (state it without printing the slur). "At the time, no American song had sold more than 5,000 copies of the sheet music; 'Oh! Susanna' sold over 100,000." Later sung by the Forty-Niners with new words.
- Wikipedia, "My Old Kentucky Home" (opened): after Japanese students sang the original words, including "darkies," to the Kentucky General Assembly on March 11, 1986, Representative Carl Hines and State Senator Georgia Davis Powers introduced resolutions to sing "people" instead, and both chambers adopted them. (Who changed the words: legislators in 1986, not Foster.)
- Films: Hollywood biopics *Harmony Lane* (1935), *Swanee River* (1939, Don Ameche), *I Dream of Jeanie* (1952) (Wikipedia, "Stephen Foster"). Fictionalized studio films. Not used as a Movie line.

### Shape notes and *The Sacred Harp*
- Wikipedia, "Shape note" (opened 2026-09-28): shape notes "first appeared in *The Easy Instructor* by William Little and William Smith in 1801 in Philadelphia"; four shapes, each tied to a syllable (fa, sol, la, mi), "intended for use in singing schools."

Singers sit "in a hollow square," four parts (treble, alto, tenor, bass), "always sing a cappella." ### Congo Square, New Orleans
- Wikipedia, "Congo Square" (opened 2026-09-28): the Code Noir (1724) gave enslaved people Sundays off. "It was not until 1817 that the mayor of New Orleans issued a city ordinance that restricted any kind of gathering of enslaved Africans to the one location of Congo Square," at the "back of town" across Rampart Street, where they held a market, sang, danced and played music. In 1819 the architect Benjamin Latrobe wrote in his journal of 500 to 600 enslaved people assembled for dancing, and called it "savage." In 1893 city leaders renamed it Beauregard Square, after the Confederate general. The name Congo Square was restored by city ordinance in 2011.

### Francis Johnson (story, era 06)
- Richard Grant, "Long Before Jazz, Frank Johnson Was Playing the Hottest Music in America," *Smithsonian* magazine, April/May 2023 (https://www.smithsonianmag.com/arts-culture/long-before-jazz-frank-johnson-was-playing-hottest-music-america-180981838/, opened 2026-09-28):
  - "It is often stated without evidence that he was born on the Caribbean island of Martinique. More persuasive is a baptismal certificate from St. Paul's Episcopal Church in Philadelphia for a Francis Johnson, born June 16, 1792." He grew up in a free Black community in Society Hill, Philadelphia.
  - By 15 he played flute, piccolo, violin, bugle and piano, and played in taverns, then as fiddler at the Exchange Coffee House. In 1810 the publisher George Willig asked him for music: "Bingham's Cotillion," "the first musical composition published by a Black American" (Grant). Wikipedia instead dates his first wide notice to Willig's *Johnson's Collection of New Cotillions*, 1818. Record both.
  - "more than 200 compositions published," cotillions, quadrilles, marches, quicksteps, operatic airs. He played the keyed bugle.
  - Firsts per Grant: first Black musician to have sheet music published, to run a training school for Black musicians, to tour widely in the US; "in 1837, he was the first Black American musician to take a band to Europe, where some historians say he played for Queen Victoria." The Victoria meeting is not settled (Wikipedia marks it "citation needed"; Wikipedia says a silver bugle was the queen's gift). State it as "some historians say."
  - November 1837: the four-piece band sailed to Liverpool; concerts at the Argyll Rooms, London, advertised as "First time in Europe of the self-taught men of Color." Homecoming concerts at the Philadelphia Museum, Christmas week 1838, drew about 3,500 people a night.
  - 1842-1843: Johnson and nine Black band members toured New York, Ohio, Indiana, Illinois, Missouri, Kentucky, Virginia and Pennsylvania. "In St. Louis in December 1842, they were served with warrants and charged with being 'Free Negros within the State of Missouri without a license.'" A firemen's association that had hired them paid a lawyer; the court adjourned without a verdict and the band left. "In Allegheny City, Pennsylvania, they faced racist violence. A mob assembled outside the concert venue and attacked the band members in the streets, hurling stones, brickbats, rotten eggs and racial epithets. Several musicians were badly hurt, but the band showed its mettle by playing the second night." Grant names no member of the mob and no hurt musician.
  - A book about Philadelphia published when he was 26 (1818), by the former congressman Robert Waln, called him the "leader of the band at all balls" and "inventor-general of cotillions."
  - Died April 6, 1844, at 51, "from a ruptured aortic aneurysm," in Philadelphia. "his band played a dirge that Johnson had composed 12 years earlier." One of the largest funeral crowds in the city's history. His pupil Joseph G. Anderson then led the band. Twentieth-century music histories dropped him until Richard J. Wolfe's research in the early 1960s.
- Wikipedia, "Francis Johnson (composer)" (opened): June 16, 1792 - April 6, 1844; "more than two hundred compositions"; "Ethiopian minstrel songs" among his genres; staged Haydn's *Creation* in March 1841 at the First African Presbyterian Church.

### The Hutchinson Family Singers
- Wikipedia, "Hutchinson Family Singers" (opened 2026-09-28): John, Asa, Jesse and Judson Hutchinson of Milford, New Hampshire; first concert November 6, 1840, Baptist Meeting House, Milford. ## Era 07: 1850 to 1900 (T-268b, 2026-09-28)

### The Fisk Jubilee Singers, from 1871
- Reavis L. Mitchell Jr., "Jubilee Singers of Fisk University," *Tennessee Encyclopedia* (Tennessee Historical Society, 2017, updated 2018; https://tennesseeencyclopedia.net/entries/jubilee-singers-of-fisk-university/, opened 2026-09-28): In 1871 Fisk University, a school for freed African Americans in Nashville, "faced impending closure"; classes met in "the decaying barracks of the Union army hospital." Treasurer George White organized singing groups from 1867. The American Missionary Association, the school's sponsor, opposed a tour; the local trustees allowed one "without the support of the association." The nine singers: Isaac Dickerson, Maggie Porter, Minnie Tate, Jennie Jackson, Benjamin Holmes, Thomas Rutling, Eliza Walker, Green Evans and Ella Sheppard. At first they sang spirituals only as encores; audiences asked for more. White named them after the biblical "year of jubilee" (Leviticus 25) that freed slaves. 1873: British tour, "culminated in a performance for Queen Victoria." May 1874: back in Nashville having "raised nearly fifty thousand dollars for Fisk University's first permanent structure." Jubilee Hall, six stories, dedicated January 1, 1876.
- Tennessee Historical Commission marker, "Ella Sheppard (Moore)" (hmdb.org, opened): the nine singers "set out on October 6, 1871 to raise funds to save the school"; "After seven years, the Singers had raised $150,000."
- Wikipedia, "Fisk Jubilee Singers" (opened): White, "a white Northern missionary," was Fisk's treasurer and music director. Four men and five women. How many had been enslaved: "seven of the nine" (unconfirmed: search summary only). Bill Newcott, National Geographic, 6 October 2022 (opened): the Fisk students of 1866 were "virtually all of them recently enslaved"; the first tour of about 18 months "raised about $40,000"; "the first nine never earned degrees." After an early concert in Ohio they gave their small profit to victims of the Chicago fire of October 1871; Maggie Porter: "We had thirty dollars and sent every penny to Chicago and didn't have anything for ourselves." In Columbus, Ohio, "lack of funding, poor hotel conditions, and overall mistreatment from the press and audiences." The first US tours earned $40,000; by the end of six weeks around New York they had raised "the full $20,000 White had promised." March 1872: sang for President Ulysses S. Grant at the White House. April 1873: sang "Steal Away to Jesus" and "Go Down, Moses" for Queen Victoria. The European tour of May 1875 to July 1878 "raised an estimated $150,000." Ella Sheppard: "our strength was failing under the ill treatment at hotels, on railroads, poorly attended concerts, and ridicule." Maggie Porter: "There were many times, when we didn't have place to sleep or anything to eat." White and another man "waded through sleet or snow or rain from hotel to hotel seeking shelter for us." Fisk president Erastus Cravath disbanded the original group in July 1878. Jubilee Hall a National Historic Landmark (1975). 2008 National Medal of Arts. Documentary: *Jubilee Singers: Sacrifice and Glory* (PBS *American Experience*; Wikipedia says it aired in 1999). A documentary about the group, usable as the Movie line for Ella Sheppard (she is in it as a member).
- Money figures disagree by source and by period: $20,000 promised and raised by spring 1872 (Wikipedia); $40,000 from the first US tours (Wikipedia); "nearly fifty thousand dollars" by May 1874 (TN Encyclopedia); $150,000 over seven years (TN marker; Wikipedia for 1875-1878). State them with their dates.
- Named refusals: no source read names a hotel or town that turned them away except the general accounts above (Columbus, Ohio, "poor hotel conditions"; Sheppard's "ill treatment at hotels, on railroads"). The outline says hotels refused them in the singers' own words and names no hotel.

### Ella Sheppard (story, era 07)
- Wikipedia, "Ella Sheppard" (opened 2026-09-28): February 4, 1851 - June 9, 1914. Born enslaved at The Hermitage, Andrew Jackson's plantation near Nashville. Her father, Simon Sheppard, hired himself out as a liveryman and cab driver and paid $1,800 for his own freedom. Her mother, Sarah, was not allowed to be bought; the woman who held her said, "She is mine and she shall die mine. Let Simon get another wife." Simon bought Ella. After an 1856 riot in Nashville, white officials tightened rules on free Black people; Simon fell into debt and, afraid his family would be seized and sold, fled to Cincinnati. There a German woman gave Ella piano lessons, and a white teacher taught her in secret at night through the back door. Her father died of cholera in 1866. She taught music in Gallatin, Tennessee, saved "a little more than $6" in five months, and enrolled at Fisk in 1868; the money lasted three weeks, and she paid her way by teaching music and washing dishes. Her mother was sold to a plantation in Mississippi when Ella was three; Ella found her again at 15. Her mother said, "My back was never struck, but my heart is like a checkerboard with its stripes of sorrow." She arranged most of the spirituals for four-part singing, collected more than a hundred songs, played piano, organ and guitar, and conducted from among the singers. She transcribed eight songs in the summer of 1875 for the published collection by J. B. T. Marsh and was not credited. Married the Rev. George Washington Moore in 1882. Rebuilt the Jubilee Singers in 1890.
- Andrew Ward, "Ella Sheppard (1851-1915)," BlackPast.org (2008, opened): born enslaved at the Hermitage in 1851; "about six dollars in five months"; 1868 to Fisk; White appointed her accompanist and assistant director; "continued to collect and transcribe spirituals until the troupe's repertoire numbered over a hundred"; she stood in as director when White resigned in 1878; died in Nashville in 1915.

### Spirituals written down: *Slave Songs of the United States* (1867) and Higginson's camp
- Wikipedia, "Slave Songs of the United States" (opened 2026-09-28): 136 songs, published 1867, "the first, and most influential, collection of spirituals to be published." Collected by three white Northern abolitionists, William Francis Allen, Lucy McKim Garrison and Charles Pickard Ware, mostly from Gullah Geechee people on St. Helena Island, South Carolina, newly freed and living in a camp run by the Union army during the war (the Port Royal Experiment).
- Thomas Wentworth Higginson, *Army Life in a Black Regiment* (Boston, 1869) (Project Gutenberg #6764, opened 2026-09-28). Higginson, a white abolitionist, was colonel of the 1st South Carolina Volunteers, a Union regiment of formerly enslaved men. His camp diary, "Christmas Day" (1862): "'We'll fight for liberty / Till de Lord shall call us home; / We'll soon be free / Till de Lord shall call us home.' This is the hymn which the slaves at Georgetown, South Carolina, were whipped for singing when President Lincoln was elected. So said a little drummer-boy ... 'Dey tink de Lord meant for say de Yankees.'" ### War songs and brass bands, 1861-1865
- Wikipedia, "Battle Hymn of the Republic" (opened 2026-09-28): the tune comes from a camp-meeting hymn, "Say, Brothers, Will You Meet Us" ("Canaan's Happy Shore"), credited to William Steffe, 1856. Soldiers of the 2nd Infantry Battalion of the Massachusetts militia ("Tiger" Battalion) made up "John Brown's Body" to it; it was played publicly at Fort Warren, Boston, on May 12, 1861. Julia Ward Howe heard soldiers sing it at a review near Washington; the Rev. James Freeman Clarke suggested she write new words; she wrote them at the Willard Hotel in November 1861 and "sold it for $4 to The Atlantic Monthly," which printed it on its front page in February 1862.
- Wikipedia, "Dixie (song)" (opened): Bryant's Minstrels, a white blackface troupe, first performed "Dixie's Land" at Mechanics' Hall, New York, on April 4, 1859 (playbill); credited to Dan Emmett of the Virginia Minstrels. On December 20, 1860, a band played it after each vote for secession at St. Andrew's Hall, Charleston. It was played at Jefferson Davis's inauguration, February 18, 1861. Emmett reportedly said that year, "If I had known to what use they were going to put my song, I will be damned if I'd have written it." Lincoln liked it and asked for it at the White House on April 10, 1865. The Snowden claim: the Mount Vernon, Ohio, tradition (from the 1910s or 1920s) that Emmett took the song from the Snowden Family Band, Black musicians of Knox County, Ohio; Ben and Lew Snowden were small children in 1860, so Howard and Judith Sacks suggest their parents, Thomas and Ellen Snowden; "many scholars, such as E. Lawrence Abel, dismiss the Snowden claims outright." Unresolved. State both sides.
- Library of Congress, *Band Music from the Civil War Era*, "The Civil War Bands" (https://loc.gov/static/collections/civil-war-band-music/articles-and-essays/the-american-brass-band-movement/the-civil-war-bands.html, opened 2026-09-28): War Department General Order 49, August 3, 1861, authorized 24 musicians per regimental band; some bands were larger (Frank Rauscher: "some of them numbering as high as fifty pieces"). By October 1861 the War Department forbade further enlistment of regimental bands. A bill of July 17, 1862, announced in General Order 91 of July 29, 1862, mustered out regimental bands in the volunteer service and allowed one band per brigade. Patrick Gilmore's band of 68 pieces went to war with the 24th Massachusetts in 1861; bandsmen worked "as hospital corpsmen." Albert Mann of the 45th Massachusetts: "The bandsmen had been well taught by the surgeon how to give first aid to the wounded, and how to use stretchers, bandages and tourniquets. We were to go with the regiment into battle, rescue the wounded, if possible, and carry them to the field hospital." At Gettysburg, J. A. Leinbach, leader of the 26th North Carolina band, recalled the 26th and 11th North Carolina bands playing "heavy firing going on meanwhile." The British observer named in the LOC essay as J. L. Freemantle, in a tree near Lee's headquarters on Seminary Ridge, heard a Confederate band play "polkas and waltzes" during the cannonade.

### Jenny Lind's tour, 1850-1852
- Wikipedia, "Jenny Lind" (opened 2026-09-28): Swedish soprano (1820-1887). P. T. Barnum proposed 150 concerts in 18 months; she demanded $1,000 a concert, and Barnum agreed in January 1850. ### John Philip Sousa (story, era 07)
- Anthony J. Cade II, "John Philip Sousa and 'Stars and Stripes Forever': A Christmas Story," VA History, U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs (https://department.va.gov/history/sousa/, opened 2026-09-28): born November 6, 1854, near the Marine Corps Barracks, Washington, D.C.; his father Antonio played in the Marine Band and enlisted him as an apprentice musician at 13; he served seven years. "On October 1, 1880, he reported for duty as the 17th band leader for the Marine Band, a position he held for the next 12 years." The tune of "Stars and Stripes Forever" came to him on Christmas Day 1896 on a ship crossing the Atlantic, on his way home after learning of the death of his band manager, David Blakely; he finished it in late April 1897. Congress named it the national march on December 11, 1987. By his death in 1932 he had written over 100 marches and 15 operettas.
- Wikipedia, "John Philip Sousa" (opened): father enlisted him at 13 "to keep him from joining a circus band"; Marine Band 1868-1875 as apprentice ("boy"); leader 1880-1892 under five presidents, Hayes to Benjamin Harrison; played Garfield's (1881) and Harrison's (1889) inaugural balls; formed his own civilian band in 1892; helped develop the sousaphone; died March 6, 1932, Reading, Pennsylvania. Film: *Stars and Stripes Forever* (20th Century Fox, 1952, Clifton Webb as Sousa), loosely based on his memoir *Marching Along*.

### Ragtime and Scott Joplin (story, era 07)
- State Historical Society of Missouri, "Scott Joplin," Historic Missourians (https://historicmissourians.shsmo.org/scott-joplin/, opened 2026-09-28): "born around 1868 in northeastern Texas"; second of six children of Giles and Florence Joplin; family moved to Texarkana, Texas, in the 1870s; father a laborer, mother a house cleaner and laundress; learned piano on her employer's piano; lessons from Julius Weiss. Moved to Sedalia, Missouri, in 1894; Queen City Cornet Band; in 1896 enrolled at George R. Smith College to study music. In 1899 John Stark, a Sedalia music store owner and publisher, printed "Maple Leaf Rag." Moved to St. Louis 1901, New York by late 1907. *Treemonisha*: "considered the first grand opera written by an African American composer," composed 1906-1910; "Between 1911 and 1915, he put on a series of unstaged run-throughs and partial performances," but it "failed to gain financial backing"; first full production January 1972, Morehouse College and the Atlanta Symphony Orchestra. "Sick, discouraged, and poor, Scott Joplin died on April 1, 1917." Buried in St. Michael's Cemetery, New York.
- Wikipedia, "Scott Joplin" (opened): father Giles Joplin "a former slave from North Carolina," mother Florence Givens "a freeborn African-American woman from Kentucky." Birth date: early biographers gave November 24, 1868; Edward A. Berlin found he was "likely born between June 1867 and mid-January 1868"; birthplace disputed (Texarkana or Linden, Texas). In July 1891 he was recorded in Texarkana as a member of the Texarkana Minstrels, raising money for a monument to Jefferson Davis. "Churches and brothels were among the few options for steady work" for Black pianists. Chicago World's Fair 1893. His first opera, *A Guest of Honor* (1903), was confiscated with his belongings for unpaid bills and is lost. 1911: published *Treemonisha* at his own cost; 1915 read-through in a Harlem hall with only Joplin at the piano. By 1916 he had tertiary syphilis (neurosyphilis); admitted to Manhattan State Hospital, a mental hospital, on February 2, 1917; died there April 1, 1917, "of syphilitic dementia"; buried in a grave with no marker for 57 years, marked in 1974. Pulitzer Prize, posthumous, 1976.

It spreads by "direct contact with a syphilis sore" during sex. Neurosyphilis, when it spreads to the brain and nervous system, can cause "severe headache, muscle weakness and/or trouble with muscle movements, changes to your mental state (trouble focusing, confusion, personality change) and/or dementia." ### Recording begins: George W. Johnson
- `research/research-technology.md` line 121 (Rutgers Edison Papers): John Kruesi built the tinfoil phonograph to Edison's design in early December 1877; Edison recorded "Mary had a little lamb" on December 6, 1877. `technology` leads on the machine.

- Wikipedia, "George W. Johnson (singer)" (opened 2026-09-28; cites Tim Brooks, *Lost Sounds*, 2004): George Washington Johnson, October 29, 1846 - January 23, 1914, born in Virginia (Fluvanna County or near Wheatland, Loudoun County). "His father was a slave, and he was likely freed in 1853." ### Native music: the 1883 ban on dances, Carlisle's band, and the first recordings

### Sousa against "canned music," 1906, and the 1909 copyright law
- Kat Eschner, "John Philip Sousa Feared 'The Menace of Mechanical Music,'" *Smithsonian* (Nov. 6, 2017; opened by curl): Sousa's 1906 essay "The Menace of Mechanical Music" warned that mechanical music was "sweeping across the country with the speed of a transient fashion in slang or Panama hats" and was becoming a "substitute for human skill, intelligence and soul." - Wikipedia, "Copyright Act of 1909": in February 1908 the Supreme Court (*White-Smith v. Apollo*) ruled that piano-roll makers did not have to pay composers. The 1909 Act created "the first compulsory license," so anyone could record a song without the owner's consent if they paid the set royalty. The family fled pogroms (attacks on Jewish villages) and reached Ellis Island on September 14, 1893. His father, a cantor, died when he was 13. He was a singing waiter at the Pelham Cafe about 1906. First published song, "Marie from Sunny Italy," 1907, for 33 cents (the retired art-music park said 37 cents; Wikipedia says 33). "Alexander's Ragtime Band," 1911. He "could only play in the key of F-sharp" and "could not read sheet music"; he used pianos with a lever that changed the key. "God Bless America," written 1918, revised and introduced by Kate Smith in 1938; he gave all its royalties to the Boy Scouts and Girl Scouts. "White Christmas," 1942. He owned his own publishing company. Died September 22, 1989, in New York, aged 101. `immigration` tells his arrival (slug `irving-berlin`).

### The blues: the first written accounts, 1903
- Charles Peabody, "Notes on Negro Music," *Journal of American Folk-Lore* 16:62 (July-September 1903), pp. 148-152 (full text, JSTOR Early Journal Content via archive.org, opened): "During May and June of 1901 and 1902 I was engaged in excavating for the Peabody Museum of Harvard University a mound in Coahoma County, northern Mississippi." His workers were Black men "nine to fifteen in number," "imported from Clarksdale, fifteen miles distant." They sang while digging; "a singer of good voice and endurance is sometimes hired for the very purpose of arousing and keeping up the energy of labor." They sang hymns and "ragtime," and made up lines about "hard luck," love and pay day. Lines he wrote down: "They had me arrested for murder / And I never harmed a man." Peabody added a racist aside after that line. He does not use the word "blues." He dug up a Native mound (the Coahoma County mounds; nation not named in the article).
- Mississippi Blues Trail marker, "W. C. Handy Encounters the Blues," Tutwiler (msbluestrail.org, opened): Handy "was waiting for a train here at the Tutwiler railway station circa 1903 when he heard a man playing slide guitar with a knife and singing 'Goin' where the Southern cross' the Dog.'" Handy later published it as "Yellow Dog Blues." Handy led an orchestra in Clarksdale 1903-1905 and played dances for white and Black audiences. "Handy's writings never gave a specific date for the Tutwiler event"; the US Senate accepted 1903 when it named 2003 the "Year of the Blues." The guitarist is not named.
- Handy's own words, from his autobiography *Father of the Blues* (1941): the guitarist played "the weirdest music I had ever heard" (unconfirmed: search summary only).
- Wikipedia, "W. C. Handy": born November 16, 1873, Florence, Alabama; died March 28, 1958, New York. He sold "The Memphis Blues" (1912) for $100 (the buyer, Theron Bennett, is named in other accounts; not opened). "Saint Louis Blues," 1914. With the banker Harry Pace he ran the publishing firm Pace and Handy. He called himself the "Father of the Blues"; Jelly Roll Morton disputed it in a letter to *Down Beat*. He went blind after a fall from a subway platform in 1943.
- Wikipedia, "Ma Rainey": born Gertrude Pridgett, April 26, 1886 (most sources) or 1882. She said a girl in Missouri in 1902 sang her a song about a man who left a woman, and she began singing it. Paramount signed her in 1923; she made her first eight records in Chicago that December and "more than 100 other recordings over the next five years." Paramount advertised her as the "Mother of the Blues." Died December 22, 1939, Columbus, Georgia. 1925 arrest at a party with women from her chorus (Queer Cultural Center via Wikipedia; not used).

### Mamie Smith, "Crazy Blues," 1920, and "race records"
- Wikipedia, "Crazy Blues": recorded August 10, 1920, Okeh Records 4169-A, written by Perry Bradford. "Within two months of release, it had sold 75,000 copies at one dollar a piece." It "opened up a market for race records, and for the first time major record companies started producing records with an African American buyer in mind." Wikipedia calls it "the first significant hit recording in the blues genre"; another claim is "the first recording with a blues title by a black artist." The seed's "first blues record by a Black singer" is narrowed to these words. Her pay is not given.
- Wikipedia, "Ralph Peer": Peer supervised the "Crazy Blues" session for Okeh; later recorded Fiddlin' John Carson (1923).
- Wikipedia, "Race record": Okeh coined the term "race records" in 1922 (Wikipedia; other accounts credit Peer personally, not opened). Okeh, Paramount, Vocalion, Victor and others sold them. Black Swan Records, founded 1921 by the Black businessman Harry Pace, paid fair wages and let artists use their own names; Paramount took it over in 1924. In June 1949 *Billboard* renamed its "race records" chart "rhythm and blues" at the suggestion of the writer Jerry Wexler.

### Bessie Smith (story)
- Wikipedia, "Bessie Smith": 1900 census gives July 1892; 1910 census and later documents give April 15, 1894, which her family kept as her birthday. Chattanooga, Tennessee; orphaned young, she and her brother Andrew sang and danced for coins on the streets. 1912: hired as a dancer in the Moses Stokes company, which had Ma Rainey. Signed to Columbia in 1923 by Frank Walker; first session February 15, 1923 (other sources say February 16).
- Cary O'Dell, Library of Congress National Recording Registry essay, "Down Hearted Blues" (added 2002; PDF opened): Walker heard her in a club in Selma, Alabama. "Down Hearted Blues," by Alberta Hunter and Lovie Austin, recorded 1923. She was 5 feet 9 inches tall. She toured with her own railroad car. More than 10,000 people attended her funeral in Philadelphia. Her grave was unmarked for more than 30 years until Janis Joplin helped buy a headstone.
- *Contemporary Musicians* and *Contemporary Black Biography*, via Encyclopedia.com (opened): "Smith's contract paid her $125 per usable recording, with no provision for royalties"; later contracts: twelve recordings at $150 each, then twelve at $200. "The public bought an astounding 780,000 copies within six months" (of "Down Hearted Blues"). Her weekly pay on tour "peaked at $2,000."
- American Blues Scene, "Blues Law: Bessie Smith vs. Columbia Records" (2015, opened): Columbia paid her "an average of $200 per recording with no royalties," while white artists were paid more and got royalties. In *Gee v. CBS* (Jack Gee Jr., son of her estranged husband), the courts ruled for CBS/Columbia on the statute of limitations (the time limit for suing), not on whether she was cheated.
- Death (Wikipedia, "Bessie Smith"): September 26, 1937, on Route 61 near Clarksdale, Mississippi. Her partner Richard Morgan was driving and hit the back of a slow truck; the truck's tailgate sheared off the roof of her Packard. "Her right arm was almost completely severed at the elbow." The first person there was a white Memphis surgeon, Dr. Hugh Smith (no relation). She was taken to the G. T. Thomas Afro-American Hospital in Clarksdale, where her right arm was amputated (cut off), and she died that morning without waking. John Hammond wrote in *Down Beat* (November 1937) that she died because a white hospital refused her. Dr. Smith: "The Bessie Smith ambulance would not have gone to a white hospital; you can forget that." The biographer Chris Albertson "puts this myth firmly to rest" (*Contemporary Musicians*). The Hammond story is not documented.

### Robert Johnson
- Wikipedia, "Robert Johnson": born May 8, 1911 (uncertain), Hazlehurst, Mississippi. Recorded November 23-25, 1936, in room 414 of the Gunter Hotel, San Antonio, and June 19-20, 1937, in Dallas, for Don Law of the ARC group (issued on Vocalion): 29 songs. "Terraplane Blues" sold about 10,000 copies. Died August 16, 1938, aged 27, near Greenwood, Mississippi, "of unknown causes"; poisoning is told but not proven. The crossroads story first belonged to Tommy Johnson. In 1998 Mississippi courts named his son Claud Johnson sole heir, worth more than $1 million in royalties. His pay for the sessions is not given.

### Jazz: New Orleans, the first record, Armstrong, the Cotton Club
- Wikipedia, "Original Dixieland Jass Band": recorded "Livery Stable Blues" and "Dixieland Jass Band One-Step" on February 26, 1917, at Victor's New York studio; "released as Victor 18255 in May 1917, the first issued jazz record." Members: Nick LaRocca, Larry Shields, Eddie Edwards, Henry Ragas, Tony Sbarbaro. The band called itself "the Creators of Jazz." John Edward Hasse, "Was the First Jazz Recording Made by a Group of White Guys?", *Smithsonian* (2017, opened by curl): "a group of five white musicians gathered in the New York City recording studios of the Victor Talking Machine Company" on February 26, 1917. "Yet the first band to make a jazz record was white." LaRocca later made "racist remarks" and claimed "that he invented jazz." By legend, Freddie Keppard, a Black New Orleans cornetist, turned down a Victor offer in late 1915 (he did not want others to "steal his stuff" or would not audition unpaid); Hasse calls it a legend. "Livery Stable Blues, by some estimates, sold more than one million copies." The band played at Reisenweber's Cafe in New York.
- Wikipedia, "Louis Armstrong": "believed to have been born in New Orleans on August 4, 1901"; he "often claimed he was born on July 4, 1900." The August 4, 1901 date is from a baptismal record found in the mid-1980s by the historian Tad Jones at Sacred Heart of Jesus Church (unconfirmed: search summary only). Arrested December 31, 1912, for "firing a blank into the air" with his stepfather's gun; "sentenced the next day to detention at the Colored Waif's Home," where Peter Davis taught him in the band. 1922: to Chicago at King Oliver's invitation, second cornet in the Creole Jazz Band. Hot Five and Hot Seven records for Okeh from November 1925. Died July 6, 1971, New York.
- Little Rock, 1957: Ricky Riccardi, "Louis Armstrong and Little Rock: 60 Years Later," *Dippermouth* blog (Riccardi is director of research collections at the Louis Armstrong House Museum; opened): on September 17, 1957, in Grand Forks, North Dakota, Armstrong told the reporter Larry Lubenow: "The way they are treating my people in the South, the government can go to hell." He called President Eisenhower "two faced" with "no guts." Consequences: station WBKJ in Hattiesburg, Mississippi, threw out his records (with Eartha Kitt's and Lena Horne's) on September 21; the University of Arkansas withdrew a prom invitation; Rep. Adam Clayton Powell said Armstrong "didn't know what he was talking about"; Sammy Davis Jr. criticized him for playing to segregated audiences. After Eisenhower sent troops on September 24, Armstrong telegraphed him: "If you decide to walk into the schools with the little colored kids, take me along Daddy. God bless you." A Soviet tour: Armstrong said he would not go; Riccardi writes that no formal State Department tour to the USSR existed in 1957. This is era 09 material (1957).
- Wikipedia, "Cotton Club": Owney Madden, a bootlegger and gangster, took it over in 1923 after prison at Sing Sing. "The Cotton Club was a whites-only establishment with rare exceptions for black celebrities such as Ethel Waters and Bill Robinson." Chorus girls had to be "tall, tan, and terrific": at least 5 feet 6 inches, light-skinned and under 21. Menus showed Black men and women dancing near-naked around a drum in a jungle. "Duke Ellington's orchestra was the house band from December 4, 1927, until June 30, 1931." "Responding to Ellington's request, the club slightly relaxed its policy of segregation." Closed 1936 after the Harlem riot, reopened in midtown, closed 1940.

### Country on record: Bristol 1927, the Carters, Lesley Riddle
- Wikipedia, "Bristol sessions": July 25 to August 5, 1927, third floor of the Taylor-Christian Hat and Glove Company, Bristol, Tennessee; Ralph Peer for Victor; 76 songs by 19 performers or groups. Performers got "$50 cash on the spot for each side cut, and 2½ cents for each single sold." Ernest Stoneman July 25; the Carter Family August 1-2; Jimmie Rodgers August 4. Peer kept the publishing rights.
- Wikipedia, "Ralph Peer": about 1921 (at Victor, later), instead of a salary, Peer took "a royalty of one cent per record side that he would divide with the artist" and kept the publishing (copyright) of the songs he recorded, including old traditional songs; Southern Music Publishing, 1928. Wikipedia's claim that he earned about $1 million a year is not used.
- New World Encyclopedia, "Carter Family" (search summary only; unconfirmed): A. P. Carter "would benefit financially with each new song he collected and copyrighted"; Peer wanted copyrights for Southern Music and pressed the Carters for new material.
- Wikipedia, "Maybelle Carter": born May 10, 1909, Nickelsville, Virginia; died October 23, 1978, Hendersonville, Tennessee. Her style: "thumb to play the melody on the bass strings" and "index finger to fill out the rhythm," which "helped to turn the guitar into a lead instrument." "Wildwood Flower," first recorded 1928. Led Mother Maybelle and the Carter Sisters; toured with her son-in-law Johnny Cash from 1968.
- Wikipedia, "Lesley Riddle": June 13, 1905 to July 13, 1979, a Black guitarist from Burnsville, North Carolina. In 1927 at a cement plant he tripped on an auger and his right leg was amputated at the knee. He met A. P. Carter in December 1928; on song-collecting trips "Riddle would act as a 'human tape recorder,' memorizing the melody while Carter gathered lyrics." Songs he brought include "Cannonball Blues" and "Hello Stranger." Maybelle took elements of his guitar playing into hers. The page does not say whether he was credited or paid.
- Longreads (June 16, 2020, on Elamin Abdelmahmoud's *Rolling Stone* piece): Riddle's role "historically erased." No figures.

### The Grand Ole Opry and DeFord Bailey (story)
- Wikipedia, "Grand Ole Opry": began as the *WSM Barn Dance* on November 28, 1925, in the studio of the National Life & Accident Insurance Company, which owned WSM, in Nashville. George D. Hay started it with the 77-year-old fiddler Uncle Jimmy Thompson. On December 10, 1927, after an NBC classical program, Hay said: "For the past hour, we have been listening to music largely from Grand Opera, but from now on, we will present 'The Grand Ole Opry.'" NBC carried one hour nationally 1939-1956.
- Wikipedia, "DeFord Bailey": December 14, 1899 to July 2, 1982. Hay's line followed Bailey's "Pan American Blues." Paid a fixed $5 at first. On tour Bailey could not get food or rooms; Roy Acuff recalled "He could draw a crowd"; bandmates brought him meals or got him into hotels by the fire escape. Later ran a shoeshine stand and rented rooms; rarely played in public until 1974.
- Country Music Hall of Fame and Museum, "DeFord Bailey" (opened): born in Smith County, Tennessee. "Polio, which struck Bailey at age three, stunted his growth and left his back somewhat bent." Family played "Black hillbilly music." On WSM's Barn Dance by June 1926. For fifteen years "one of the program's best loved, and highest paid, stars" (punctuation adapted). Toured with Roy Acuff, Bill Monroe and Uncle Dave Macon and met Jim Crow segregation's "routine hardships and indignities." WSM fired him late in 1941 during the ASCAP dispute; the station "warned Bailey against playing his ASCAP numbers, several of which were listener favorites." Inducted into the Hall of Fame in 2005.
- Pay dispute between sources: Wikipedia ($5 fixed, less than touring companions) vs. the Hall of Fame ("highest paid"). State both.
- George D. Hay, *A Story of the Grand Ole Opry* (1945), quoted via search results (Saving Country Music, Steven Paul, Feb. 19, 2025, opened, gives "lazy" and "refused to learn new tunes"; full sentence from search summary only): "Like some members of his race and other races DeFord was lazy. He knew about a dozen numbers ... but he refused to learn more, even though his reward was great." Bailey later said Hay had been kind to him and that the book was written to please Hay's bosses (Saving Country Music).
- ASCAP and BMI (Wikipedia, "Broadcast Music, Inc."): the National Association of Broadcasters founded BMI in 1939 after ASCAP announced a large increase in fees. For 1941 "the vast majority of U.S. radio stations, and all three radio networks, refused to renew their ASCAP licenses," and played only BMI and public-domain music. BMI became the first licensing group to represent writers of blues, jazz, rhythm and blues, gospel, country, folk and Latin music, which ASCAP had not wanted.

### Radio, records and the union ban
- University of Michigan Press, *Phonograph Boom* (introduction PDF, ISBN 9780472131037, opened): "recording industry sales peaked in 1921 at $106 million, a pinnacle not reached again until 1945." The 1929 stock market crash pushed the Edison company out of records.
- Unit sales fell from over 100 million records in 1929 to about 6 million in 1932 (unconfirmed: search summary only). IASA, "A brief history of the record industry" (opened): in the early 1930s sales fell "to one tenth of the previous decade's level"; "not until the 1950s did record sales again reach the peak level of 1929." (The two sources disagree on when the peak was matched: 1945 in dollars vs. the 1950s.)
- Wikipedia, "1942-1944 musicians' strike": on August 1, 1942, the American Federation of Musicians under its president James C. Petrillo stopped recording for the record companies. Musicians wanted pay when records replaced live players on radio and jukeboxes. Decca settled in September 1943 with payments for each record to a union-run relief fund; RCA Victor and Columbia gave in on November 11, 1944, on similar terms. Singers could still record with voices only, so singers replaced big bands as the stars.

### Gospel: Thomas A. Dorsey and Mahalia Jackson
- Wikipedia, "Thomas A. Dorsey": July 1, 1899, Villa Rica, Georgia, to January 23, 1993. As "Georgia Tom" he played piano for Ma Rainey and recorded "It's Tight Like That" with Tampa Red in 1928. His wife Nettie and their newborn son died in August 1932, and he wrote "Take My Hand, Precious Lord." In 1932-1933 he founded Dorsey House of Music, "the first Black-owned gospel publishing company," and the National Convention of Gospel Choirs and Choruses. Music director at Pilgrim Baptist Church, Chicago, for fifty years. Called the "Father of Gospel Music." His words: "I'm not ashamed of my blues. It's all the same talent."
- Wikipedia, "Mahalia Jackson": October 26, 1911, New Orleans, to January 27, 1972. Came to Chicago in December 1927. Dorsey trained her. She refused to sing blues even when Decca offered to keep recording her if she did. "Move On Up a Little Higher" (1947) sold 50,000 copies in Chicago and two million nationwide. Carnegie Hall, 1950 (Wikipedia says the approach came "the next year," the concert usually dated October 1950; not settled here). Montgomery bus boycott, 1956; sang "I've Been 'Buked and I've Been Scorned" at the March on Washington, August 28, 1963, at King's request; sang "Take My Hand, Precious Lord" at his funeral in 1968.

### Judge Jackson and *The Colored Sacred Harp*, 1934
- Wikipedia, "Judge Jackson": March 12, 1883, Montgomery County, Alabama, to April 7, 1958, Ozark, Alabama. "Judge" was his given name. *The Colored Sacred Harp* (1934), 77 songs, 18 of them his own. "Jackson and an associate, Bishop J.D. Walker, paid out of pocket to print 1,000 copies"; Jackson sold it door to door and at singing conventions.

### Lead Belly and the Lomaxes
- Wikipedia, "Lead Belly": Huddie Ledbetter, January 20, 1889 (uncertain) to December 6, 1949. Convicted in Texas of killing a relative, Will Stafford; imprisoned 1918, at Sugar Land from 1920; pardoned in 1925 by Governor Pat Neff after singing him a song asking for freedom, having served the minimum seven years of a 7-to-35-year sentence. 1930: sent to Angola, the Louisiana State Penitentiary, for stabbing a man in a fight. John Lomax and his son Alan recorded him there in 1933. Released August 1, 1934; "a prison official later wrote to John Lomax denying that Ledbetter's singing had anything to do with his release." After a quarrel over money in March 1935, Ledbetter got his earnings and release from the management contract. The Weavers' 1950 "Goodnight Irene" reached number one. Died of ALS (amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, a disease that kills the nerves that move the muscles) in New York.
- Wikipedia, "John Lomax": honorary curator of the Library of Congress Archive of American Folk Song from 1934 at "a salary of one dollar." From June 1933 he and Alan recorded in Southern prisons, looking for music "not yet influenced by jazz and the radio." Hired Ledbetter as "driver and assistant" in September 1934. Accused of "paternalism" and of shaping Ledbetter's songs and clothes.
- Brian Lukasavitz, "Blues Law: Lead Belly vs. Lomax," American Blues Scene (Jan. 13, 2014, opened): the January 1935 contract split performance fees and royalties and assigned copyrights; "It is unlikely that Lead Belly's suit was ever filed"; the settlement was one lump-sum payment and a share of royalties from Lomax's book *Negro Folk Songs as Sung by Lead Belly*.
- Wikipedia, "Goodnight, Irene": published in 1936 "in Lomax's version" as "a joint Ledbetter-Lomax composition." The Weavers' 1950 record sold 2 million copies and was No. 1 for 13 weeks. Lomax did not write it: he recorded it (the credit is the record's fact; "had not written a single line" is from a search summary only).

### Woody Guthrie (story)
- Wikipedia, "Woody Guthrie": born July 14, 1912, Okemah, Oklahoma. His mother Nora had Huntington's disease and was put in a hospital. Joined the "Okies" going to California in the Dust Bowl years; sang on KFVD radio in Los Angeles. *Dust Bowl Ballads* for Victor, 1940. His guitar read "This machine kills fascists." Merchant Marine 1943-1945. Huntington's diagnosed 1952; in Greystone Park and Creedmoor hospitals; died October 3, 1967.
- Wikipedia, "This Land Is Your Land": written February 23, 1940, at the Hanover House hotel in Manhattan, titled "God Blessed America for Me," an answer to Irving Berlin's "God Bless America," which Kate Smith sang on the radio. First recorded April 1944 by Moses Asch; published September 1945. The manuscript had a verse about a "no trespassing" sign and a verse about hungry people at a relief office; these were often left out. Manuscript kept by the Woody Guthrie Center (Library of Congress holds copies). A 2016 lawsuit over its copyright ended when the plaintiff paid for a license. (Lyrics are not reproduced: describe the verses.)
- Robert C. Carriker, "Ten Dollars a Song: Woody Guthrie Sells His Talent to the Bonneville Power Administration," *Columbia Magazine* 15:1 (Spring 2001, PDF opened): in May 1941 BPA director Paul Raver gave Guthrie "a one-month contract worth $266"; sworn in May 13; Stephen Kahn of BPA's information division "demanded that Woody produce three pages of songs each day"; "In his single month of employment Guthrie wrote 26 songs"; Kahn later: "I don't think the government has ever gotten a better investment on its money." (Wikipedia and search summaries: $266.66.) The songs were for a film promoting federal dams on the Columbia River.
- Wikipedia, "Roll On, Columbia, Roll On": one of the 26; official folk song of Washington State since 1987. The dams (Grand Coulee, Bonneville) flooded Native fishing places; Celilo Falls was flooded in 1957 by The Dalles Dam (not in pages opened; `native-nations` leads).
- Huntington's disease: an inherited disease in which nerve cells in the brain break down, causing uncontrolled movements and loss of thinking and memory MedlinePlus Genetics (NIH, opened): "Huntington's disease is a progressive brain disorder that causes uncontrolled movements, emotional problems, and loss of thinking ability (cognition)." One changed copy of the gene, from one parent, is enough to cause it.

### Billie Holiday and "Strange Fruit" (story)
- Wikipedia, "Strange Fruit": written by Abel Meeropol, a Bronx schoolteacher and Communist Party member, under the name Lewis Allan, after "Lawrence Beitler's photograph of the 1930 lynching of Thomas Shipp and Abram Smith" in Marion, Indiana; published as "Bitter Fruit" in January 1937 in *The New York Teacher*. Holiday first sang it at Café Society in 1939: "Holiday would close with it; the waiters would stop all service in advance; the room would be in darkness except for a spotlight on Holiday's face; and there would be no encore." Columbia would not record it; John Hammond declined, worried about Southern record sellers and CBS radio stations. Milt Gabler of Commodore Records recorded it April 20, 1939. It became her best-selling record, about a million copies. What the song describes (from the song, described not quoted): Black people's bodies hanging from trees in the South after lynchings, with bulging eyes and twisted mouths, and the smell of burning flesh.
- Equal Justice Initiative calendar, August 7 (opened): on August 7, 1930, "a white mob broke into the Grant County Jail in Marion, Indiana," to lynch three young Black men accused of murdering a white man and sexual assault. Thomas Shipp and Abram Smith, both 19, "were severely beaten and hanged." James Cameron, 16, was beaten and survived when someone in the crowd said he was innocent. The mob numbered "between 5,000 and 10,000." Spectators took body parts as "souvenirs." The NAACP named 27 suspects; all-white juries acquitted the leaders. Cameron was convicted as an accessory and served four years; Indiana pardoned him in 1993. (Search summary adds that the mob used sledgehammers and hanged Shipp from a jail window before hanging both from a tree on the courthouse square; unconfirmed: search summary only.)
- Wikipedia, "Abel Meeropol": February 10, 1903 to October 29, 1986; taught English at DeWitt Clinton High School for 17 years; "Strange Fruit" and other songs "provided most of the royalty income of the family" (his son Robert Meeropol). He and his wife Anne adopted the two sons of Julius and Ethel Rosenberg after their parents were executed.
- Wikipedia, "Billie Holiday": born Eleanora Fagan, April 7, 1915, Philadelphia; grew up in Baltimore; at nine sent to the House of the Good Shepherd, a Catholic reform school, for missing school. With Artie Shaw's white band in 1938 she was told to use the service elevator at the Lincoln Hotel in New York because white guests complained. Arrested May 16, 1947, for narcotics possession in her New York apartment; in court May 27: "It was called 'The United States of America versus Billie Holiday'. And that's just the way it felt." Pleaded guilty; sent to Alderson Federal Prison Camp, West Virginia. The conviction cost her the New York City Cabaret Card (the police license needed to work where alcohol was sold). Arrested again January 22, 1949, at the Hotel Mark Twain in San Francisco by the federal agent George Hunter White. The journalist Johann Hari (2015) wrote that Anslinger's bureau targeted her from 1939 because of "Strange Fruit"; the jazz writer Lewis Porter disputes this, noting the claim first appears in Hari's book. On May 31, 1959, federal narcotics agents arrested her in her bed at Metropolitan Hospital, New York, where she was being treated for liver and heart disease, and handcuffed her to the bed. She died July 17, 1959, aged 44, of pulmonary edema (fluid in the lungs) and heart failure caused by cirrhosis (scarring of the liver). She had 70 cents in the bank.
- John H. Halpern and David Blistein, *TIME* (Aug. 12, 2019, opened): "Anslinger ordered agents from his Federal Bureau of Narcotics to storm into her room and arrest her for drug possession. They handcuffed her and put her under a police guard until a judge ordered the cuffs removed. ... Billie Holiday died in that locked hospital room on July 17, 1959, at age 44."
- Rachel Chang, Biography.com (Feb. 26, 2021, opened): her methadone was cut off after 10 days "on Anslinger's instruction" (relying on Hari). Sentence length: "a year" (Wikipedia elsewhere: a year and a day; release date not opened).
- Harry J. Anslinger (from `drugs-alcohol` bank): commissioner of the Federal Bureau of Narcotics, August 12, 1930, to August 17, 1962.
- Disputed: whether the bureau warned her in 1939 never to sing "Strange Fruit" again (Hari; Biography.com; Porter disputes). State as Hari's claim, disputed.

### Marian Anderson (story)
- Wikipedia, "Marian Anderson": February 27, 1897, Philadelphia, to April 8, 1993, Portland, Oregon. Turned away by the Philadelphia Music Academy, which had "an all white policy." In Salzburg in 1935 Arturo Toscanini told her she had "a voice heard once in a hundred years." The DAR, under its president general Sarah Corbin Robert (Mrs. Henry M. Robert Jr.), refused Constitution Hall under a "white performers-only policy." April 9, 1939: "a crowd of more than 75,000 in addition to a national radio audience of millions"; she opened with "My Country, 'Tis of Thee." January 7, 1955: "the first Black American singer to perform at the Metropolitan Opera," as Ulrica in Verdi's *Un ballo in maschera* (she was 57). Sang at the 1963 March on Washington; Presidential Medal of Freedom 1963.
- FDR Library, "Eleanor Roosevelt and Marian Anderson" (opened): in January 1939 Howard University asked for Constitution Hall for her Easter concert; the DAR refused, keeping its whites-only rule. Eleanor Roosevelt resigned from the DAR on February 26, 1939, writing that the DAR had "set an example which seems to me unfortunate" and had "an opportunity to lead in an enlightened way" and had "failed." Interior Secretary Harold Ickes announced the Lincoln Memorial concert on March 30 and introduced her: "Genius knows no color line" (FDR Library). The `rights-movements` bank quotes it as "Genius draws no color line"; wording differs by source.
- National Archives, *Prologue* blog, Jessie Kratz (Feb. 28, 2022, opened): the DAR said publicly the hall was unavailable "due to booking issues." The D.C. school superintendent Frank Ballou refused her the auditorium of Central High School, a white school, and the school board upheld him, though he had let her sing at Armstrong High School, a Black school. National Archives exhibit "American Originals": the DAR rule barring Black performers dated from 1932; Eleanor Roosevelt's telegram of February 27, 1939: "I regret exceedingly that Washington is to be deprived of hearing Marian Anderson, a great artist."

## Era 09: 1950 to 2000 (T-268c, 2026-09-28)

### Rock and roll: the Black musicians
- Wikipedia, "Sister Rosetta Tharpe": born Rosetta Nubin, March 20, 1915, Cotton Plant, Arkansas; played electric guitar in gospel from the late 1930s. "Strange Things Happening Every Day" (1944) was the first gospel record to cross to the R&B chart (No. 2); some scholars call it "the first rock and roll record." Influenced Elvis Presley, Little Richard, Chuck Berry and Johnny Cash. Her 1951 wedding at Griffith Stadium, Washington, drew 25,000 paying guests. Died 1973; unmarked grave until 2008; Rock and Roll Hall of Fame 2018 ("Early Influence").
- Wikipedia, "Fats Domino": February 26, 1928, New Orleans, to October 24, 2017. "The Fat Man" (1949, Imperial, with Dave Bartholomew) sold a million by 1951. More than 65 million records (some say 110 million). Black and white fans at his shows in the segregated 1950s; four riots at his concerts, one in Fayetteville, North Carolina, in November 1956 broken up with tear gas.
- Wikipedia, "Chuck Berry": October 18, 1926, St. Louis, to March 18, 2017. "Maybellene" recorded May 21, 1955, for Chess; over a million sold; No. 1 R&B. When he saw the record, two other men, including the disc jockey Alan Freed, were listed as co-writers; he later won back full credit. Arrested December 1959 under the Mann Act (a law against taking a woman or girl across state lines "for immoral purposes") over a 14-year-old Apache waitress, Janice Escalante; first conviction thrown out because the judge's remarks were racist; second trial, three years; served February 1962 to October 1963. First class of the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame, 1986.
- Wikipedia, "Little Richard": Richard Penniman, December 5, 1932, Macon, Georgia, to May 9, 2020. He said Specialty Records cut his royalty rate without his knowledge. In 1984 he sued Specialty, its owner Art Rupe, and two publishers for $112 million for unpaid royalties since 1959; settled out of court in 1986. His shows let Black and white fans dance together where halls seated them apart. 1995: "I've been gay all my life."
- Wikipedia, "Tutti Frutti (song)": recorded September 14, 1955, New Orleans. Dorothy LaBostrie rewrote his original sexual words. Little Richard's record reached No. 2 R&B. Wikipedia gives its pop peak as No. 13 in one line and says Pat Boone's cover "made No. 12 on the national pop chart, with Little Richard's trailing behind reaching only No. 21 in February 1956" in another. State only that Boone's version ranked higher on the national pop chart.
- Wikipedia, "Ain't That a Shame": Fats Domino's 1955 record: No. 1 R&B, No. 10 pop, eventually a million sold. Pat Boone's cover: his first No. 1 (jukebox chart, two weeks). Boone reportedly wanted to sing "Isn't That a Shame" ("according to some sources"). Domino and Bartholomew, as writers, got royalties from Boone's cover.
- Wikipedia, "LaVern Baker": "Tweedle Dee" (1955), No. 4 R&B, No. 14 pop. Georgia Gibbs, a white singer, made "a note-for-note cover," which reached No. 1. Baker tried to sue her for $250,000 and "petitioned Congress to consider such covers copyright violations"; Rep. Charles Diggs led a congressional inquiry into "song theft." No law resulted (the page gives no outcome).
- Wikipedia, "Cover version": the singer Don McLean called the cover version "a racist tool."

### "Hound Dog": Big Mama Thornton and Elvis Presley
- Wikipedia, "Hound Dog (song)": written by Jerry Leiber and Mike Stoller. Willie Mae "Big Mama" Thornton recorded it August 13, 1952; Don Robey's Peacock Records released it in late February 1953. Robey estimated 500,000 to 750,000 sold; 14 weeks on the R&B chart, seven at No. 1. Thornton: "That song sold over two million records. I got one check for $500 and never saw another." Also: "Didn't get no money from them at all." Robey put his own name (as "Murphy L. Robey") on the copyright application as writer. Presley recorded it July 2, 1956, for RCA; about 10 million sold worldwide; No. 1 pop for 11 weeks. His version followed Freddie Bell and the Bellboys' reworked version, which he heard in Las Vegas in 1956. Leiber and Stoller earned large royalties only after Presley's record.
- Wikipedia, "Big Mama Thornton": December 11, 1926, Ariton, Alabama, to July 25, 1984, Los Angeles, of heart and liver disease. "Everybody livin' in a house but me." Wrote "Ball and Chain" (1961); missed royalties at first when Janis Joplin recorded it because she did not hold the copyright; in 1972 said she was "still drawing royalties." Wore men's suits. Died poor in a boarding house; buried in a grave shared with two others at Inglewood Park Cemetery.

### Elvis Presley (story)
- Wikipedia, "Elvis Presley": January 8, 1935, Tupelo, Mississippi; family moved to Memphis in November 1948. First musical influence: the Assembly of God church; he also heard Black blues and rhythm and blues musicians and radio. At Sam Phillips's Sun studio on the night of July 5, 1954, he sang Arthur Crudup's blues "That's All Right," and Phillips began taping. On November 21, 1955, Colonel Tom Parker and Phillips sold his Sun contract to RCA Victor for $40,000. Drafted 1958, Army until 1960. Died August 16, 1977, at Graceland, aged 42. About 500 million records sold worldwide (estimate).
- Encyclopedia.com, "Sam Phillips": Marion Keisker, Sun's office manager, reported that Phillips said many times: "If I could find a white man who had the Negro sound and the Negro feel, I could make a billion dollars." Phillips opened the Memphis Recording Service at 706 Union Avenue in 1950 and Sun Records in 1952; he recorded Howlin' Wolf and B. B. King. RCA paid $35,000 plus $5,000 in back royalties.
- Wikipedia, "Arthur Crudup": August 24, 1905, Forest, Mississippi, to March 28, 1974. Recorded "That's All Right" in 1946 for RCA's Bluebird label under Lester Melrose. "I realised I was making everybody rich, and here I was poor." In 1968 the promoter Dick Waterman arranged a $60,000 settlement, but the publisher Hill and Range refused to sign at the last moment. In 1971 a songwriters' group got him more than $10,000 in back royalties. 1970: "I was born poor, I live poor, and I am going to die poor."
- Wikipedia, "Colonel Tom Parker": Parker took 25 percent; on January 2, 1967, Presley agreed to raise Parker's share to 50 percent on some income. In 1973 Parker sold RCA the rights to all Presley's past recordings for $5.4 million, so after Presley's death his estate got no royalties on records made before 1973. In 1981 the court-appointed lawyer Blanchard Tual called the 50 percent deal "extortionate compared to the industry average of 15-20%" and the 1973 sale "unethical and poorly handled."
- Movie: *Elvis* (2022), Warner Bros. drama by Baz Luhrmann, Austin Butler as Presley, Tom Hanks as Parker, released June 24, 2022 (Wikipedia; search results).

### Payola, 1959-1960
- Wikipedia, "Payola": "the illegal practice of paying a commercial radio station to play a song without the station disclosing the payment." The House Subcommittee on Legislative Oversight, led by Rep. Oren Harris, investigated in 1959-1960. Alan Freed would not cooperate and was fired; he pleaded guilty to commercial bribery in 1962 ($300 fine, suspended sentence). Dick Clark testified and was not punished, partly because he had sold his music-business holdings. A 1960 change to the Communications Act made payola a misdemeanor (up to $10,000 and one year in jail). Old-line publishers and ASCAP pushed for the hearings partly out of opposition to rock and roll and the small labels selling it.
- Wikipedia, "Alan Freed": popularized the name "rock and roll" on WJW radio, Cleveland, from 1951; the Moondog Coronation Ball, March 21, 1952; played Black musicians' records for Black and white audiences. WABC fired him November 21, 1959, after he refused to sign a statement that he had taken no bribes. 1964: indicted for tax evasion on about $38,000. Died January 20, 1965, at 43. His name on "Maybellene" (Chuck Berry entry above).

### Motown and soul
- Wikipedia, "Motown": Berry Gordy Jr. founded Tamla Records on January 12, 1959, with an $800 loan from a family savings fund. Hitsville U.S.A., 2648 West Grand Boulevard, Detroit, 1959-1968. From 1960 to 1969, 79 Motown songs reached the Billboard top ten. Moved to Los Angeles (announced June 14, 1972). Artists were trained in "grooming, poise, and social graces" for white audiences. Sold in June 1988 to MCA and Boston Ventures for $61 million.
- Black Enterprise, "45 Great Moments in Black Business, No. 28" (opened): in 1973, the magazine's first ranking of Black-owned businesses, "Motown debuted at the No. 1 spot, grossing $40 million in revenues," and held first place "on every list until 1983," when revenues were $91.7 million. This settles the seed's "most successful Black-owned business" claim in the magazine's own terms: the largest Black-owned business by revenue on the *Black Enterprise* list, 1973-1983.
- Wikipedia, "The Funk Brothers": Detroit studio musicians who played on most Motown records from 1959 to 1972; some of them played on each of Motown's 100-plus R&B No. 1s and 50-plus pop No. 1s from 1961 to 1972. Not named on a Motown record until Marvin Gaye's *What's Going On* (1971). Gordy fined players caught recording for other labels. Documentary: *Standing in the Shadows of Motown* (2002).
- Wikipedia, "Sam Cooke": born 1931 (January 22 per other sources, not opened), Clarksdale, Mississippi. Lead singer of the gospel Soul Stirrers from 1950. Founded SAR Records with J. W. Alexander and the publisher Kags Music, "among the first Black artists to control his own business interests"; in 1963 his holding company, Tracey Ltd., owned his RCA master recordings. "A Change Is Gonna Come," December 1964. Shot and killed December 11, 1964, by Bertha Franklin, a Los Angeles motel manager; a coroner's jury ruled it justifiable homicide; his family has questioned the account.
- Wikipedia, "Ray Charles": September 23, 1930, Albany, Georgia; blind by seven, probably from glaucoma; Florida School for the Deaf and the Blind, St. Augustine, 1937-1945. "I Got a Woman" (1954) mixed gospel with blues, and some church people objected. On March 15, 1961, he refused to play a dance in Augusta, Georgia, when he learned the audience would be split by race; the promoter sued; he was fined $757 on June 14, 1962. ABC-Paramount contract of 1959: $50,000 a year advance, higher royalties and "eventual ownership of his master tapes." "Georgia on My Mind" became Georgia's state song in 1979.
- Wikipedia, "James Brown": May 3, 1933, Barnwell, South Carolina, to December 25, 2006. Convicted of robbery at 16. Paid for the recording of *Live at the Apollo* (released June 1963) himself. On April 5, 1968, the day after Martin Luther King Jr. was killed, he gave a free televised concert at the Boston Garden. "Say It Loud, I'm Black and I'm Proud," released August 1968 on King Records, No. 1 R&B, No. 10 pop, written by Brown and Alfred "Pee Wee" Ellis (search summary of Wikipedia song page; unconfirmed: search summary only for chart figures).
- Mahalia Jackson (era 08 entry) and the March on Washington (below).

### Aretha Franklin (story)
- Wikipedia, "Aretha Franklin": March 25, 1942 (born in Memphis) to August 16, 2018, Detroit. Her father, the Rev. C. L. Franklin, led New Bethel Baptist Church in Detroit, where she sang. At 14 she recorded *Songs of Faith* (1956), gospel. She had a child at 12 and a second on August 31, 1957, at 15. Columbia, 1960-1966, with little success. Jerry Wexler brought her to Atlantic in 1966. In January 1967 she recorded one day at FAME Studios in Muscle Shoals, Alabama, before "an altercation broke out." 1970, of the jailed activist Angela Davis: "Angela Davis must go free." *Amazing Grace* (1972) sold more than two million copies. 1987: first woman in the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame.
- Wikipedia, "Respect (song)": Otis Redding wrote and released it in 1965 (No. 35 pop, No. 4 R&B); in his version a man asks for respect when he comes home and offers "all [his] money." Franklin recorded it February 14, 1967, at Atlantic Studios, New York. She and her sister Carolyn changed it so a woman demands respect, spelled out "R-E-S-P-E-C-T" and added "sock it to me." Two weeks at No. 1 pop, eight at No. 1 R&B. It became a feminist and civil rights song. Redding called it the song "that a girl took away from me, a friend of mine."
- Movie: *Amazing Grace* (2018), concert documentary of her January 13-14, 1972, church recording at New Temple Missionary Baptist Church in Watts, Los Angeles, filmed under Sydney Pollack, held back for decades because sound and picture could not be matched, finished by Alan Elliott; premiered November 2018 (Wikipedia; AFI press release, search results).

### Songs of the civil rights movement
- Wikipedia, "We Shall Overcome": from Charles Albert Tindley's hymn "I'll Overcome Some Day" (1901). In the 1945-1946 strike at the cigar factory in Charleston, South Carolina, the tobacco worker Lucille Simmons led strikers in "We Will Overcome." Zilphia Horton of the Highlander Folk School learned it from her and printed it in 1947. Pete Seeger changed "will" to "shall": "'We shall' opens the mouth wider." Guy Carawan taught it to young activists from 1959. Joan Baez led it at the March on Washington (1963). President Lyndon Johnson used the words in his speech of March 15, 1965, after "Bloody Sunday" in Selma. In 2017 a federal court ruled the 1960 copyright invalid in part, and in January 2018 the publisher, the Richmond Organization, settled and gave the song to the public domain.
- SNCC Digital Gateway, "Freedom Singers" (opened): Cordell Reagon formed the SNCC Freedom Singers (Reagon, Bernice Johnson, Rutha Mae Harris, Chuck Neblett, sometimes Bertha Gober). Johnson: "There weren't soloists; there were song leaders." The first tour "raised nearly $50,000 for SNCC." Johnson called them "a singing newspaper." Harris: "we managed to go fifty thousand miles in nine months without any flights."
- SNCC Digital Gateway, "Bernice Johnson Reagon" (opened): born October 4, 1942, Albany, Georgia; NAACP Youth Council; Albany State College suspended her for her activism; at Albany Movement mass meetings she changed the words of spirituals: "So instead I put in freedom and by the second line everyone was singing." "Basically the singing was the 'bed' and the 'air' of everything." Founded Sweet Honey in the Rock (1973); Smithsonian curator; died July 16, 2024.
- Wikipedia, "March on Washington for Jobs and Freedom": August 28, 1963. Mahalia Jackson ("I've Been 'Buked and I've Been Scorned," "How I Got Over"); Marian Anderson ("He's Got the Whole World in His Hands"); Joan Baez ("We Shall Overcome," "Oh Freedom"); Bob Dylan ("When the Ship Comes In" with Baez, "Only a Pawn in Their Game"); Peter, Paul and Mary ("If I Had a Hammer," "Blowin' in the Wind"); Odetta ("I'm On My Way"). During King's speech, "Mahalia Jackson shouted from the crowd, 'Tell them about the dream, Martin!'"

### Bob Dylan (story)
- Wikipedia, "Bob Dylan": born Robert Allen Zimmerman, May 24, 1941, Duluth, Minnesota; grew up in Hibbing. Moved to New York in January 1961 and visited Woody Guthrie in a New Jersey psychiatric hospital. *The Freewheelin' Bob Dylan* (1963). Sang at the March on Washington. Played electric at the Newport Folk Festival in 1965 to "a hostile response from the folk music establishment." Nobel Prize in Literature, 2016, "for having created new poetic expressions within the great American song tradition."
- "Only a Pawn in Their Game" is about the murder of the civil rights leader Medgar Evers in Jackson, Mississippi, June 12, 1963 (song subject: general knowledge; `rights-movements` holds Evers; not in a page opened this session).
- Movie: *No Direction Home: Bob Dylan* (2005), Martin Scorsese documentary, PBS *American Masters*, broadcast September 26-27, 2005 (Wikipedia; PBS).

### Country music, 1950-2000
- Wikipedia, "Hank Williams": September 17, 1923, Mount Olive, Alabama. The Black street musician Rufus "Tee-Tot" Payne taught him guitar "in exchange for money or meals." Grand Ole Opry debut June 11, 1949 (six encores); dismissed August 11, 1952, "for habitual drunkenness and missing shows." Born with spina bifida occulta, a defect of the spine that caused lifelong pain and led to drinking and painkillers. 12 No. 1 country hits. Died January 1, 1953, at 29, in the back seat of a car near Oak Hill, West Virginia.
- Wikipedia, "Patsy Cline": September 8, 1932, Winchester, Virginia. "Walkin' After Midnight" (1957); "Crazy" (1961), by Willie Nelson. Opry member January 1960. Killed in a plane crash near Camden, Tennessee, March 5, 1963, at 30. First woman in the Country Music Hall of Fame (1973).
- Wikipedia, "At Folsom Prison": Johnny Cash recorded two shows at Folsom State Prison, California, on January 13, 1968. "Folsom Prison Blues" (1955). The album closes with "Greystone Chapel," by the prisoner Glen Sherley. Gold (500,000) by October 1968. Cash never served a prison sentence.
- Wikipedia, "The Pill (song)": by Lorene Allen, Don McHan, T. D. Bayless and Loretta Lynn; recorded December 12, 1972, released 1975; about a wife tired of being pregnant who gets birth-control pills; "a number of country radio stations" refused to play it (number not given); No. 5 country, No. 70 pop.
- Wikipedia, "Charley Pride": March 18, 1934, Sledge, Mississippi, one of eleven children of sharecroppers; played in the Negro leagues from 1952; RCA 1965/1966; early singles went to radio stations without his photograph (he disputed that this was deliberate). In 1967 "he became the first Black performer to appear at the Grand Ole Opry since founding member DeFord Bailey, who had last appeared in 1941." 30 country No. 1s; CMA Entertainer of the Year 1971; Country Music Hall of Fame 2000; died December 12, 2020, of COVID-19, at 86.
- Wikipedia, "Bakersfield sound": mid-to-late 1950s, Bakersfield, California, against "the slickly produced, orchestra-laden Nashville sound"; Buck Owens, Merle Haggard, Wynn Stewart; twin Fender Telecasters; audience largely the families of Dust Bowl migrants.

### Hip-hop from the Bronx
- Wikipedia, "DJ Kool Herc": Clive Campbell, born April 16, 1955, Kingston, Jamaica; moved to the Bronx in November 1967, at 13. "On August 11, 1973, DJ Kool Herc was a disc jockey and emcee at a party hosted by himself and his younger sister Cindy at 1520 Sedgwick Avenue." Cindy organized it "to earn extra cash for back-to-school clothes." His "Merry-Go-Round": "He used two copies of the same record to elongate the break," the drum section of a funk record. He named the dancers "b-boys and b-girls." Stopped after being stabbed at the Executive Playhouse while trying to stop a fight; out of DJing by 1980. The city named 1520 Sedgwick Avenue the "birthplace of hip-hop" in 2007. What he earned from hip-hop: not in the page.
- Wikipedia, "Rapper's Delight": released September 16, 1979, by Sugar Hill Records, produced by Sylvia Robinson. No. 36 Hot 100, No. 4 soul. "The first commercial rap song in history" is how it is widely described, though the Fatback Band's "King Tim III" came earlier in 1979. It used Chic's "Good Times"; Nile Rodgers and Bernard Edwards threatened to sue and got writing credit. Big Bank Hank used Grandmaster Caz's rhymes without credit. Sales estimates 2 to 5 million.
- Wikipedia, "The Message": released July 1, 1982, Sugar Hill; written by Ed "Duke Bootee" Fletcher, Melle Mel, Clifton "Jiggs" Chase and Sylvia Robinson; about "the stress of inner-city poverty"; No. 62 Hot 100; in 2002 "the first hip hop recording" added to the National Recording Registry.
- Wikipedia, "1970s South Bronx building fires": "seven census tracts lost more than 97% of their buildings, with 44 tracts losing more than 50% of their buildings, to fire and abandonment"; "250,000 left without housing." "Most fires were the result of arson, usually caused by landlords recruiting Bronx residents to start them," for insurance money (one landlord collected $125,000 from 21 fires, 1970-1975). After a RAND Corporation study, city officials closed 12 fire companies in the South Bronx in 1974-1976 during the city's money crisis. The Bronx had up to 130,000 fires a year by the mid-1970s. `city-building` leads.

### Television, tapes, discs, festivals and the lyrics hearing
- Wikipedia, "MTV": began August 1, 1981, at 12:01 a.m. with "Video Killed the Radio Star." It played few Black artists, saying it kept a "rock format"; Rick James complained; David Bowie questioned it on air with the VJ Mark Goodman in 1983. CBS Records president Walter Yetnikoff threatened to pull CBS's videos unless MTV played Black artists; MTV then played Michael Jackson's "Billie Jean," which went into heavy rotation in March 1983.
- Wikipedia, "Sony CDP-101": compact disc players went on sale in Japan on October 1, 1982, and in the United States and Europe in March 1983. CDs outsold LP records in the US by 1987 or 1988 (sources differ; unconfirmed: search summary only).
- Wikipedia, "Woodstock": August 15-18, 1969, Max Yasgur's dairy farm, Bethel, New York. Organizers expected about 50,000. "More than 460,000" in one line and "an estimated peak of 450,000" in another; the seed's "about 400,000" is the common figure. Who counted is not given. Tickets $18 in advance, $24 at the gate; became free when fences were not finished. Three deaths: two drug overdoses and a 17-year-old run over by a tractor while sleeping. 32 acts.
- Wikipedia, "Parents Music Resource Center": founded May 1985 by Tipper Gore (wife of Sen. Al Gore), Susan Baker (wife of Treasury Secretary James Baker), Pam Howar and Sally Nevius. Their "Filthy Fifteen" included Prince's "Darling Nikki." On November 1, 1985, the record companies' group (RIAA) agreed to put warning labels on some records; the standard black-and-white "Parental Advisory" sticker came in 1990; Walmart and some other stores refused to stock labeled albums.

### Selena Quintanilla (story)
- Wikipedia, "Selena": born April 16, 1971, Lake Jackson, Texas; father Abraham Quintanilla Jr., a Mexican American former musician; sang with her brother and sister in Selena y Los Dinos; learned Spanish lyrics "phonetically," by sound, with her father's help. Tejano Music Award for Female Vocalist of the Year in 1987 and nine years after. Grammy for Best Mexican/American Album (*Live!*, 1994 ceremony). *Dreaming of You* (1995) made her "the first Latin artist" to debut at No. 1 on the Billboard 200. Texas governor George W. Bush declared April 16 Selena Day. The seed's "best-selling Latin artist of the 1990s" is not in the page: cut.
- Wikipedia, "Murder of Selena": March 31, 1995, Days Inn, Corpus Christi, Texas. She reached the lobby and named Saldívar and the room number. Died at Corpus Christi Memorial Hospital at 1:05 p.m., at 23, of blood loss and cardiac arrest. Saldívar surrendered after more than nine hours. Motive: Selena meant to fire her after finding she had embezzled (secretly taken) money: "more than $60,000" (this page) or "more than $30,000 via forged checks" (Wikipedia "Selena"). Life in prison with parole possible after 30 years. 30,000 to 40,000 people viewed her casket.

### Native music, 1950-2000
- From the `religion` bank: Public Law 95-341, the American Indian Religious Freedom Act, approved August 11, 1978. Congress found that "traditional American Indian ceremonies have been intruded upon, interfered with, and in a few instances banned," and declared a policy to protect "the freedom to worship through ceremonials and traditional rites." The 1883 rules on dances (era 07) could be enforced until then.

### Music Modernization Act, 2018
- Wikipedia, "Music Modernization Act" (Pub. L. 115-264): three bills in one. The House passed it April 25, 2018. The Senate passed it unanimously Sept. 19, 2018. President Donald Trump signed it Oct. 11, 2018.
- Title I: set up a nonprofit (the Mechanical Licensing Collective) with a database of who owns each song, so streaming services pay songwriters through one blanket license. Title II (the CLASSICS Act): recordings made before Feb. 15, 1972, had no federal copyright, so digital radio did not have to pay for them. The act brought them under federal protection, so their performers get paid for digital radio play. Title III (AMP Act): producers and engineers can be paid through SoundExchange.
- In 2021 the Mechanical Licensing Collective began paying out "unmatched" royalties: money for songs whose owners had not been identified.
- On Dec. 31, 2017, Wixen Music Publishing (for Tom Petty, Neil Young and others) sued Spotify for $1.6 billion in unpaid songwriter royalties, settled by December 2018 for an undisclosed sum.
- Connection to eras 8-9: many of the Black rhythm-and-blues singers of the 1950s and 1960s (see era 09) recorded before 1972. Only from 2018 did federal law require digital services to pay for those recordings. Do not claim any named singer was paid under it unless a source says so.
