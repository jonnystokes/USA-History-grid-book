## Parked from Ch2 Immigration research (2026-07-23) — immigration & naturalization law as policy
The *arrival* side of these laws is written up in `research/research-immigration.md`. Here they are parked as **law/policy** for Ch14. All dates below are source-checked in the Ch2 file (National Archives, LOC/Constitution Annotated, USCIS, Britannica, History.com, Tenement Museum, USHMM, Truman/LBJ libraries).

### 5. 1750 to 1800
- **Naturalization Act of 1790** — first federal law defining who could become a citizen: a "free white person" of good character, two years' U.S. residence (one in the state of application), oath to the Constitution. First federal word on belonging; the racial limit lasted (in part) until 1952.

### 6. 1800 to 1850
- **Act Prohibiting Importation of Slaves** — signed March 2, 1807 by Jefferson; took effect January 1, 1808 (earliest the Constitution's Article I, Section 9 allowed). Ended legal transatlantic importation.

### 7. 1850 to 1900
- **Page Act of 1875** — first restrictive federal immigration law (barred "coolie" contract labor and, in practice, most Chinese women).
- **Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882** — signed May 6, 1882 by President Chester A. Arthur; barred Chinese laborers for 10 years; first U.S. law to bar a group by nationality/ethnicity; renewed and widened; repealed 1943.
- Federal takeover of immigration processing (Ellis Island opens 1892) after New York State ran Castle Garden (1855–1890).

### 8. 1900 to 1950
- **Emergency Quota Act of 1921** — first numerical caps; 3% of each nationality's foreign-born in the 1910 census.
- **Immigration Act of 1924 (Johnson-Reed / National Origins Act)** — 2% of each nationality's foreign-born in the 1890 census; cut Southern/Eastern Europeans, barred nearly all Asians; total cap ~165,000.
- **Displaced Persons Act of 1948** — first U.S. refugee law; authorized 200,000 displaced Europeans (later raised); ~350,000–400,000 admitted 1948–1952.

### 9. 1950 to 2000
- **Immigration and Nationality Act of 1952 (McCarran-Walter)** — removed the racial bar on naturalization (ending the "free white persons" limit from 1790). [Verify specifics when Ch14 is worked; noted in passing via the 1790 source.]
- **Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965 (Hart-Celler)** — abolished national-origins quotas; family + skills basis; first numerical cap on Western Hemisphere immigration.
- **Refugee Act of 1980** — permanent, standardized refugee-admission system; defined "refugee" on UN lines.
- **Immigration Reform and Control Act of 1986 (IRCA)** — signed Nov 6, 1986; legalized ~2.7 million long-term unauthorized residents; created employer sanctions.

### 10. 2000 to Today
- **Homeland Security Act of 2002** — created the Department of Homeland Security (began operating 2003); the old INS was split into USCIS, ICE, and CBP.
- **DACA (Deferred Action for Childhood Arrivals)** — DHS policy, June 15, 2012; forbearance from removal + work authorization for certain people brought as children; repeatedly litigated.

### 8. 1900 to 1950
- **June 30, 1906 — Theodore Roosevelt signed BOTH the Pure Food and Drug Act and the Meat Inspection Act the same day.** Pure Food and Drug Act: banned misbranded/adulterated food, drink, and drugs in interstate commerce; enforcement under Harvey Wiley's Bureau of Chemistry → renamed the **Food and Drug Administration (1930)**. (Sources: FDA history office "Part I: The 1906 Food and Drugs Act"; USDA FSIS "Our History"; Britannica.)

## Era 3 — the 1600s

### The first General Assembly of Virginia, 30 July to 4 August 1619

- Governor **Sir George Yeardley** summoned burgesses from Virginia's **eleven settlements** in the
  summer of 1619. **Twenty-two** elected representatives sat, two from each settlement, together
  with the governor and his Council.
  (https://encyclopediavirginia.org/entries/house-of-burgesses/)
- The authority to call it came from the **"Great Charter" issued in 1618 by the Virginia Company of
  London**, drafted by **Sir Thomas Smythe and Sir Edwin Sandys**, which authorised the governor to
  "summon a General Assembly to legislate as appropriate." (same source)
- It met **30 July to 4 August 1619**, in the church at Jamestown — the only building on the island
  big enough to hold the governor, six councillors and twenty-two burgesses.
  (https://encyclopediavirginia.org/entries/house-of-burgesses/ ;
  https://history.house.virginia.gov/members/2376)
- What it did in six days: set parliamentary procedure, protected colonists' land titles, made the
  Church of England the established church of the colony, and passed laws on tobacco, dealings with
  Native nations, gambling and conduct.
  (https://encyclopediavirginia.org/entries/house-of-burgesses/)
- **Who could vote is not on the record for 1619.** The assembly did not limit the vote for burgesses
  to adult men who owned land until **1670**. Do not write 1619 as if the franchise were settled.
  (https://encyclopediavirginia.org/entries/house-of-burgesses/)
- The Crown **revoked the Virginia Company's charter in 1624**; Virginia became a royal colony in
  **1625**. The assembly kept meeting with no royal authorisation, and its legal standing stayed
  unclear until it got **de facto recognition in 1627**.
  (https://encyclopediavirginia.org/entries/house-of-burgesses/)

### John Pory — the man who wrote the record (story: slug `john-pory`)

- Born about **1572** at **Thompson, Norfolk, England**. Gonville and Caius College, Cambridge:
  **B.A. 1592**, **M.A. 1595**. Oxford gave him an honorary M.A. on **18 April 1610**.
  (https://history.house.virginia.gov/members/2376)
- Appointed **secretary of the colony of Virginia** by the Virginia Company of London on **2 December

  (https://history.house.virginia.gov/members/2376)
- Yeardley appointed him **Speaker** of the 1619 General Assembly. **He was not an elected burgess.**
  He presided as an appointed officer alongside Yeardley, and he had sat in the English Parliament
  before, so he knew the procedure. (https://history.house.virginia.gov/members/2376)
- He wrote **"The Proceedings of the First General Assembly, July 30, 1619."** That report is the
  record of the meeting. (https://history.house.virginia.gov/members/2376 ;
  https://www.jyfmuseums.org/Home/Components/News/News/70/148 — page returned HTTP 403 to this
  agent; cited only as the location of the Jamestown-Yorktown Foundation's transcription, not relied
  on for any fact above)
- He left Virginia in **early summer 1622** aboard the *Discovery*, after Christopher Davidson was
  appointed secretary in his place. Died **1636**, buried at **Sutton Saint Edmunds, Lincolnshire**.
  (https://history.house.virginia.gov/members/2376)
- **Date disagreement to keep:** the Virginia House of Delegates' own historical database gives

### The Massachusetts school laws of 1642 and 1647 — [VERIFY] cleared

- **1642.** Required parents and masters to "indeavour to teach by themselves or others, their
  children & apprentices so much learning as may enable them perfectly to read the English tongue, &
  knowledge of the Capital Lawes," and to "breed & bring up their children & apprentices in some
  honest lawful calling, labour or employment." Enforcement was a fine for smaller failures and, if
  the selectmen judged it necessary, **removing the children or apprentices to another master**.
  This law does not build a school; it puts the duty on the household.
  (https://www.paulreverehouse.org/that-old-deluder-satan-puritan-emphasis-on-compulsory-education/)
- **1647 — the "Old Deluder Satan" act.** Printed in *Records of the Governor and Company of the
  Massachusetts Bay in New England* (1853 edition), **volume II, page 203**. Preamble: "It being one
  chief project of that old deluder, Satan, to keep men from the knowledge of the Scriptures."
  Every township, once "the Lord hath increased them to fifty households," shall "forthwith appoint
  one within their town to teach all such children as shall resort to him to write and read," paid
  by the parents or masters or by the town. A town of **one hundred families** shall "set up a
  grammar school" fitting boys for the university.
  **The penalty, which is the [VERIFY] item:** "if any town neglect the performance hereof above one
  year that every such town shall pay 5 pounds to the next school till they shall perform this
  order." **The fine falls on the town, not the parent, and it is paid to the nearest school.**
  (https://web.csulb.edu/colleges/cla/projects/EM/smdeluder.html ;
  https://constitution.org/1-History/primarysources/deluder.html — both print the same text with the
  same Records II:203 citation)

## Era 4 — 1700 to 1750

### Crown v. John Peter Zenger — [VERIFY] cleared, and one inherited claim corrected

**The outline this agent inherited said Zenger's trial "established that truth is a defense in a
libel case." That is wrong and it has to be corrected in the outline.** The Historical Society of
the New York Courts states the case "did not establish legal precedent in seditious libel or freedom
of the press." (https://history.nycourts.gov/case/crown-v-zenger/) The UMKC Famous Trials account
says the trial "established no new law with respect to seditious libel."
(https://www.famous-trials.com/zenger/87-home) Truth did not become a defence to libel in New York
until the legislature said so on **6 April 1805** (L. 1805, ch. 90), which allowed truth "where
published with good motive and for justifiable ends," seventy years after the verdict; that rule
went into New York's second constitution in **1821**.
(https://history.nycourts.gov/case/people-v-croswell/)

Facts, each sourced:

- **1725** — the *New York Gazette* founded; it printed the governor's side.
  (https://history.nycourts.gov/case/crown-v-zenger/)
- **1733** — in *Cosby v. Van Dam*, Chief Judge **Lewis Morris** dissented against Governor **William
  Cosby**. Cosby removed Morris from office. Morris's allies then started the *New-York Weekly
  Journal*, the province's first independent newspaper, with Zenger as its printer.
  (https://history.nycourts.gov/case/crown-v-zenger/)
- **5 November 1733** — the *New-York Weekly Journal* began.
  (https://www.famous-trials.com/zenger/87-home)
- **January 1734** — a grand jury refused to indict Zenger. **October 1734** — a second grand jury
  refused. Cosby had copies of the paper burned by the common hangman.
  (https://www.famous-trials.com/zenger/87-home)
- **17 November 1734** — Zenger arrested. Bail set at **£400**, more than he had. He stayed in jail
  until the trial. (https://history.nycourts.gov/case/crown-v-zenger/)
- **16 April 1735** — his lawyers **James Alexander** and **William Smith** challenged whether the
  court was lawfully constituted at his arraignment. Chief Justice **James De Lancey** struck their
  names off the roll of attorneys. **Zenger lost both his lawyers before trial.**
  (https://history.nycourts.gov/case/crown-v-zenger/)
- **4 August 1735** — trial, second floor of City Hall, New York. **Andrew Hamilton** of Philadelphia
  appeared for Zenger without a fee, with **John Chambers**.
  (https://history.nycourts.gov/case/crown-v-zenger/ ;
  https://constitutioncenter.org/blog/a-huge-free-press-victory-by-the-original-philadelphia-lawyer)
- De Lancey refused to let Hamilton put on evidence that what Zenger printed was true: **"The law is
  clear that you cannot justify a libel."** (https://www.famous-trials.com/zenger/87-home)
- Hamilton then argued to the jury instead: **"I know that they have the right beyond all dispute to
  determine both the law and the fact."** (https://www.famous-trials.com/zenger/87-home) And: "The
  laws of our country have given us a right to liberty of both exposing and opposing arbitrary power
  (in these parts of the world at least) by speaking and writing truth." And: "The question before
  the Court and you, Gentlemen of the jury, is not of small or private concern... It is the best
  cause. It is the cause of liberty."
  (https://constitutioncenter.org/blog/a-huge-free-press-victory-by-the-original-philadelphia-lawyer ;
  the "cause of liberty" line also at https://history.nycourts.gov/case/crown-v-zenger/)
- Verdict: **not guilty**. Zenger was let out of jail the day after.
  (https://history.nycourts.gov/case/crown-v-zenger/)
- **Source disagreement on the verdict date.** The Historical Society of the New York Courts and
  UMKC put the trial and verdict on **4 August 1735**. Encyclopedia.com's account has the closing
  arguments finishing **5 August 1735** with a ten-minute deliberation. The 4 August date is the one
  carried by the court system's own historical society; write 4 August 1735 and do not treat the
  ten-minute figure as established.

### Andrew Hamilton — [VERIFY] cleared, promoted from candidate to verified

- A lawyer from Philadelphia. He took Zenger's defence **for free**, with John Chambers.
  (https://constitutioncenter.org/blog/a-huge-free-press-victory-by-the-original-philadelphia-lawyer)
- He was elected **Speaker of the Pennsylvania Assembly**, and he oversaw the planning of the
  Pennsylvania State House — the building now called **Independence Hall** — and pushed to get it
  finished. The National Park Service's Independence Hall material credits him with both, and notes
  he "had won renown for his successful 1735 defense of Peter Zenger."
  (https://www.nps.gov/inde/learn/historyculture/places-independencehall.htm ;
  https://www.ushistory.org/tour/independence-hall.htm)
- The Zenger case "brought the term 'Philadelphia Lawyer' into the American lexicon."
  (https://constitutioncenter.org/blog/a-huge-free-press-victory-by-the-original-philadelphia-lawyer)
- **Not settled by any source this agent could reach:** his birth year and his death date. Do not put
  a birth or death year in the book for him.

### What the era teaches, plainly

A colonial jury refused to convict a printer the governor wanted convicted, and the judge could not
make them. That is jury power, not press law. The law it was decided under stayed the same for
another seventy years. `news-communication` tells the newspaper; `immigration` tells Zenger's
arrival; **this chapter owns the courtroom and the law.**

## Era 5 — 1750 to 1800

### James Madison — inherited `verified` tag, now actually sourced

- Born **16 March 1751**; died **28 June 1836**. (Encyclopedia Virginia entry "James Madison
  (1751-1836)", https://encyclopediavirginia.org/entries/madison-james-1751-1836/)
- **8 June 1789** — Madison stood up in the House of Representatives and introduced his list of
  amendments. He had been a leading voice *against* a bill of rights during ratification, and then
  drove this through. (https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs/bill-of-rights/how-did-it-happen ;
  https://constitutioncenter.org/blog/on-this-day-james-madison-introduces-the-bill-of-rights)
- From that speech: **"I think we should obtain the confidence of our fellow citizens, in proportion
  as we fortify the rights of the people against the encroachments of the government."**
  (https://constitutioncenter.org/the-constitution/historic-document-library/detail/james-madison-speech-in-support-of-amendments-june-8-1789)
- He wanted the changes **woven into the text of the Constitution itself**, not added at the end. In
  August 1789 the House took Roger Sherman's view instead and voted to add them as a supplement.
  (https://constitutioncenter.org/blog/on-this-day-james-madison-introduces-the-bill-of-rights)
- The House passed a joint resolution with **17** amendments; the Senate cut and merged them to
  **12**; a conference committee settled the differences in September 1789. Congress proposed the
  **12 amendments on 25 September 1789**. Washington sent them to the states on **2 October 1789**.
  **Ten** were ratified by three-quarters of the states on **15 December 1791**.
  (https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs/bill-of-rights ;
  https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs/bill-of-rights/how-did-it-happen)
- **Madison enslaved people, and this goes in the book, not softened.** About **100 enslaved
  people** worked at Montpelier at any one time; **nearly 300** in all across the life of the
  plantation. **He freed none of them in his will.** He asked Dolley Madison not to sell them,
  except for misbehaviour, without their consent; she sold some and kept others enslaved. Under
  financial pressure he sold **sixteen** enslaved people to his cousin **William Taylor**, who owned
  a sugar plantation and a cotton plantation in Louisiana. The source gives no date for that sale.
  (https://encyclopediavirginia.org/entries/madison-james-and-slavery/ ;
  https://www.montpelier.org/learn/slavery-constitution-lasting-legacy/)
- **The three-fifths clause.** Madison argued for representation by population at the Convention, and
  he backed the formula that counted an enslaved person as **three-fifths of a free person**. That
  formula went into the Constitution. In *Federalist* No. 54 he defended it on the ground that
  enslaved people had a "mixed status as property and persons."
  (https://encyclopediavirginia.org/entries/madison-james-and-slavery/)
- He attacked slavery repeatedly as against republican principles and never put forward a workable
  plan to end it. (https://encyclopediavirginia.org/entries/madison-james-and-slavery/)

### George Washington — inherited `verified` tag, now actually sourced

- **6 April 1789** — the electoral ballots were counted, over a month late because a cold, snowy
  winter kept members of the First Congress from reaching New York. Washington had **69 electoral
  votes**, every one cast. He is the only person ever elected unanimously by the Electoral College,
  and it happened twice, in 1789 and 1792.
  (https://history.house.gov/Historical-Highlights/1700s/The-first-inauguration-at-Federal-Hall-in-New-York-City/ ;
  https://www.archives.gov/legislative/features/gw-inauguration)
- **30 April 1789** — sworn in on the second-floor balcony of **Federal Hall**, New York, by **Robert
  R. Livingston**, Chancellor of New York, in front of a crowd in the street. He then read the first
  inaugural address to both houses in the Senate Chamber.
  (https://www.archives.gov/legislative/features/gw-inauguration ;
  https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/george-washington-inauguration.htm)
- **Farewell Address.** He never delivered it out loud. It was printed on **19 September 1796**,
  first in a Philadelphia newspaper. The Senate Historical Office edition names it the
  *Philadelphia Daily American Advertiser*; the National Archives Founders Online editorial note
  names it *Claypoole's American Daily Advertiser*. **Same paper, two forms of the name — write
  "a Philadelphia newspaper" or give both.** It is signed "G. Washington / 19th September 1796"
  though the text carries the date of the 17th.
  (https://www.senate.gov/artandhistory/history/resources/pdf/Washingtons_Farewell_Address.pdf ;
  https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Washington/05-20-02-0440-0002)
- **Alexander Hamilton and James Madison both helped write it.** Madison drafted a version in
  1792, when Washington first wanted to leave; Hamilton did the main drafting in July 1796 and
  Washington revised it. (Senate Historical Office introduction, PDF above.)
- The sentence that made the two-term rule: he told the country of **"the resolution I have formed,
  to decline being considered among the number of those out of whom a choice is to be made."**
  (Senate PDF.)
- On parties: **"Let me now take a more comprehensive view and warn you in the most solemn manner
  against the baneful effects of the spirit of party, generally."** He said that spirit "is
  inseparable from our nature," that in popular governments "it is seen in its greatest rankness and
  is truly their worst enemy," and that one faction dominating another is "itself a frightful
  despotism." (Senate PDF.)
- On foreign alliances: **"It is our true policy to steer clear of permanent alliances with any
  portion of the foreign world."** He added that existing promises must be kept, and that the
  country "may safely trust to temporary alliances for extraordinary emergencies." (Senate PDF.)
  **`america-world` owns the foreign-policy consequences; this chapter uses this only as part of the
  address.**
- **The Senate reads it aloud every year.** The practice began **22 February 1862**, after a
  thousand Philadelphians petitioned Congress in January 1862 to mark Washington's 130th birthday
  while the Civil War was on. Since **1896** a senator has read the **7,641-word** text each year,
  alternating parties, and signs a leather-bound book afterwards; the first signature is **Joseph
  Foraker of Ohio, 22 February 1900**.
  (https://www.senate.gov/about/traditions-symbols/washingtons-farewell-address.htm ; Senate PDF.)
- **The two-term rule stayed a habit, not a law, for 150 years.** Franklin Roosevelt broke it in
  **1940** and again in **1944**. The House proposed **House Joint Resolution 27 in 1947**; the
  **22nd Amendment** became part of the Constitution on **27 February 1951**, when **Minnesota**
  became the 36th state to ratify.
  (https://constitution.congress.gov/browse/essay/amdt22-1/ALDE_00001008/ ;
  https://reagan.blogs.archives.gov/2022/10/11/constitutional-amendments-series-amendment-xxii-term-limits-for-the-presidency/)
- **Washington enslaved people, and this goes in the book.** He owned **123 people** outright and
  managed **153** more who belonged to the estate of Martha Washington's first husband — the "dower"
  enslaved people. He could not legally sell or free the dower people, or their children. His will
  of **9 July 1799** freed his valet **William Lee** at once and directed that the other 122 be freed
  when Martha died. **Martha Washington freed them on 1 January 1801**, about eighteen months before
  her own death. The Custis estate then took the dower people back and divided them among Martha's
  grandchildren, **splitting up families**.
  (https://encyclopediavirginia.org/entries/washington-george-and-slavery/ ;
  https://encyclopediavirginia.org/entries/enslaved-community-at-mount-vernon/ ;
  https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Washington/06-04-02-0404-0001)

### The school-tax fight — [VERIFY] cleared

## Era 7 — 1850 to 1900

### Abraham Lincoln — inherited `verified` tag, now actually sourced (angle: the Constitution in a war)

`war` tells the fighting; `slavery-freedom` tells emancipation. **This chapter takes the
constitutional question: what a president may do to the law during a rebellion.** Sourced from the
Federal Judicial Center's own case study, *Ex parte Merryman and Debates on Civil Liberties During
the Civil War*, downloaded 2026-09-09 from
https://www.fjc.gov/sites/default/files/trials/merryman.pdf

- **25 April 1861** — Lincoln wrote to General **Winfield Scott** endorsing "the most prompt, and
  efficient means," including "the bombardment of their cities — and in the extremest necessity, the
  suspension of the writ of habeas corpus."
- **27 April 1861** — Lincoln explicitly authorised Scott to **suspend habeas corpus anywhere along
  the troop routes between Philadelphia and Washington.** No federal authority had suspended it
  since the last days of the War of 1812.
  **Plain-word definition for the book:** habeas corpus is the right to be brought before a judge
  who decides whether the government may keep holding you. Suspend it and the government can hold
  you without taking you to a judge at all.
- **25 May 1861, 2:00 a.m.** — federal troops entered the country house of **John Merryman**, a
  Baltimore County planter, and took him to **Fort McHenry**, held by order of **General George
  Cadwalader**. The charge was that he was an officer of a "secession company" holding federal arms.
- **26 May** — **Chief Justice Roger Taney**, sitting as a circuit judge for Maryland, issued the
  writ and ordered Cadwalader to bring Merryman to court.
- **27 May** — Cadwalader refused in a letter, saying Lincoln had authorised officers to suspend the
  writ. A deputy marshal took Taney's contempt writ to the gate of Fort McHenry and a guard would
  not let him in.
- **28 May 1861** — a crowd outside the Masonic Hall in Baltimore; Taney, then **84 years old**,
  ruled from the bench that the president had **no constitutional or statutory power** to suspend
  the writ, and that Merryman was entitled to immediate release. **He issued no order to enforce
  it.** Written opinion filed **1 June 1861**.
- **Taney is the same judge who wrote *Dred Scott v. Sandford* in 1857**, holding that Black
  Americans, free or enslaved, had no standing as citizens under the Constitution. That has to be
  said in the same breath; it is why almost nobody at the time read him as neutral.
- **4 July 1861** — Lincoln's message to the special session of Congress. He said the suspension had
  "purposely been exercised but very sparingly," and asked: **"are all the laws, but one, to go
  unexecuted, and the government itself go to pieces, lest that one be violated?"** He argued that
  the Constitution is silent on **who** may suspend the writ in "Cases of Rebellion or Invasion," and
  that the framers cannot have meant "the danger should run its course, until Congress could be
  called together."
- **March 1863** — Congress authorised the president to suspend the writ for the rest of the war.
  The dispute Taney raised was settled by statute, not by a court.
- **31 January 1865** — the House passed the Thirteenth Amendment **119 to 56**. Lincoln signed the
  joint resolution on **1 February 1865**, which he did not have to do. Ratified **6 December 1865**.
  (https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/13th-amendment ;
  https://history.house.gov/HistoricalHighlight/Detail/36815)

### Joseph Hayne Rainey — fills the `reconstruction-black-officeholder` target slot

The slot asked for "a Reconstruction-era Black officeholder." Rainey is named, documented in the
House's own records, and **not used anywhere else in this book** (checked against all 612 story slugs
on 2026-09-09; `revels-bruce` and `robert-smalls` belong to `slavery-freedom`). New slug:
`joseph-rainey`. All facts from
https://history.house.gov/People/Listing/R/RAINEY,-Joseph-Hayne-(R000016)/

- Born **21 June 1832 at Georgetown, South Carolina**; died there **2 August 1887**.
- **His parents were enslaved.** His father **Edward L. Rainey** worked as a barber and bought the
  family's freedom out of his earnings **in the early 1840s**.
- During the Civil War the Confederate army **forced him to work on Charleston's defences**, and he
  worked as a ship's steward on a Confederate blockade runner. In **1862** he and his wife escaped
  to **Bermuda**, where he ran a barbershop and she ran a dress shop, until **1866**.
- **19 October 1870** — elected to a full term with **63 percent**. **8 November 1870** — won a
  special election for the rest of the 41st Congress with **86 percent**.
- **12 December 1870** — sworn in. **First African American to serve in the U.S. House.**
- Committees: Freedmen's Affairs (41st-42nd), Indian Affairs (43rd), Invalid Pensions (44th-45th),
  Select Committee on the Centennial Celebration (44th), Joint Committee on Enrolled Bills (45th).
- **29 April 1874** — first African American to preside over the House from the Speaker's chair.
- **1 April 1871** — spoke for the **Ku Klux Klan Act**, arguing the Constitution owed "protection to
  the humblest citizen, without regard to rank."
- Supported the **Civil Rights Act of 1875**, and pressed for equal access to schooling; he described
  wanting "mixed schools" as a way to "annihilate" prejudice.
- Re-elected unopposed in October 1872; won with **52 percent** in October 1874 and again in November
  1876; **lost in 1878 by 62 percent to 38**. Left the House **3 March 1879**, after **more than
  eight years** — the longest-serving Black member of Congress until the **1950s**.
- Afterwards: Treasury Department special agent in South Carolina 1879-1881; a brokerage and banking
  business in Washington 1881-1886; back to Georgetown, died 1887.
- **First of 14 African American Representatives to serve during Reconstruction.** After

### Cumming v. Richmond County Board of Education — [VERIFY] cleared

- Full style: ***J. W. Cumming, James S. Harper, and John C. Ladeveze v. County Board of Education of
  Richmond County, State of Georgia***, **175 U.S. 528**, decided **18 December 1899**, opinion by
  **Justice John Marshall Harlan**. (https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/175/528)
- **June 1897** — the Richmond County board closed **Ware High School**, the high school for Black
  students, saying it could not afford to run a high school for 60 Black children while 300 Black
  children had no primary schooling. **It kept funding the high schools for white students.**
- The Black taxpayers who sued asked the court to stop the board spending public money on white high
  schools until there was an equal one for their children, and to stop collecting school tax from
  them for schools their children could not attend.
- **The Court refused.** It found no violation of the Fourteenth Amendment, treating the closure as a
  money decision rather than a racial one. Harlan wrote that "if it appeared that the board's refusal
  to maintain such a school was in fact an abuse of its discretion and in hostility to the colored
  population because of their race, different questions might have arisen."
- **The fact worth telling a young reader:** Harlan is the justice who dissented alone in *Plessy v.
  Ferguson* three years earlier. Here he wrote the opinion the other way. **Do not smooth this over.**

## Era 8 — 1900 to 1950

### The 16th and 17th Amendments, 1913 — [VERIFY] cleared

- **16th Amendment.** Passed by Congress **2 July 1909**; **ratified 3 February 1913**. Text: "The
  Congress shall have the power to lay and collect taxes on incomes, from whatever source derived,
  without apportionment among the several States, and without regard to any census or enumeration."
  Background: Congress put a **2 percent tax on incomes over $4,000** into a tariff bill in **1894**
  and the Supreme Court struck it down **5-4**, even though it had upheld the Civil War income tax
  in 1881. (https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/16th-amendment)
  **Watch this date.** The same National Archives page says ratified **3 February 1913** and also
  that it "took effect February 25, 1913" — the second is the day the Secretary of State certified
  it. Both are true; write 3 February 1913 as the ratification date and do not treat the two as a
  contradiction.
  **A detail worth keeping:** the page notes conservatives put the amendment forward believing it
  would never be ratified. It was.
- **17th Amendment.** Passed by Congress **13 May 1912**; **ratified 8 April 1913**, when Connecticut
  became the state that made three-quarters. It changed Article I, Section 3 so that voters, not
  state legislatures, choose senators. **Augustus Bacon of Georgia** was the first senator directly
  elected under it, on **15 July 1913**.
  (https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/17th-amendment ;
  https://www.senate.gov/about/origins-foundations/senate-and-constitution/seventeenth-amendment.htm)

### *Pierce v. Society of Sisters*, 1925 — [VERIFY] cleared

- Full style: ***Pierce, Governor of Oregon, et al. v. Society of the Sisters of the Holy Names of
  Jesus and Mary***, and the companion case against **Hill Military Academy**. **268 U.S. 510**,
  decided **1 June 1925**, opinion by **Justice James Clark McReynolds**.
  (https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/268/510)
- Oregon's **Compulsory Education Act of 1922** required parents and guardians of children **aged 8
  to 16** to send them to the **public** school in their district for the whole school year, with
  narrow exceptions. Breaking it was a misdemeanour.
- The Court struck it down: the law interfered with the liberty of parents to direct their children's
  education, protected by the Fourteenth Amendment.
- The sentence the whole later law rests on: **"The child is not the mere creature of the state;
  those who nurture him and direct his destiny have the right, coupled with the high duty, to
  recognize and prepare him for additional obligations."**
- **Why it matters for this book:** a state may require a child to be educated; it may not require
  every child to be educated in a state school. Every private, parochial and later homeschool
  arrangement stands on this. `education` tells the schools; the ruling as law is this chapter's.

### The 1925 evolution trial — [VERIFY] cleared

- **Butler Act**, Tennessee, signed by **Governor Austin Peay** on **21 March 1925**. It made it
  unlawful to teach "any theory that denies the story of Divine Creation of man as taught in the
  Bible, and to teach instead that man has descended from a lower order of animals."
  (https://tennesseeencyclopedia.net/entries/the-scopes-trial/ ;
  https://www.zinnedproject.org/news/tdih/scopes-trial-ruling/)
- **John T. Scopes** was a football coach and science teacher at the high school in **Dayton,
  Tennessee**. **It was a set-up test case.** Civic leaders in Dayton heard the ACLU would pay for a
  challenge to the law and asked Scopes whether biology could be taught without evolution. He said
  it could not, because the state's own approved textbook covered it, and he remembered going over
  it in an April review class. (https://tennesseeencyclopedia.net/entries/the-scopes-trial/)
- Trial opened **10 July 1925** at Dayton. Judge **John T. Raulston**. Defence: **John Randolph Neal,
  Clarence Darrow, Dudley Field Malone, Arthur Garfield Hays**. Prosecution: Tennessee Attorney
  General **Thomas Stewart**, **S. K. Hicks**, and **William Jennings Bryan**.
- **Verdict 21 July 1925: guilty. Fine $100**, the minimum the act allowed.
- **January 1927** — the **Tennessee Supreme Court reversed the conviction on a technicality**: the
  state constitution required a jury, not a judge, to set any fine over **$50**, and Raulston had
  set this one himself. The defence asked for a rehearing so the law itself could be tested, and was
  refused. **So the Butler Act was never ruled on.**
- **The Butler Act stayed on the books until 1967.**
  (https://tennesseeencyclopedia.net/entries/the-scopes-trial/)
- `religion` and `education` lead. **This chapter owns the statute, the conviction and the appeal.**

### Franklin D. Roosevelt — inherited `verified` tag, now actually sourced (angle: the presidency against the Court)

`economy` leads on the Depression and the New Deal programmes. **This chapter takes the
constitutional fight: what happens when a president loses in court and tries to change the court.**

- ***A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495**, argued 2 and 3 May 1935,
  **decided 27 May 1935**. The Court struck down the part of the **National Industrial Recovery Act**
  that let the president approve "codes of fair competition," holding that Congress had handed the
  executive powers it could not hand over, and that the poultry code was not regulation of
  interstate commerce. (https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/295/495 ;
  https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/schechter_poultry_corp_v_united_states_(1935))
- **5 February 1937** — Roosevelt sent Congress a plan to reorganise the federal courts. It "added
  one justice to the Court for each justice over the age of 70, with a maximum of six additional
  justices," which would have taken the Supreme Court from **nine to fifteen**.

## Era 9 — 1950 to 2000

### [VERIFY] cleared — "all deliberate speed": *Brown v. Board of Education* II, 349 U.S. 294

- Decided **31 May 1955**, Chief Justice **Earl Warren** writing for a unanimous Court. The phrase
  the outline needed is in the order: the district courts were to admit the plaintiffs to public
  schools "on a racially nondiscriminatory basis **with all deliberate speed**."
- The 1954 decision (*Brown* I, 17 May 1954) held segregated schooling unconstitutional but set no
  remedy. *Brown* II is the remedy, and it contains **no date**. It sent the cases back to the
  district courts, told school boards to make "a prompt and reasonable start toward full
  compliance," let the district courts weigh local problems — buildings, transport, staff, school
  district boundaries — and kept those courts in charge during the transition.
- So the enforcement problem is built into the wording. A school board that moved slowly was not
  obviously breaking the order, because the order named no deadline.
  Source: Cornell LII, 349 U.S. 294 — https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/349/294

### [VERIFY] cleared — the school prayer ruling of 1962: *Engel v. Vitale*, 370 U.S. 421

- Argued **3 April 1962**, decided **25 June 1962**, **6-1**, **Justice Hugo Black** writing.
  Frankfurter and White took no part.
- The New York State Board of Regents had written a prayer for schools to say each morning:
  "Almighty God, we acknowledge our dependence upon Thee, and we beg Thy blessings upon us, our
  parents, our teachers and our Country." Saying it was voluntary for any individual child.
- The Court held the prayer unconstitutional anyway. Black: "the constitutional prohibition against
  laws respecting an establishment of religion must at least mean that in this country it is no
  part of the business of government to compose official prayers for any group."
- The point of law for this chapter: it did not matter that the prayer was short, that it named no
  denomination, or that a child could sit it out. What mattered was that a government body wrote it.
  Source: Cornell LII, 370 U.S. 421 — https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/370/421

### [VERIFY] cleared — the Bible-reading ruling of 1963: *Abington School District v. Schempp*, 374 U.S. 203

- Argued **27-28 February 1963**, decided **17 June 1963**, **Justice Tom Clark** writing; **Justice
  Potter Stewart** the sole dissenter.
- Two cases decided together: *Abington School District v. Schempp* from **Pennsylvania**, where the
  school day opened with readings from the Bible and no comment on them, and ***Murray v. Curlett***
  from the **Baltimore City** schools, where children said the Lord's Prayer together.
- Both practices were struck down under the Establishment Clause, applied to the states through the
  Fourteenth Amendment.
  Source: Cornell LII, 374 U.S. 203 — https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/374/203

### [VERIFY] cleared — *Miranda v. Arizona*, 384 U.S. 436

- Argued **28 February and 1-2 March 1966**, decided **13 June 1966**, Chief Justice **Earl Warren**
  writing. Four cases were decided together: *Miranda v. Arizona*, *Vignera v. New York*,
  *Westover v. United States*, and *California v. Stewart*.
- **Ernesto Miranda** was arrested at his home in Phoenix on **13 March 1963** and questioned in a
  police interrogation room. The officers admitted at trial that he was never told he could have a
  lawyer with him. He signed a written confession after about two hours and was convicted of
  kidnapping and rape.
- The Court required that before questioning, a person in custody be told: that he has the right to
  remain silent; that anything he says can be used against him in court; that he has the right to a
  lawyer; and that if he cannot afford one, a lawyer will be appointed. If he asks for those rights,
  the questioning has to stop.
  Source: Cornell LII, 384 U.S. 436 — https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/384/436

### [VERIFY] cleared — *Wisconsin v. Yoder*, 406 U.S. 205

- Argued **8 December 1971**, decided **15 May 1972**, **6-1**, Chief Justice **Warren Burger**
  writing. Powell and Rehnquist took no part.
- The three fathers prosecuted were **Jonas Yoder**, **Wallace Miller** (Old Order Amish) and
  **Adin Yutzy** (Conservative Amish Mennonite). Wisconsin required school attendance to age 16.
  The fine was **$5 each**.
- Their children — **Frieda Yoder, 15**, **Barbara Miller, 15**, and **Vernon Yutzy, 14** — had
  finished eighth grade and were not sent to high school.
- Held: "the First and Fourteenth Amendments prevent the State from compelling respondents to cause
  their children to attend formal high school to age 16." Wisconsin could not show that two more
  years of schooling mattered enough to override a religious practice the Court found to be lived
  daily and centuries old.
  Source: Cornell LII, 406 U.S. 205 — https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/406/205

### [VERIFY] cleared — *San Antonio Independent School District v. Rodriguez*, 411 U.S. 1

- Argued **12 October 1972**, decided **21 March 1973**, **5-4**, **Justice Lewis Powell** writing.
  **Justice Potter Stewart concurred.** A summary this agent fetched first listed Stewart as a
  dissenter; the opinion headers say "MR. JUSTICE STEWART, concurring". The dissenters were
  **Brennan**; **White, joined by Douglas and Brennan**; and **Marshall, joined by Douglas**.
- The comparison in the record, school year **1967-68**, was between two districts in the same city:
  **Edgewood Independent School District**, mostly Mexican American, spending **$356 per pupil**
  ($248 state and local, $108 federal), and **Alamo Heights**, spending **$594 per pupil** ($558
  state and local, $36 federal).
- Held: education is **not a fundamental right** under the Constitution — the Court looked for a
  right to education "explicitly or implicitly guaranteed by the Constitution" and found none — so
  Texas's property-tax school funding only had to be rational, and keeping local control of schools
  counted as a rational purpose.
- Consequence for this chapter: after 1973 the federal courts were closed to school-funding
  equality suits, and the fight moved to **state courts under state constitutions**, which is where
  it has been ever since.
  Source: Cornell LII, 411 U.S. 1 — https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/411/1

### [VERIFY] cleared — *Ingraham v. Wright*, 430 U.S. 651

- Argued **2-3 November 1976**, decided **19 April 1977**, **5-4**, **Justice Lewis Powell** writing;
  **White, Brennan, Marshall and Stevens** dissenting.
- **James Ingraham** and **Roosevelt Andrews** were pupils at **Drew Junior High School, Dade
  County, Florida**, in **October 1970**. School policy allowed one to five "licks" on the buttocks
  with a flat wooden paddle "less than two feet long, three to four inches wide, and about one-half
  inch thick."
- What the record says was done: "Because he was slow to respond to his teacher's instructions,
  Ingraham was subjected to more than 20 licks with a paddle while being held over a table in the
  principal's office." The beating raised a **hematoma** — a pocket of blood under the skin — that
  needed medical attention and kept him out of school for several days. Andrews was paddled several
  times for small infractions; once he was struck on the arms and lost the full use of an arm for a
  week.
- Held, two things. The Eighth Amendment's ban on cruel and unusual punishment "was designed to
  protect those convicted of crimes" and does not reach school discipline at all. And the
  Fourteenth Amendment does not require a school to give a child notice or a hearing before hitting
  him, because he can sue afterwards in the ordinary courts if the beating was excessive.
- Consequence for this chapter: the Constitution left school paddling alone, so it ended, where it
  ended, one state legislature at a time and not by court order.
  Source: Cornell LII, 430 U.S. 651 — https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/430/651

### [VERIFY] cleared — the 1965 school-money act: the Elementary and Secondary Education Act

- **Public Law 89-10**, H.R. 2362, **approved 11 April 1965** — the date printed on the statute
  itself, 79 Stat. 27. **A conflict worth recording:** the National Center for Education Statistics'
  Title I history page says Johnson signed it on **12 April 1965**. The Statutes at Large and the
  LBJ Library both say **11 April**; use 11 April, and note that a federal education agency's own
  page carries the other date.
- Johnson signed it outside the former **Junction Elementary School near Stonewall, Texas**, the
  one-room school he had started in at age four, with his first teacher, **Kate Deadrich Loney**,
  beside him. He said: "I do not wish to delay by a single day the program to strengthen this
  Nation's elementary and secondary schools," that "education is the only valid passport from
  poverty," and that "no law I have signed or will ever sign means more to the future of America."
- What it did, as law: **Title I** sent federal money to school districts according to how many
  children in them came from low-income families. That is the mechanism that matters. Before 1965
  the federal government paid for almost none of American schooling; after 1965 it paid for a slice
  and attached conditions to the slice. Title I is still the largest federal school programme; in
  fiscal year **2015** it distributed about **$14.3 billion** (NCES, published 2019).
  Sources: govinfo, 79 Stat. 27, PUBLIC LAW 89-10 — APR. 11, 1965 —
  https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-79/pdf/STATUTE-79-Pg27.pdf ;
  LBJ Library, remarks on signing, 11 April 1965 —
  https://www.lbjlibrary.org/object/text/remarks-signing-elementary-education-bill-04-11-1965 ;
  NCES, Title I introduction — https://nces.ed.gov/pubs2019/titleI/intro.asp

### [VERIFY] cleared — Minnesota's charter school law, 1991

### [VERIFY] cleared, and the outline's claim corrected — homeschooling, state by state

- The outline said states legalised homeschooling "one at a time through the 1980s, ending in the
  early 1990s," and asked for the last state and year. The usual answer is **Michigan, 1993**. The
  case is real; the shorthand is looser than the record.
- ***People v. DeJonge***, 442 Mich. 266, 501 N.W.2d 127, Docket No. 91479, argued **10 November
  1992**, decided **25 May 1993**. Justice **Dorothy Comstock Riley** wrote: "At issue is the
  constitutionality of MCL 388.553; MSA 15.1923, which requires parents who conduct home schooling
  for their children to provide instructors certified by the state. We hold that the teacher
  certification requirement is an unconstitutional violation of the Free Exercise Clause of the
  First Amendment as applied to families whose religious convictions prohibit the use of certified
  instructors."
- **The same court decided the companion case the same day and those parents lost.** ***People v.
  Bennett***, 442 Mich. 316, 501 N.W.2d 106, Docket No. 91480, decided **25 May 1993**, Justice
  **James Brickley** writing: the question there was "whether, in a challenge not involving
  religious convictions, a teacher certification requirement for home schools violates a parent's
  right to direct a child's education under the Fourteenth Amendment," and the answer was no.
- So on 25 May 1993 Michigan's certified-teacher rule fell for families with a religious objection
  and stood for everyone else. Michigan's legislature then wrote a plain home-education exemption
  into the compulsory attendance statute — **MCL 380.1561(3)(f)** — amended by **1995 Public Act
  289**, effective **1 July 1996**.
- **Say this honestly in the book:** homeschooling became lawful everywhere across the 1980s and
  early 1990s, state by state, by statute and by court ruling. "Michigan was the last state, in
  1993" is the shorthand, and the shorthand hides that the 1993 ruling covered only religious
  objectors and that the statute came three years after it.
  Sources: CourtListener search API, Michigan Supreme Court, 1993 —
  https://www.courtlistener.com/api/rest/v4/search/?q=DeJonge&court=mich&type=o ;
  Michigan Legislature, MCL 380.1561 —
  https://legislature.mi.gov/Laws/MCL?objectName=MCL-380-1561

### Five amendments in twenty years, 1951 to 1971 — all fetched from the National Archives text

The National Archives page carrying Amendments 11-27 gives passage and ratification dates for each.
Source: https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs/amendments-11-27

- **22nd** — passed by Congress **21 March 1947**, ratified **27 February 1951**. "No person shall be
  elected to the office of the President more than twice." Washington's two terms had been a custom;
  Franklin Roosevelt broke it four times; this made it a rule.
- **23rd** — passed **16 June 1960**, ratified **29 March 1961**. It gave the District of Columbia
  presidential electors, "equal to the whole number of Senators and Representatives in Congress" it
  would have if it were a state, and never more than the smallest state has. It gave the District no
  senators and no voting member of the House, and it still has none. **This is the territories-and-
  voting thread: the amendment fixed one part of the problem and left the rest.**
- **24th** — passed **27 August 1962**, ratified **23 January 1964**. It bans a poll tax as a
  condition of voting "in any primary or other election for President or Vice President ... or for
  Senator or Representative in Congress." **Read the words: it only covers federal elections.** A
  state could still charge for its own elections until the Supreme Court struck state poll taxes down
  under the Fourteenth Amendment in 1966.
- **25th** — passed **6 July 1965**, ratified **10 February 1967**. It says plainly what happens when
  a president dies, resigns or is removed, and it created a way to fill a vacant vice presidency: the
  president nominates and both houses of Congress confirm.
- **26th** — passed **23 March 1971**, ratified **1 July 1971**. Eighteen-year-olds may vote. It is
  the fastest ratification of any amendment in American history.

### "One person, one vote" — where the phrase actually comes from

- The sentence is **Justice William O. Douglas's**, in ***Gray v. Sanders***, 372 U.S. 368, decided
  **18 March 1963**, **8-1** (Harlan dissenting): "The conception of political equality from the
  Declaration of Independence, to Lincoln's Gettysburg Address, to the Fifteenth, Seventeenth, and
  Nineteenth Amendments can mean only one thing — one person, one vote." *Gray* struck down Georgia's
  county unit system, which handed all of a county's unit votes to whoever won that county and so
  made a rural vote count for many times an urban one in statewide elections.
  Source: Cornell LII, 372 U.S. 368 — https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/372/368
- ***Reynolds v. Sims***, 377 U.S. 533, argued **13 November 1963**, decided **15 June 1964**, Chief
  Justice **Earl Warren** writing, **Harlan dissenting**, applied it to state legislatures. Alabama
  had not redrawn its legislative districts since **1901**, on the **1900 census**. By 1960, **25.1
  per cent** of Alabamians could elect a majority of the state senate and **25.7 per cent** a
  majority of the state house; the worst population gaps between districts ran about **41 to 1** in
  the senate and **16 to 1** in the house.
  Source: Cornell LII, 377 U.S. 533 — https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/377/533
- Why it belongs to this chapter and not to `rights-movements`: nobody marched for *Reynolds*. It is
  a rule about arithmetic in district lines, and it forced almost every state legislature in the
  country to redraw itself.

### The Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965 — as statutes, and as votes

**`rights-movements` leads on the campaign that forced these. This chapter owns the machinery.**

- **Civil Rights Act of 1964, signed 2 July 1964 by Lyndon Johnson.** Ten titles. Title II bars
  segregation in "any place of public accommodation" — its words are "All persons shall be entitled
  to the full and equal enjoyment of the goods, services, facilities, and privileges ... of any place
  of public accommodation ... without discrimination or segregation." Title VI bars discrimination in
  anything paid for with federal money, which is the hook that later let Washington pull school money
  from segregated districts. Title VII bars job discrimination and created the Equal Employment
  Opportunity Commission.
  Source: National Archives milestone document —
  https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/civil-rights-act
- **How it got through the Senate.** Southern senators led by **Richard Russell of Georgia** held the
  floor for **60 working days, including seven Saturdays**. On **10 June 1964** the Senate voted
  **71-29** for cloture — the motion that cuts off debate — and it was the first time cloture had ever
  been invoked on a civil rights bill. Senator **Clair Engle of California**, who had a brain tumour
  and could not speak, raised a crippled arm and pointed to his eye to vote aye. The Senate passed the
  bill **73-27** on **19 June 1964**.
  Source: U.S. Senate, "Civil Rights Filibuster Ended" —
  https://www.senate.gov/about/powers-procedures/filibusters-cloture/civil-rights-filibuster-ended.htm
- **Voting Rights Act of 1965, signed 6 August 1965.** Section 2 states the rule: "No voting
  qualification or prerequisite to voting, or standard, practice, or procedure shall be imposed or
  applied by any State or political subdivision to deny or abridge the right of any citizen" to vote
  on account of race. **Section 4** contained the coverage formula: a state or county was covered if
  it had used a literacy test or similar device as of **1 November 1964** *and* fewer than **50 per
  cent** of its voting-age residents were registered or had voted in the 1964 presidential election.
  **Section 5** then required every covered place to get permission from the U.S. Attorney General or
  the federal district court in Washington before changing any voting rule at all.
  Source: National Archives milestone document —
  https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/voting-rights-act
- **The formula in Section 4(b) is the part the Supreme Court struck down in 2013** (*Shelby County v.
  Holder*, 25 June 2013). Detail and citation are in this file's parked `rights-movements` era-10
  block. Congress has written no replacement formula as of 9 September 2026.

### The War Powers Resolution, 1973 — Congress against the presidency

- **Public Law 93-148**, H.J. Res. 542, a joint resolution "Concerning the war powers of Congress and
  the President." Nixon vetoed it on **24 October 1973**. On **7 November 1973** the House voted
  **284-135** and the Senate **75-18** to override, and it became law over his veto.
- What it requires, from the statute text: **Section 4(a)** — when the president sends armed forces
  into hostilities without a declaration of war, "the President shall submit within 48 hours to the
  Speaker of the House of Representatives and to the President pro tempore of the Senate a report, in
  writing," setting out the circumstances, the legal authority claimed, and the estimated scope and
  duration. **Section 5(b)** — within **sixty calendar days** of that report the president "shall
  terminate any use of United States Armed Forces" unless Congress has declared war, passed a specific
  authorisation, or extended the sixty days; the sixty days may be stretched by up to thirty more only
  if the president certifies in writing that getting the troops out safely requires it.
  Sources: govinfo, 87 Stat. 555, PUBLIC LAW 93-148 — NOV. 7, 1973 —
  https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-87/pdf/STATUTE-87-Pg555.pdf ;

  congress.gov, H.J.Res.542, 93rd Congress —

### Watergate, told as constitutional machinery

- ***United States v. Nixon***, 418 U.S. 683, argued **8 July 1974**, decided **24 July 1974**,
  **8-0**, Chief Justice **Warren Burger** writing; **Justice William Rehnquist took no part** because
  he had worked in Nixon's Justice Department. The special prosecutor had subpoenaed tape recordings
  of the president's own conversations. The Court accepted that a president's talks are presumptively
  confidential and then held that the privilege is not absolute: "The generalized assertion of
  privilege must yield to the demonstrated, specific need for evidence in a pending criminal trial."
  Source: Cornell LII, 418 U.S. 683 — https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/418/683
- **The House Judiciary Committee approved three articles of impeachment**: obstruction of justice on
  **27 July 1974**, abuse of power on **29 July**, and contempt of Congress on **30 July**. Two further
  proposed articles were voted down. The full House never voted, because Nixon resigned first.
  Source: U.S. House, History, Art & Archives, "Impeachment Inquiries into President Richard Nixon" —
  https://history.house.gov/HistoricalHighlight/Detail/15032448776
- **The resignation is a piece of paperwork, and the paperwork is the point.** Nixon signed a
  one-sentence letter on the morning of **9 August 1974**. It is addressed to the **Secretary of
  State** — not to Congress, not to the country — because a **law passed in 1792** says that is where
  a president's resignation is filed. It took effect when Secretary of State **Henry Kissinger
  initialled it at 11:35 a.m.** The original is in the National Archives among the General Records of
  the Department of State.
  Sources: National Archives, American Originals exhibit —
  https://www.archives.gov/exhibits/american_originals/nixon2.html ; National Archives Museum,
  "A President Resigns - 50 Years Later" —
  https://museum.archives.gov/featured-document-display-a-president-resigns-50-years-later
- **Gerald Ford took the oath at noon on 9 August 1974** and said, from his reading copy held at the
  Ford Library: "My fellow Americans, our long national nightmare is over. Our Constitution works;
  our great Republic is a government of laws and not of men. Here the people rule." In the same
  remarks he told the country plainly: "I am acutely aware that you have not elected me as your
  President by your ballots," and referred to "the sudden summons to the second office of our
  government only eight months ago."
- **That is the 25th Amendment doing its work, twice over.** Vice President Spiro Agnew resigned in
  October 1973; Ford was nominated under Section 2 and confirmed by both houses, becoming the first
  vice president chosen that way. When Ford became president in August 1974 he nominated Nelson
  Rockefeller the same way. **For about two and a half years the United States had a president and a
  vice president whom no voter had chosen for either job.**
  Source: Gerald R. Ford Presidential Library, swearing-in ceremony reading copy, 12 noon, 9 August
  1974 — https://www.fordlibrarymuseum.gov/library/document/0122/1252055.pdf

### [VERIFY] cleared — the rulings that let public money reach religious schools

Four cases, twenty years, each one a step. The first says a state **may** fund religious schools;
the last three say a state that funds private schools **must** include religious ones.

- ***Zelman v. Simmons-Harris***, 536 U.S. 639, argued **20 February 2002**, decided **27 June 2002**,
  **5-4**, Chief Justice **Rehnquist** writing (with O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas); **Stevens,
  Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer** dissenting. Ohio's Pilot Project Scholarship Program in the
  **Cleveland** schools gave up to **$2,250** a year to low-income families and up to **$1,875** to
  others. **82 per cent** of the private schools taking part were religious and **96 per cent** of the
  children using scholarships went to a religious school. Held: an aid programme is not readily open
  to Establishment Clause attack "if it is neutral with respect to religion and provides assistance
  directly to a broad class of citizens who direct government aid to religious schools wholly as a
  result of their own genuine and independent private choice."
  Source: Cornell LII, 536 U.S. 639 — https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/536/639
- ***Trinity Lutheran Church of Columbia v. Comer***, No. 15-577, argued **19 April 2017**, decided
  **26 June 2017**, Chief Justice **Roberts** writing (Kennedy, Alito and Kagan joining in full,
  Thomas and Gorsuch joining except as to footnote 3); **Sotomayor dissenting with Ginsburg**.
  Missouri paid non-profits to resurface playgrounds with rubber from recycled tyres, funded by a fee
  on new tyre sales. The church's preschool came **fifth of 44 applicants** and was refused because
  the state had a flat rule against paying anything to a church. Held: "denying a generally available
  benefit solely on account of religious identity imposes a penalty on the free exercise of religion."
  Source: Cornell LII, No. 15-577 — https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/15-577
- ***Espinoza v. Montana Department of Revenue***, No. 18-1195, argued **22 January 2020**, decided
  **30 June 2020**. **This one was 5-4, and a summary this agent fetched wrongly called it 6-3 with
  Kagan in the majority.** The slip opinion's own line-up: "ROBERTS, C. J., delivered the opinion of
  the Court, in which THOMAS, ALITO, GORSUCH, and KAVANAUGH, JJ., joined. ... GINSBURG, J., filed a
  dissenting opinion, in which KAGAN, J., joined. BREYER, J., filed a dissenting opinion, in which
  KAGAN, J., joined as to Part I. SOTOMAYOR, J., filed a dissenting opinion." Montana's 2015 tax-credit
  scholarship programme gave donors credits of up to **$150**; the state revenue department then made
  a rule barring the scholarships from religious schools, to obey **Article X, Section 6(1)** of
  Montana's constitution, which bans aid to a school "controlled in whole or in part by any church,
  sect, or denomination." Held: applying that no-aid clause to shut religious schools out of the
  programme violated the Free Exercise Clause.
  Sources: Supreme Court slip opinion 18-1195 —
  https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/19pdf/18-1195_g314.pdf ;
  Cornell LII, No. 18-1195 — https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/18-1195
- ***Carson v. Makin***, No. 20-1088, argued **8 December 2021**, decided **21 June 2022**, **6-3**,
  Chief Justice **Roberts** writing; **Breyer, Sotomayor and Kagan** dissenting. Maine has towns too
  small to run a secondary school, so it pays tuition for their children at a school of the family's
  choosing; since **1981** the school had to be "nonsectarian." Held: "Maine's 'nonsectarian'
  requirement for otherwise generally available tuition assistance payments violates the Free Exercise
  Clause."
  Source: Cornell LII, No. 20-1088 — https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/20-1088

### [VERIFY] cleared — the 2007 ruling on using race to assign children to schools

- ***Parents Involved in Community Schools v. Seattle School District No. 1***, Nos. **05-908** and
  **05-915**, argued **4 December 2006**, decided **28 June 2007**, **5-4**.
- **Seattle School District No. 1** sorted children as white or nonwhite and used race as a
  tiebreaker in handing out high school places. **Jefferson County, Kentucky** sorted children as
  black or "other" for elementary school assignments and transfer requests. Both plans were held to
  violate the Equal Protection Clause because they were not narrowly tailored to a compelling
  interest.
- **Read the line-up carefully; it changes what the case means.** Chief Justice **Roberts** was joined
  by **Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Alito** for Parts I, II, III-A and III-C, but only by **Scalia,
  Thomas and Alito** for Parts III-B and IV. The most-quoted sentence in the case — "The way to stop
  discrimination on the basis of race is to stop discriminating on the basis of race." — is in a part
  **four** justices joined, not five.
- **Justice Kennedy's separate opinion is the one that controls what schools may do**, because he was
  the fifth vote and he would not join Part IV. He wrote: "In the administration of public schools by
  the state and local authorities it is permissible to consider the racial makeup of schools and to
  adopt general policies to encourage a diverse student body, one aspect of which is its racial
  composition." He listed what is allowed: "strategic site selection of new schools; drawing
  attendance zones with general recognition of the demographics of neighborhoods; allocating resources
  for special programs; recruiting students and faculty in a targeted fashion; and tracking
  enrollments, performance, and other statistics by race." Of a colour-blind Constitution he wrote:
  "In the real world, it is regrettable to say, it cannot be a universal constitutional principle."
- **So the plain statement for the book is:** after 2007 a school district may still try to mix its
  schools, but it may not decide an individual child's school by that child's race.
  Sources: Cornell LII syllabus — https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/05-908.ZS.html ;
  opinion — https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/05-908.ZO.html ;
  Kennedy concurrence — https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/05-908.ZC1.html

### *Bush v. Gore*, 531 U.S. 98 — the courts decide a presidential election

- Decided **12 December 2000**, **per curiam** (unsigned). Seven justices agreed there was a
  constitutional problem with the recount; **five** agreed on the equal protection holding, and
  **five** voted to end the recount rather than send it back for a fix. **Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg
  and Breyer** dissented. **Rehnquist, Scalia and Thomas** wrote separately.
- The Florida Supreme Court had ordered a hand recount of about **9,000** Miami-Dade ballots that
  machines had read as blank, ordered **215** Palm Beach and **168** Miami-Dade votes added to the

  With no time left, the Court stopped the count.
- **The opinion limits itself**: "Our consideration is limited to the present circumstances, for the
  problem of equal protection in election processes generally presents many complexities."
  Source: Cornell LII, 531 U.S. 98 — https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/531/98

### 6 January 2021 and the count of electoral votes

- **What the joint session is.** Every four years the House and Senate meet in the House chamber; the
  President of the Senate (the Vice President) presides; tellers open the states' certificates and
  read the votes; the presiding officer announces the result. A written objection to a state's votes
  used to need only **one member of each chamber**; the two houses then separate and each debates and
  votes. Electoral votes have not been thrown out by a joint session since the late nineteenth
  century. Objections were debated and rejected in **1969, 2005 and 2021**.
  Source: CRS report R48309, "Joint Session of Congress for Counting Electoral Votes for President,"
  **10 December 2024** — https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/PDF/R48309/R48309.2.pdf
- **The two objections of 6 January 2021 and how they ended**, from the Congressional Record as cited
  by CRS: the objection to **Arizona's** electoral votes was rejected by the Senate **6 ayes to 93
  nays** and by the House **121 to 303**; the objection to **Pennsylvania's** was rejected by the
  Senate **7 ayes to 92 nays** and by the House **138 to 282**. Congress finished the count and
  declared the result.
- **The Capitol was attacked while that session was under way.** The Government Accountability Office,
  reporting in 2022, found that over roughly **seven hours** about **140 police officers** were
  assaulted, about **80** of them U.S. Capitol Police; that about **114 Capitol Police officers
  reported injuries**; and that damage came to about **$1.5 million**. GAO also found that Capitol
  Police planning for the day "did not reflect the potential for extreme violence aimed at the
  Capitol," even though the force had information that protesters could be armed and were planning to
  target Congress.
  Sources: GAO-22-105001 — https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-22-105001 ;
  GAO-22-104829 — https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-22-104829 ;
  GAO-23-106625 — https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-23-106625.pdf
- **Documents that were not certificates.** CRS records that in 2020 "individuals in some states
  prepared documents alleging to be certificates of vote of electors for President and Vice President,
  and sent the documents to the Senate and the National Archives. The documents did not carry a
  certificate of ascertainment issued by the state executive as required by federal law. Therefore,
  they were not presented to the joint session." The National Archives has published them as
  "2020 presidential election unofficial certificates."
  Source: https://www.archives.gov/foia/2020-presidential-election-unofficialcertificates
- **Congress then changed the rules.** The **Electoral Count Reform and Presidential Transition
  Improvement Act of 2022** was enacted as part of **Public Law 117-328**, 136 Stat. 5240,
  **29 December 2022**. It raised the threshold for an objection from one member of each chamber to
  **one fifth of each chamber** — about **87 Representatives and 20 Senators**. It named the state's
  **executive**, usually the governor, as the one official who issues the certificate of ascertainment,
  and required electors to be appointed under state laws enacted **before** election day. And it wrote
  the Vice President's role into the statute in so many words. **3 U.S.C. §15(b)(2)**: "The President
  of the Senate shall have no power to solely determine, accept, reject, or otherwise adjudicate or
  resolve disputes over the proper certificate of ascertainment of appointment of electors, the
  validity of electors, or the votes of electors."

### Era 1 — Before 1500

The outline cell claims: the Haudenosaunee Confederacy and its Great Law, "a written-down oral
constitution with defined roles, including clan mothers choosing and removing leaders"; Pueblo town
governments; Mississippian chiefdoms.

- **The Great Law of Peace** — *Gayanesshagowa*. The National Museum of the American Indian's
  Haudenosaunee guide for educators describes it as the law that "provides the Haudenosaunee people
  with instructions on how to treat others, directs them on how to maintain a democratic society," and
  calls it "one of the earliest examples of a formal democratic governance structure." The full telling
  "takes several days to tell." Five nations joined first — **Mohawk, Oneida, Cayuga, Seneca and
  Onondaga** — and the **Tuscarora joined in 1722**, making six.
- **The seats are counted, and they add up.** The Grand Council is "made up of fifty *hoyaneh*":
  **fourteen Onondaga, nine Mohawk, eight Seneca, ten Cayuga and nine Oneida**. Positions are unpaid
  and for life. "When decisions are made or laws passed, all council members must agree on the issue;
  this is called CONSENSUS."
- **The clan mothers' power, in the source's own words:** council members "are selected by the clan
  mothers"; "Clan mothers serve for life. They are appointed by consensus of the clan members. Clan
  mothers have the authority to remove from office any *hoyaneh* who does not serve the best interests
  of his nation." So the outline's phrasing — clan mothers choosing and removing leaders — is exact.
  Source: National Museum of the American Indian, *Haudenosaunee Guide for Educators* —
  https://americanindian.si.edu/sites/1/files/pdf/education/HaudenosauneeGuide.pdf
- **A date this book must not state as settled.** Sources give the Confederacy's founding anywhere from
  about **1142** to the fifteenth or sixteenth century, and the NMAI guide itself gives no year, saying
  only "Long ago." **Do not put a founding year in the book.** Say that it was founded before Europeans
  arrived and that the date is not agreed.
- **A related claim to handle carefully.** The NMAI guide says that in **1987 the U.S. Senate formally
  acknowledged, in a special resolution, the influence of the Haudenosaunee Great Law of Peace on the
  U.S. Constitution**, and quotes Benjamin Franklin on the Confederacy's union. **Historians disagree
  about how much the Constitution actually took from it.** If the book uses this, say both things: that
  Congress passed such a resolution, and that the extent of the influence is argued over.
- **Pueblo town governments.** When Spanish expeditions came up the Rio Grande from **1540**, they
  found people who were not nomadic but settled farmers living in **independent communities, each with
  its own leaders, culture and language**. At Zuni, authority sat with a council of priests, with the
  bow priests dealing with outsiders. The point for this chapter: each pueblo governed itself; there was
  no single Pueblo state. (National Park Service, Pueblo and Puebloan pages, e.g.
  https://www.nps.gov/elmo/learn/historyculture/the-puebloans.htm and
  https://www.nps.gov/petr/planyourvisit/pueblos.htm)
- **Mississippian chiefdoms.** **Cahokia**, on the Mississippi east of present-day St. Louis, covered
  over **4,000 acres** and at its height between about **1050 and 1150 CE** may have held **10,000 to
  20,000 people**, with higher estimates given. It was led as a chiefdom: flat-topped mounds carried the
  buildings of religious and secular officials, and burial mounds held high-status graves. **Population
  estimates for Cahokia vary widely between archaeologists; give a range, never one number.**
  (National Park Service, Cahokia Mounds State Historic Site: World Heritage Site —
  https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/cahokia-mounds-state-historic-site-world-heritage-site.htm ;
  Ocmulgee Mounds, Mississippian Culture —
  https://www.nps.gov/ocmu/learn/historyculture/mississippian-culture.htm)

### Era 2 — The 1500s (state = thin, and it should stay thin)

The outline cell claims: the first European government here answers to a king across an ocean; Spanish
governors, the Laws of the Indies, and church authority in Florida and New Mexico.

- **Florida.** **Pedro Menéndez de Avilés** founded **St. Augustine on 8 September 1565**, under orders
  from **King Philip II**, sailing from Spain with ten ships and about a thousand men. He "soon became
  Florida's first governor." His title was **adelantado** — a Spanish colonial office granted by
  contract with the crown. St. Augustine is the oldest continuously occupied European settlement in the
  continental United States.
  Sources: NPS, "Pedro Menendez" — https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/pedro-menendez.htm ;
  NPS, "St. Augustine Town Plan Historic District" —
  https://www.nps.gov/places/st-augustine-town-plan-historic-district-st-augustine-florida.htm
- **The Laws of the Indies, and what the outline should mean by them.** The National Park Service dates
  the town-planning rules to **1573**. They required a town to be "laid out in a gridiron with a
  symmetrical network of streets running both parallel and perpendicular to a central town plaza," with
  the important civic and religious buildings facing that plaza. **St. Augustine's own grid was laid out
  in 1603, thirty-eight years after the town was founded** — so the rules reached the ground slowly, and
  in the 1500s the phrase means a body of royal orders being written, not a code already in force here.
- **New Mexico.** **Juan de Oñate** left Santa Bárbara in early 1598 under a contract with the Viceroy
  in Mexico City to settle New Mexico and be its governor, in exchange for loans, supplies and soldiers.
  In 1598 he founded **San Juan de los Caballeros** on the Rio Grande, the first European capital of New
  Mexico, and **governed until 1608**. In the autumn of 1598 he went to the pueblos in turn and, through
  translators, required each to swear allegiance to the Spanish crown, and assigned a missionary to each.
  That is what "Spanish governors and church authority" means in practice: one man holding a royal
  contract, a soldier's commission and a friar's list at the same time.
  Sources: NPS, "San Gabriel del Yunque-Ouinge and San Miguel" —
  https://www.nps.gov/subjects/travelspanishmissions/san-gabriel-del-yunque-ouinge-and-san-miguel.htm ;
  NPS, "History and Significance of El Camino Real de Tierra Adentro" —
  https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/history-and-significance-of-el-camino-real-de-tierra-adentro.htm
- **What this chapter deliberately does not tell here.** Oñate's soldiers attacked **Acoma Pueblo in
  January 1599**, and the killings and the sentences that followed belong to **`native-nations`**, which
  already owns them (see `outlines/BOOK-OUTLINE.md`, "Acoma, 1598 and 1599"). **Parked here for a later
  patch of this chapter, because it is a government-and-law fact and nobody else is telling it that
  way:** the Spanish crown put its own governor on trial for Acoma. Oñate faced **30 charges, was
  convicted on 12, and was banished for life from New Mexico, exiled and fined.** Whether the mutilation
  sentence he ordered was actually carried out is disputed among historians — **the order is in the
  record; the execution of it is not settled.** If a future agent adds this to era 2, say exactly that.

### Added 10 September 2026 — *Harper v. Virginia State Board of Elections*, 383 U.S. 663

The 24th Amendment reaches federal elections only. State poll taxes fell separately, in *Harper*,
decided **24 March 1966**, **Justice William O. Douglas** writing; **Black, Harlan and Stewart**
dissenting. Two cases were joined: *Annie E. Harper et al. v. Virginia State Board of Elections* and
*Evelyn Butts v. Albertis Harrison, Governor*. **Section 173 of Virginia's constitution** set "an
annual poll tax not exceeding $1.50 on every resident of the State 21 years of age and over," and
paying it was a condition of voting. Held: a state breaks the Equal Protection Clause when it makes
"the affluence of the voter or payment of any fee an electoral standard."
Source: Cornell LII, 383 U.S. 663 — https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/383/663
