## 1. Before 1500

- **No wheeled transport, no horses.** The horses of the Americas died out after the ice age; there was no large draft animal left north of Mexico. Historians' standard explanation for the absent wheel: without an animal to pull a cart, a wheel had no transport work to do, and the continent-wide trail-and-river network moved people and goods without one. Wheeled toys existed in Mesoamerica; no wheeled vehicles in use for transport. (Sources: general syntheses — e.g., science-history essays on pre-Columbian technology; Britannica-level consensus. Write plainly, no speculation beyond "no draft animals + working alternatives.")
- **The trail network was real infrastructure, with names.**
  - **The Great Trail** (also "Great Path"): footpath network of Algonquian- and Iroquoian-speaking peoples connecting New England and the mid-Atlantic to the Great Lakes region. (Sources: Great Trail references; W.Va. Encyclopedia "Indian Trails.")
  - **The Natchez Trace**: began as bison paths from the lower Mississippi toward salt licks near present Nashville; followed, widened, and maintained by the Natchez, Choctaw, and Chickasaw for trade, travel, diplomacy, and war. Human use traced back roughly 8,000 years. (Sources: NPS Natchez Trace Parkway history pages; Mississippi Encyclopedia; Natchez Trace Parkway Association.)
  - Long-distance trade as proof of the network: marine shell and native copper moved between the Great Lakes and the Gulf Coast as early as **6000 BC** (Library of Congress research guide, Native American trails).
  - Many trails became colonial roads and later highways (Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia, "Trails (Indian)").
- **Watercraft.**
  - **Dugout canoe**: hollowed single log, fire and tools; principal craft of the Eastern Woodlands and Southeast for thousands of years. Dugouts as old as ~4,000+ years found in Midwest lakes (Smithsonian Magazine, 2024–25 reporting on Wisconsin lake finds).
  - **Birchbark canoe**: Northeast/Great Lakes; light (portable by one or two people over portages), shallow draft, repairable from forest materials; scaled from solo boats to large freight canoes. (Sources: NativeTech; Canadian Encyclopedia "Birchbark Canoe" — Canadian source, same technology zone.)
  - **Portages**: overland links between watersheds; with trails they made a continental network.
- **The travois**: two poles dragged behind a **dog** (the only pack/draft animal), load slung between; Plains standard before the horse. (Source: Encyclopedia of the Great Plains, "Travois.")

## 2. The 1500s

- **Horses return by ship**: Columbus's second voyage carried horses to the Caribbean, 1493; onto the continental mainland with **Coronado, 1540–42**; **Oñate, 1598**, brought the first wagon train — about 83 wagons and carts up the Camino Real into New Mexico. All verified in `research/research-migration.md` (kept there with sources; reused here for the vehicles/wheels angle only). The horse's spread among Native nations and its timing dispute → `native-nations`.
- **St. Augustine, founded September 8, 1565** by Pedro Menéndez de Avilés with ~1,500 soldiers and colonists: oldest continuously occupied European settlement and **port** in the continental U.S.; a defense/resupply harbor for Spanish shipping between Spain and the Caribbean. (Sources: City of St. Augustine official history; EBSCO research starter; Smithsonian Magazine travel-history feature.)

## 3. The 1600s

- **First chartered ferry**: Massachusetts Court of Assistants, **June 14, 1631** — "Edw: Converse hath vndertaken to sett vpp a fferry betwixte Charlton & Boston," authorized to charge 2 pence a single passenger, 1 penny each for two or more. Some Boston sources date the service's start 1630. Often called the first chartered public transportation in North America. In **1640** the colony granted the Charlestown ferry income to Harvard College (the ferry had earned £50 in 1639). (Sources: Court of Assistants record quoted by West End Museum; Charlestown Historical Society timeline; MBTA "History of the T.")
- **Edward Converse (Convers)**, 1589–1663: Puritan settler, deacon, ferry operator — the era's ordinary-person story. (Sources above.)
- **First post rider / Boston Post Road**: the first rider left New York for Boston in **January 1673** (departure January 22; the papers used the old-style year 1672/3), via New Haven–Hartford–Springfield–Worcester, ~250 miles, arriving in about two weeks. Route pushed by Gov. Francis Lovelace of New York with Connecticut's John Winthrop Jr. The mail → `news-communication` (their outline has "[VERIFY 1673]" — confirmation parked for them); the road that grew from the riders' path — the Boston Post Road — is transportation's. Parts followed existing Native paths. (Sources: Connecticut History / CTHumanities project; New England Historical Society.)
- **Rivers as roads**: colonial freight overwhelmingly waterborne — canoes, shallops, coastal ships. General colonial-history consensus; keep unquantified.

## 4. 1700 to 1750

- **Conestoga wagon**: first documented use of the name **December 31, 1717** — James Logan (William Penn's former secretary) recorded buying a "Conestogoe Waggon" in his account book. Developed by Pennsylvania German/Swiss builders along the Conestoga River, Lancaster County: curved bed (cargo settles to the middle), white cloth cover over hoops, 4–6 horse teams, freight hauls of 60+ miles to Philadelphia. Dominant eastern freight wagon into the railroad era. NOTE: "born ~1750" (the seed's guess) is wrong — first record 1717, peak use mid-to-late 1700s onward. (Sources: explorepahistory.com PHMC marker page; Lancaster County historical accounts, incl. the Logan account-book citation.)
- **Stage lines begin**: New Jersey patent to Hugh Huddy for a Burlington–Perth Amboy stage line, **1706**; irregular New York–Philadelphia coach service from **1732**; Trenton–New Brunswick stage 1738; Bordentown & New York line 1740; by 1756 several regular NY–Philadelphia services taking about three days. (Sources: Steven M. Roth, "Stage Operations and the Mails in New Jersey," NJ Postal History Society; Colonial Williamsburg Digital Library research report "Stage Waggons and Coaches.")
- **Corduroy roads**: logs laid side-by-side across swampy stretches; named for the ribbed cloth. Standard early-road practice; descriptive, no single citation needed beyond FHWA general highway history.
- **Great Wagon Road**: ~730 miles Philadelphia → Shenandoah Valley → toward Augusta, Georgia; heavy use from the 1720s. Verified in `research/research-migration.md`; the road is ours, the movers theirs.
- **Sarah Kemble Knight**: Boston businesswoman; October 1704 journey Boston→New Haven→New York riding with post riders as guides; journal (published 1825) records fords, canoe crossings in the dark, feared bridges, hired guides — the best eyewitness account of what the road was like. Featured in `home-family` (slug sarah-kemble-knight — households angle); ours is the road (slug sarah-kemble-knight-transportation). (Sources: EBSCO; Connecticut History; the published Journal of Madam Knight.)

## 5. 1750 to 1800

- **Philadelphia and Lancaster Turnpike**: chartered/ground broken **1792**; sources disagree on the opening — some say completed and in use **1794**, most say first used **1795**. State the range. **62 miles**, engineered stone-and-gravel surface, toll gates; the country's first long-distance engineered toll road; model for the turnpike boom of the early 1800s. The seed's "[VERIFY 1795]" resolves to "1794–95, sources differ." (Sources: FHWA "Rakeman" highway-history page for 1795; Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia "Turnpikes"; AARoads wiki summary of the charter dates.)
- **John Fitch**:
  - **August 22, 1787**: successful steamboat trial on the Delaware before Constitutional Convention delegates; propulsion by banks of steam-driven oars.
  - **Summer 1790**: scheduled passenger service on the Delaware (Philadelphia–Trenton/Burlington/Bordentown area, also Wilmington), boat *Experiment* — the first scheduled steamboat passenger service anywhere; carried up to ~30 passengers. Commercially failed: thin ridership, breakdowns, lost financing. Died 1798.
  - The seed's "[VERIFY]" on Fitch resolves: he WAS first with a working, scheduled steamboat; Fulton was first to make it a lasting business. State that order plainly.
  - (Sources: Pennsylvania Heritage magazine "Poor John Fitch"; Encyclopedia.com Fitch biography; Historical Society of Montgomery County PA; explorepahistory.com marker.)

## 6. 1800 to 1850

- **Fulton's 1807 run**: the North River Steamboat (popularly "Clermont" later) left New York **August 17, 1807**, reached Albany — **150 miles in 32 hours**; sailing sloops took about **four days**. Regular commercial service began immediately; partner/financier Robert Livingston held the New York steamboat monopoly. (Sources: Hudson River Valley Institute essay; History.com; Wikipedia "North River Steamboat" cross-checked.)
  - **"Fulton's Folly" honesty**: the mockery nickname is repeated in nearly every retelling (Encyclopedia.com has an entry for the phrase), but contemporary 1807 documentation is thin; it is a staple of later accounts. Say "later accounts say doubters called it…" — do not present as a documented 1807 quote.
- **Steamboat explosions**: Mississippi-system boiler explosions killed thousands over the era; the disasters and the Steamboat Acts → `disasters` (their outline already carries it with their own VERIFY tags).
- **National Road**: authorized 1806 (Jefferson); construction began **Cumberland, Maryland, 1811**; reached **Wheeling (Ohio River) 1818**; extended to Vandalia, Illinois, by the 1830s, where federal funding stopped. First highway built entirely with federal funds. (Sources: NPS "The National Road"; FHWA highway history; Britannica.)
- **Erie Canal**:
  - Dug **1817–1825**, opened **October 26, 1825**; **363 miles**, Albany (Hudson) to Buffalo (Lake Erie); cost about **$7.1 million**; tolls repaid construction in about **nine years**. Mocked as **"Clinton's Ditch"/"Clinton's Folly"** during construction. (Sources: Library of Congress This-Month-in-Business-History guide; EBSCO "Erie Canal Opens"; govinfo.gov feature.)
  - **Freight cost drop**: about **$100/ton by wagon to under $10 — some sources about $6/ton** (state the range; LOC guide and EBSCO give the $100→$6–10 span).
  - **Hoggees**: boy mule-drivers, often ~10 years old; about **$8–$10/month**; walked **18–20 miles a day**; six hours on, six off; frequently cheated of pay. (Sources: Lockport Union-Sun & Journal "Erie Canal Discovery: Child labor on the canal" and "Life for young workers on the canal" columns; Erie Canal Museum materials.)
  - **DeWitt Clinton** (governor, canal champion): poured Lake Erie water into New York harbor at the opening ("wedding of the waters"), 1825. (LOC; EBSCO.)
- **James A. Garfield, canal boy, 1848**: age 16, ran away to work on the **Ohio & Erie Canal** boat ***Evening Star***; about **six weeks** hauling coal/copper ore/wood; could not swim; documented dramatic night fall overboard; contracted **malaria** and went home. Campaign biographies later inflated the falls (the "fourteen times" figure is legend—flag it). (Sources: NPS James A. Garfield NHS article "A Fickle Current"; Eno Center for Transportation "James Garfield (1881): A Canal That Forged a City and a President"; EBSCO.)
- **B&O Railroad**: first stone laid **July 4, 1828**; first **13 miles** (Baltimore–Ellicott's Mills) opened **1830**; first U.S. railroad in scheduled freight+passenger common-carrier service. (Sources: B&O Railroad Museum.)
  - **Tom Thumb race, August 28, 1830**: Peter Cooper's demonstration engine racing a horse-drawn car, losing when a blower belt slipped — **the story rests on John H. B. Latrobe's 1868 lecture, 38 years later; no contemporary documentation confirms the race**. B&O Museum and historians present it as tradition/legend. Tell it labeled as legend. (Sources: B&O Railroad Museum "Tom Thumb"; History.com "When a Horse Raced Against a Locomotive"; american-rails.com.)
- **DeWitt Clinton locomotive**: **August 9, 1831**, Mohawk & Hudson, Albany→Schenectady, 16 miles in about an hour — New York State's first steam passenger run; ~4th locomotive built in the U.S. (Sources: The Henry Ford collection page; transportationhistory.org; Wikipedia cross-checked.)
- **First street railway**: **November 14, 1832** — the *John Mason* horsecar of the New York & Harlem Railroad, lower Manhattan; horse-drawn omnibus (no rails) in NYC from 1827 (Abraham Brower). (Sources: Hagley Museum; History.com; Museum of the City of New York blog.)
- **Covered wagons and ox teams** (from Ch3 park, 2026-07-23): the wagon-and-team as machine, design, loads, ferries/bridges along the routes (e.g., Brigham Young's Mormon Trail ferries) — ours; the journeys → `migration`. (Facts source-checked in `research/research-migration.md`.)

## 7. 1850 to 1900

- **Rail mileage**: about **9,000 miles in 1850** (Census historical statistics: 8,589 in operation); by 1900 roughly **193,000–215,000** depending on whether sidings/multiple tracks are counted — state "more than 190,000" or give the count basis. (Sources: LOC railroad history guides & classroom materials; Historical Statistics of the United States, Census 1949 compendium.)
- **Transcontinental railroad** (merging earlier parked notes, all retained):
  - Completed **Promontory Summit, Utah, May 10, 1869** (golden spike). Union Pacific west from Omaha, Central Pacific east from Sacramento. Coast-to-coast travel fell from ~4 months to ~1 week. (LOC; Gilder Lehrman; parked 2026-07-23 from Ch3 research, sources checked there.)
  - **Chinese workers**: Central Pacific hired about **10,000–12,000** Chinese workers 1865–1869, most from Guangdong; roughly **1,200 died**. (PBS American Experience; American Immigration Council; parked from Ch2 research 2026-07-23.)
  - **The 1867 strike** (parked from `work-workers` 2026-08-07, retained): **June 25, 1867**, several thousand (estimates 2,000–5,000 — state the range) struck 8 days for equal pay ($35 vs $40/month), shorter days, end of whipping/holding; company cut off food to the mountain camps; ended without formal concessions though experienced men's wages quietly improved; largest U.S. labor action to that date. (LOC business-history guide "1867 Sierra Strike"; PBS American Experience "The Chinese Workers' Strike.")
  - **Hung Wah**: appears on the CP's **January 1864** payroll supervising its first ~23-man Chinese crew (with Ah Toy); became the leading Chinese headman over hundreds and at times thousands — handled pay, living arrangements, company relations. Named-individual documentation of ordinary Chinese laborers is scarce because payrolls usually listed only headmen — the story must say his name stands for thousands. (Sources: Stanford Chinese Railroad Workers in North America Project; Gordon Chang's project publications; CPRR Museum "Chinese Laborers and the Construction of the Central Pacific.")
  - **Ten-Mile Day**: **April 28, 1869** — 10 miles 56 feet of track laid in one day (eight Irish rail-carriers plus several thousand Chinese workers). **Ging Cui, Wong Fook, Lee Shao** — three of the eight men who brought up the last rails — rode in the 1919 50th-anniversary parade. (CPRR Museum; Stanford project.)
  - **Topic documentary**: *Transcontinental Railroad* (PBS American Experience, **2003**, dir. Michael Chin & Mark Zwonitzer) — verified real; about the railroad, not one person. (PBS; IMDb.)
- **Cornelius Vanderbilt**: shifted capital from steamboats to railroads early 1860s; control of the **New York Central 1867**; merged with his Hudson River RR into NYC&HR **November 1, 1869**; built **Grand Central Depot, opened 1871**; died **1877** leaving about **$100 million** — the largest American fortune of its day (Britannica; New York Central history pages). Corporate-power side → `big-business`.
- **Standard time**: **November 18, 1883** — "the day of two noons"; U.S. and Canadian railroads adopted standard zones (plan by William F. Allen of the railway guide); federal law only with the **Standard Time Act, March 19, 1918**. (Sources: LOC business-history guide "Day of Two Noons"; LOC law blog.)
- **Pullman**: George Pullman's first purpose-built luxury sleeper, the ***Pioneer*, 1865**; folding upper berths, extendable seats. Britannica notes Pullman got cars attached to Lincoln's funeral train — repeat with Britannica attribution only, it is part fact part company legend. Pullman Palace Car Co. from 1867. The porters → era 8; the 1894 strike → `work-workers`/`big-business`. (Britannica; Smithsonian Magazine.)
- **Brakemen and couplers**: link-and-pin coupling required standing between moving cars; mangled hands were "the badge of the experienced worker." **Eli Janney patented the automatic coupler 1873** (ASME landmark); Westinghouse air brake existed from 1869 (standard reference); adoption was slow until the **Safety Appliance Act of 1893** (effective 1900) mandated automatic couplers and air brakes in interstate service. Coupling accidents fell from **~11,000 (1892) to ~2,000 (1902)** while employment grew. (Sources: ASME Janney Coupler landmark documents; Railroad Safety Appliance Act histories; Linda Hall Library exhibition "Couplers & Brakes.") — Parked for `work-workers` (the job-danger angle).
- **Sprague / Richmond** (parked from `city-building` 2026-08-07, retained): first successful large-scale electric street railway — regular operation **February 2, 1888**, Richmond, Virginia (10 cars at start; ~12 miles of track and 23 cars by May 1888); within ~2 years ~**110 electric railways** built or under contract on Sprague equipment. IEEE Engineering Milestone. Vehicle/motors/Sprague = ours; "streetcar suburbs" city-shape effect = `city-building`. (IEEE Milestone "Richmond Union Passenger Railway, 1888," ethw.org; National Inventors Hall of Fame.)
- **Safety bicycle craze, 1890s**: equal wheels, chain drive, pneumatic tires → mass adoption, including by women. **Susan B. Anthony to Nellie Bly, New York World, February 2, 1896**: bicycling "has done more to emancipate women than anything else in the world… I stand and rejoice every time I see a woman ride by on a wheel." Documented interview — quote verified, date verified. (Sources: the 1896 New York World interview as reproduced by Connecticut teaching archives (teachitct.org PDF) and multiple bicycle-history secondaries.)
- **Mark Twain**: steamboat pilot's license **April 9, 1859**, age 23; piloted until the war closed the river (1861); pen name = leadsman's call for two fathoms; *Life on the Mississippi* (1883). Documentary: **Mark Twain (Ken Burns, 2001; aired PBS January 2002)** — about his whole life. (Sources: History.com this-day; twainquotes.com steamboat records; PBS Ken Burns site.)

## 8. 1900 to 1950

- **Model T**: introduced **October 1, 1908**, at **$825**; price fell to **$260 by 1925** (touring-car figures $850→under $300 also cited — the range is model/body dependent; state one series consistently); **15 millionth car May 26, 1927**, last day of Model T production. Assembly line & Ford himself → `technology` (their researched block: magneto line spring 1913, chassis line October 7, 1913, $5 day January 1914). (Sources: Ford corporate history; Britannica "Model T"; History.com.)
- **Lincoln Highway, 1913**: first named coast-to-coast auto route, Times Square to San Francisco, private boosters (Carl Fisher, Henry Joy); broken into numbered routes after 1926. (FHWA "The Lincoln Highway.")
- **U.S. numbered routes, 1926** — including **Route 66, designated November 11, 1926**: Chicago→Santa Monica, **2,448 miles**; fully paved only by the late 1930s; "Main Street of America." Dust Bowl migration on it → `migration` (lead). **Decertified June 27, 1985** (era 9/10 note). (Sources: FHWA; Britannica "Route 66"; Route 66 centennial/timeline references.)
- **First scheduled airline**: **St. Petersburg–Tampa Airboat Line, January 1, 1914** — pilot **Tony Jannus**, Benoist Type XIV flying boat; ~23-minute crossing of Tampa Bay, flown low over the water; first round-trip ticket auctioned for **$400** to former St. Petersburg mayor **Abram C. Pheil**. Operated about four months. (Sources: IATA centennial history pages; *Tampa Bay History* journal (USF Digital Commons); Tony Jannus Society.)
- **Airmail**: first regular route **May 15, 1918**, Washington–Philadelphia–New York (218 miles), Army pilots first, Post Office pilots from August 1918. **Kelly Act 1925** moved mail to private contractors; the airlines grew from those contracts. (Smithsonian National Postal Museum; USPS history.)
- **Jack Knight, February 22–23, 1921**: day-and-night transcontinental airmail relay to prove air beat rail; Knight flew North Platte→Omaha, then — when the next pilot refused in the storm — continued Omaha→Chicago at night in snow/fog over ground he had never flown; relay delivered coast-to-coast in **33 h 20 m over 2,629 miles**; Congress then funded the airmail. (Smithsonian National Postal Museum "Jack Knight Saves the Airmail" and exhibition pages; American Heritage Center, Univ. of Wyoming.)
- **Lindbergh, May 1927** → the flight is `exploration`'s (their verified story). Transport effect ("Lindbergh boom"): U.S. airline passengers **5,782 (1926) → 173,405 (1928)**; pilot-license applications tripled in 1927. (FAA Historical Chronology 1926–1996; Cradle of Aviation Museum; EBSCO.)
- **DC-3, 1936**: 21+ passengers, 207 mph; per Smithsonian NASM curator van der Linden, "widely considered to be the first airliner capable of making money just by carrying passengers"; by 1939 DC-3s carried ~90% of world airline traffic. (Smithsonian NASM object page; Smithsonian Magazine.)
- **Pullman porters**: the sleeping-car service jobs; Pullman for years the largest single employer of Black Americans (standard; NMAAHC). Union fight/A. Philip Randolph → `work-workers` (their verified story + film *10,000 Black Men Named George*). **Documentary: *Miles of Smiles, Years of Struggle* (1982, Jack Santino & Paul Wagner; four regional Emmys)** — eight retired porters plus **Rosina Tucker** (porter's wife, organizer; narrates) tell the job from inside: required smiles, tip dependence, sleepless runs, being called "George." Porters speaking in the transcript include **C.L. Dellums** (fired October 1927 for organizing) and **E.D. Nixon** (later called the Montgomery boycott meeting). (Sources: Wikipedia film page; NMAAHC object record; Folkstreams full transcript; paulwagnerfilms.com.)
- **WWII**: railroads carried about **43.7 million service members, Dec 1941–Aug 1945 — over 97% of organized troop movement**; ~100,000 personnel on troop trains daily; half of all Pullman sleepers in troop service. (Association of American Railroads fact sheet; american-rails.com; Trains magazine.) **The Jeep**: ~**640,000** built (≈363,000 Willys MB + ≈280,000 Ford GPW). (Kaiser Willys history; Jeep corporate history.)

## 9. 1950 to 2000

- **Interstate Highway System** (merging the parked Ch3 note, retained):
  - **Federal-Aid Highway Act signed June 29, 1956** (National Interstate and Defense Highways Act); **41,000 miles** authorized; largest U.S. public-works program to that date. Today about **48,900 miles**; core designated mileage "more than 46,000." Congress added Eisenhower's name (Dwight D. Eisenhower National System of Interstate and Defense Highways) in **1990**. (National Archives milestone-documents page; FHWA interstate history; Eisenhower Presidential Library.)
  - **Eisenhower's 1919 convoy**: first Army transcontinental motor convoy, **81 vehicles, 3,251 miles, Washington DC→San Francisco, 62 days** — mud, breakdowns, failed bridges; Lt. Col. Eisenhower volunteered and reported on it; with his 1945 view of German autobahns, the formative case for the interstates. (Eisenhower Presidential Library; U.S. Army article; FHWA.)
  - **Topic documentary**: *Divided Highways* (PBS, aired October 22, 1997; Hott & Lewis; Peabody Award) — about the interstates. (Peabody Awards; Spokesman-Review TV review.)
  - Route 66 bypassed by interstates; **decertified June 27, 1985** (AASHTO). (FHWA; Britannica.)
- **Jet age**: **Pan Am Boeing 707, New York→Paris, October 26, 1958** — first U.S. transatlantic jet service; 111 passengers + 12 crew, ~8 hours; roughly double piston speed/capacity. (EBSCO; thisdayinaviation.com; Smithsonian Air & Space Magazine "The Flight That Changed Everything.")
- **Airline deregulation**: **Airline Deregulation Act signed October 24, 1978** (Carter). Fare effects — give ranges with sources: Brookings (Morrison & Winston) estimate fares ~**22% lower** than they would have been under continued regulation; industry/GAO figures show real average fares down ~**40–45%** over the following decades. (Econlib "Airline Deregulation"; GAO reports; Brookings.)
- **Amtrak**: created by the **Rail Passenger Service Act of 1970**; service began **May 1, 1971**, taking over most intercity passenger trains from money-losing private railroads. (Eno Center "Amtrak at 50"; EBSCO.)
- **Containerization**:
  - **Ideal X sailed Port Newark → Houston, April 26, 1956, with 58 containers** (35-foot boxes, truck-trailer size), arriving five days later. (Port Houston; Maritime Executive.)
  - **Loading cost: about $5.86/ton hand-loaded vs about $0.16/ton by container** (1956 figures; from Marc Levinson's *The Box*, universally cited). Label as Levinson's calculation.
  - **Malcom McLean**: NC trucker (one used truck, 1934), sold his trucking company to bet on sea containers; standardization (ISO, late 1960s) made the box global. (Maritime Executive "The Story of Malcom McLean"; American Business History Center.) No film about him — none claimed.
- **Flight attendants / Dusty Roads**: **Barbara "Dusty" Roads (1928–2023)**, American Airlines from 1950; fought the fire-at-32 and no-marriage rules — lobbied Congress on her own DC flights, staged the "pick the 32-year-old out of the lineup" press conference; co-founded the Association of Professional Flight Attendants. **Film: *Fly With Me* (PBS American Experience, premiered February 20, 2024; dirs. Sarah Colt, Helen Dobrowski)** — about flight attendants broadly; Roads is featured. The sex-discrimination law fight → `rights-movements` / `work-workers` (parked). (Wikipedia "Barbara Roads" + obituaries; PBS American Experience.)
- **Minivan**: first **Dodge Caravan/Plymouth Voyager built November 2, 1983**, Windsor, Ontario (1984 models). (Stellantis media timeline; automotive-history sites.)
