## 01 · Before 1500

### Mount Mazama and Crater Lake, about 7,700 years ago
- Mount Mazama is in the southern Cascade Range, about sixty miles north of the Oregon-California line. It holds Crater Lake (*giiwas* in the Klamath language), 1,943 feet deep, the deepest freshwater body in the United States. The climactic eruption "took place about 7,700 years ago" and "was more than forty times as powerful as the eruption of Mount St. Helens in 1980." The eruption and collapse "lasted only a few days." Ash fell "as far away as present-day central Alberta." People lived around the mountain: to the east, people "whose existence is documented through artifacts such as sagebrush sandals". To the south, the eruption "disrupted the lives of people sufficiently so that stories about its wrath and after-effects have been passed down." (Stephen R. Mark, "Mount Mazama," Oregon Encyclopedia, Oregon Historical Society, https://www.oregonencyclopedia.org/articles/mt_mazama/, opened with curl 2026-09-27.)
- Klamath Tribes News: "Native Americans witnessed the formation of Crater Lake 7,700 years ago." "Archeologists have found sandals buried under layers of ash and pumice." "The Klamath Tribes have occupied the Klamath basin for 13,000 years." The Llao and Skell account: Llao, Chief of the Below World, attacked the people with fire after a chief's daughter refused him. Skell, Chief of the Above World, defended them from Mount Shasta. Two holy men sacrificed themselves, Skell drove Llao back into Mount Mazama, the mountain collapsed, and the basin filled with water. ("Crater Lake and its origins," Klamath Tribes News, 2024-04-01, https://www.klamathtribesnews.org/2024/04/01/crater-lake-and-its-origins/, opened 2026-09-27. The same text is on the Crater Lake Trust site.)
- Fort Rock Cave as the find-spot of sagebrush-bark sandals under Mazama pumice: first seen in a search summary. **CHECKED and CORRECTED:** Thomas J. Connolly, "Fort Rock Cave," Oregon Encyclopedia (https://www.oregonencyclopedia.org/articles/fort_rock_cave/, opened with curl 2026-09-27): the cave is in a volcanic butte about half a mile west of the Fort Rock crater in northern Lake County, Oregon. In 1938 University of Oregon archaeologist **Luther Cressman** found "dozens of sandals below a layer of volcanic ash, subsequently determined to have come from the 7,600-year-old eruption of Mount Mazama." Radiocarbon dating later showed the sandals "more than 9,000 years old". Fort Rock-style sandals from half a dozen sites all date "between about 10,500 and 9,100 years old." Adult and child-sized sandals, heavily worn, reflect "the multigenerational extended family that made the cave their home 10,000 years ago." **So the sandals predate the eruption by about 1,500 to 3,000 years. They show people lived in the region, not that the wearers saw the eruption.** The claim that people witnessed it rests on the Klamath account and Oregon Encyclopedia's "disrupted the lives of people" to the south. Note the two Oregon Encyclopedia entries give 7,700 and 7,600 years for the eruption. The outline uses "about 7,700".

### Sunset Crater, about 1085
- "Sunset Crater Volcano erupted in 1085 CE," dated by dendrochronology and dendrochemistry. Earlier tree-ring work gave 1064. Chemical changes in tree rings "between 1085 and 1090 CE" tie the eruption to that span. Ash reached Wupatki National Monument, "about 12-15 miles (20-25 km) away." "Ash deposits deeper than about 6-8 inches (15-20 cm) are not suitable for agriculture." The "Sinagua (ancestral Puebloan) people" living very close "probably had to abandon their agricultural fields and move further away." Where ash was thin, there was "an increase in productivity because the ash served as a mulch." (NPS, "NPS Geodiversity Atlas: Sunset Crater Volcano National Monument, Arizona," https://www.nps.gov/articles/nps-geodiversity-atlas-sunset-crater-volcano-national-monument-arizona.htm, opened 2026-09-27.)
- NPS history page: the eruption, "around the year 1085, displaced area residents, buried homes." "People who lived in the area told stories of the eruption that survive to the present day." 13 federally recognized tribes are traditionally associated with the Flagstaff-area monuments. (NPS, Sunset Crater "History and Culture," https://www.nps.gov/sucr/learn/historyculture/index.htm, opened 2026-09-27.)
- Ash blanket "more than 2,100 square kilometers (810 sq mi)", and many Sinagua back near Wupatki by 1100 (unconfirmed: search summary only).
- **No deaths are recorded.** The sources speak of displacement and buried homes, not of a count of dead.

### The mid-1100s megadrought and Chaco
- Susan C. Ryan (Executive Vice President, Crow Canyon Research Institute), "The A.D. 1130–1180 Megadrought in the Northern Southwest," Crow Canyon Archaeological Center, posted June 5, 2024 (https://crowcanyon.org/news/the-a-d-1130-1180-megadrought-in-the-northern-southwest/, opened with curl 2026-09-27): "The single greatest megadrought recorded for North America occurred in the western half of the continent from A.D. 1140–1162. ## 02 · The 1500s

### The Luna colony and the hurricane of September 19 to 20, 1559 (story `tristan-de-luna-disasters`, marked verified: the facts below back it)
- **Main source:** John E. Worth (University of West Florida), "The Luna Expedition: An Overview from the Documents," paper for the Society for Historical Archaeology symposium "The Tristan de Luna Shipwrecks and Settlement (1559-1561)," 2017, https://pages.uwf.edu/jworth/WorthSHA2017.pdf (downloaded and read with pypdf 2026-09-27). Worth works from *The Luna Papers* (Priestley, reprint 2010).
  - The expedition "was the largest and most well-financed Spanish attempt to colonize southeastern North America up to that time."
  - People: "the 500-man army" plus "perhaps 1,000 additional people including servants, African slaves, a number of wives and children, and some 200 Aztec warriors and craftsmen enlisted from Mexico City itself." Four new ships were built at San Juan de Ulua (Veracruz).
  - "When the fleet of 11 ships and a 12th vessel in tow arrived in Pensacola Bay on August 15, they chose the high, level bluff of Emanuel Point," spent "the next five weeks offloading people and equipment and building Santa María de Ochuse," a town planned for 140 house-lots.
  - "The destruction of 7 ships and nearly all the expedition's food reserves in the hurricane of September 19-20, 1559." The survival phase lasted "some 19 months until mid-April of 1561."
  - Relief: one resupply from Havana right after the hurricane, then "four separate relief expeditions launched from Veracruz, arriving in Pensacola at 5-7 month intervals after the arrival of the first two relief ships three months after the hurricane."
  - "Temporary relocation of most of Luna's forces inland to the Native American village of Nanipacana along the Alabama River in central Alabama for just over 4 months between February and June of 1560," and "a 200-man detachment under Mateo del Sauz" to the chiefdom of Coosa in northwestern Georgia, April to November 1560.
  - "Some 100 people remained at the Pensacola settlement throughout Luna's inland stay, but after deaths as well as the evacuation of many survivors with the second relief expedition, just 362 souls remained at Ochuse by September of 1560, not counting the other 200 at Coosa."
  - Luna's late-summer 1560 order for "a detachment of 100 soldiers and 50 servants to travel inland from Ochuse to Coosa" on short rations: "the entire body of Luna's army rejected his ill-advised plan." The march never took place.
  - "Leading a small fleet to Pensacola by early April of 1561, Villafañe dismissed Luna to return to Spain, gathered most of the remaining 150 or so settlers at Ochuse, and departed for Havana, leaving only a detachment of 50 men." Two small frigates were lost "and all aboard in yet another storm off Cape Hatteras." The garrison was evacuated "in August of 1561." In February 1562 the viceroy "officially abandoned the effort."
- **Ships:** "Seven ships were lost as a result of the storm, including six that were grounded and sank, and one that was pushed a short distance inland." Lost: *Jesús* (flagship), *San Juan de Ulua*, *San Andrés*, *Sancti Espiritus*, *San Amaro*, *Santa María de Ayuda*, *Corpus Christi*. Survived: caravel *Santi Espiritus*, barks *San Luís Aragón* and *La Salvadora*. (John E. Worth, "Luna Expedition: Fleet," https://pages.uwf.edu/jworth/jw_spanfla_luna_fleet.html, opened 2026-09-27.)
- **Deaths and food:** John E. Worth, "Santa María de Ochuse, Spain's First Colonial Settlement on Pensacola Bay, Part 2: After the Hurricane," Luna Settlement Project blog, May 11, 2021 (http://lunasettlement.blogspot.com/2021/05/santa-maria-de-ochuse-spains-first_11.html, opened 2026-09-27): "loss of life seems to have been mostly limited to the sailors and passengers on the ships at anchor." The worst loss was "the food stores that were still on the ships that sank in the bay, leaving far too few provisions." Survivors built shelter, cleared debris and salvaged the wrecks. "Four companies (some 200 men) were dispatched inland" to find towns with surplus food. They found "a large Native town called Nanipacana in a province called Piachi, situated along the Alabama River some 40 leagues north." "As Native relations quickly worsened in the interior, many people sickened and died from malnourishment and even as a result of Native attacks."
- UWF news, "Archaeologists Gather to Discuss Luna Settlement, Wrecks" (https://news.uwf.edu/archaeologists-gather-discuss-luna-settlement-wrecks/, opened 2026-09-27): the hurricane struck "five weeks after the expedition arrived, sinking seven of the ships." "The Spaniards left all the food for 1,500 people on board the ships" because they thought the fleet safer than the land site. Settlement site identified December 2015, spread "across at least nine city blocks or 27 acres." Wrecks: Emanuel Point I (1992, Florida Bureau of Archaeological Research team led by Dr. Roger Smith), Emanuel Point II (2006, UWF), Emanuel Point III (June 2016, UWF field course).
- David H. Dye, "Luna Expedition," Tennessee Encyclopedia (https://tennesseeencyclopedia.net/entries/luna-expedition/, opened 2026-09-27): fleet anchored August 14, 1559. "The people of Nanipacana, however, fled their towns, destroying their stored food supplies as they left." **Date note:** Dye gives August 14 and "five days later" for the hurricane. Worth (the documents specialist) gives arrival August 15 and hurricane September 19 to 20, five weeks later. The outline follows Worth.
- Florida Historical Society, "Don Tristan de Luna," Florida Frontiers (https://myfloridahistory.org/frontiers/article/29, opened 2026-09-27): "The colonists were dispersed to Mexico, Cuba, and Spain."
- **Luna himself:** born in Borobia, Spain, to a noble family. Served with Coronado. Died September 16, 1573, in New Spain. (Wikipedia, "Tristán de Luna y Arellano," opened 2026-09-27.) Service with Coronado is also in UWF's Luna Settlement page.
- **The land:** "The name Ochuse had been the indigenous name of the bay, as recorded by Hernando de Soto's lieutenant Francisco Maldonado during his 1540 discovery of the Native American town and province by that name." (Worth, "Luna Settlement," https://pages.uwf.edu/jworth/jw_spanfla_luna_settlement.html, opened 2026-09-27.) Luna-era accounts of "a meager population of Indian fishermen" along the bay (unconfirmed: search summary only). Nanipacana was in the province of Piachi (Worth 2021).
- Florida would not see another Spanish Gulf Coast settlement attempt until 1693: hurricanescience.org says so, citing Wikipedia. Not used in the outline.

## 03 · The 1600s

### Jamestown: the drought, the Sea Venture hurricane and the Starving Time
- **Drought.** Stahle et al., *Science* 280 (1998), full citation under era 02: "the settlers of Jamestown Colony had the monumental bad luck to arrive in April 1607, during the driest 7-year period in 770 years." The drought ran 1606 to 1612, most severe "in the Tidewater region near Jamestown." "Only 38 of the 104 original settlers were still alive after the first year at Jamestown, and 4800 of the 6000 settlers sent to Jamestown between 1607 and 1625 died during this extraordinary period." "Malnutrition was a leading cause of death at Jamestown." "The native Algonquian people and the English domestic livestock also suffered during the years of heavy mortality among the colonists." "The lower James River is a brackish estuary, and there are archival references to foul drinking water and associated illnesses among the settlers, particularly before 1613," and "water quality is poorest during drought."
- **The land:** Jamestown was planted inside Tsenacomoco, the Powhatan paramount chiefdom (`research/research-big-business.md` era 03, citing Encyclopedia Virginia, and `research/research-native-nations.md` line 56).
- **The Sea Venture.** Lorri Glover, "Sea Venture," Encyclopedia Virginia, 2020-12-07 (https://encyclopediavirginia.org/entries/sea-venture/, opened with a script 2026-09-27):
  - "On June 2, 1609, the Virginia Company of London sent across the Atlantic Ocean the largest fleet England had ever amassed in the West: nine ships, 600 passengers, and livestock and provisions to last a year." The flagship carried captain Christopher Newport, admiral George Somers, the new governor Sir Thomas Gates "and 150 passengers and crew members."
  - "On July 24 the voyagers were within seven days of landfall when they were hit by a hurricane. The Sea Venture bore the brunt of the storm and was soon separated from the other ships." It "sprung a leak so severe" that a passenger wrote "we almost drowned within." The 150 "escaped death at sea but found themselves marooned on Bermuda. Before the ship sank, crewmen salvaged many of their supplies and even the rigging." They stayed ten months and built the *Patience* and *Deliverance*.
  - The other ships "arrived in Virginia sick, with damaged ships, having jettisoned many of their supplies." Timeline: "Late August 1609 ... eight of nine English ships ... arrive safely at Jamestown."
  - "The settlers in Virginia endured the 'Starving Time,' with a mortality rate of 70 percent and survivors resorting to cannibalism, raiding the graves of their fallen countrymen and Indians they had killed in warfare."
  - "May 24, 1610 ... arrives at Jamestown. They find only sixty survivors of a winter famine." Gates decided to abandon the colony and was met on the river by Thomas West, baron De La Warr, with a year's supply of food, "and so everyone returned to Jamestown."
  - Accounts by William Strachey (*A true repertory of the wracke*, published 1625), Silvester Jourdain and Robert Rich. Shakespeare's *The Tempest* drew partly on the story (first recorded performance November 1, 1611).
  - George Percy: "There were never Englishmen left in a forreigne Countrey in such miserie as wee were in this new discovered Virginia." "Half the colonists who arrived in April 1607 were dead by October, and fewer than forty survived the winter." (Glover's "fewer than forty" and Stahle's "38 of the 104" agree.)
- Starving Time numbers and Powhatan's siege: `research/research-food-farming.md` line 33 (about 500 at the start of winter 1609 to 1610, roughly 61 by spring, Powhatan cut off trade and besieged the fort, citing Historic Jamestowne, Encyclopedia Virginia, Britannica, Percy). `food-farming` leads on the Starving Time. This chapter tells the storm and the drought.

### The Great Colonial Hurricane, August 15, 1635 (old calendar)
- **William Bradford, *Of Plimoth Plantation*, entry for 1635** (Project Gutenberg eBook 24950, https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/24950/pg24950.txt, opened 2026-09-27): "This year, ye 14. or 15. of August (being Saturday) was such a mighty storme of wind & raine, as none living in these parts, either English or Indeans, ever saw." "It blew downe sundry houses, & uncovered others; diverce vessells were lost at sea." "It caused ye sea to swell (to ye southward of this place) above 20. foote, right up & downe, and made many of the Indeans to clime into trees for their saftie." It took the board roof off the trading house at Manamet. "It blew downe many hundered thowsands of trees, turning up the stronger by the roots." It lasted "not ... above 5. or 6. houers" at its worst.
- **Calendar note:** August 15, 1635 on the Julian calendar the colonists used is August 25, 1635 on today's Gregorian calendar, which fell on a Saturday (checked with Python's calendar, 2026-09-27), matching Bradford's "being Saturday." Wikipedia's reanalysis-based dates (Jamestown August 24, Long Island landfall early August 26) are Gregorian.
- Wikipedia, "Great Colonial Hurricane of 1635" (opened with a script 2026-09-27): estimated Category 4, surge 14 to 20 feet on south-facing coasts per Bradford and Winthrop, "46+ direct" deaths in its infobox. It says Bradford wrote that the storm "drowned seventeen Indians." **CORRECTION:** Bradford's own text (above) does not say this. It says Native people climbed into trees. Do not use "seventeen." The Wikipedia claim may come from Winthrop's journal, which was not opened. The Angel Gabriel wreck at Pemaquid: "Three or four passengers and one seaman perished" (E. O. Jameson, *The Cogswells in America*, 1884, quoted by Wikipedia). Wikipedia's figure of 23 aboard the Thacher pinnace matches Thacher's letter.
- New England Historical Society (as quoted in the Boyer transcription below): "Unknown numbers of Native Americans caught in the surge drowned." (Secondary. No count exists in the sources opened.)

### Anthony Thacher (story `anthony-thacher`, marked verified: his own letter backs it)
- **Primary:** Anthony Thacher's letter to his brother Peter, written days after the wreck, first printed in Increase Mather's *Remarkable Providences* (1684) and reprinted in Alexander Young's *Chronicles of the First Planters of the Colony of Massachusetts Bay* (1846). Read in a modernized transcription: J. F. Boyer, "Shipwreck off Cape Ann, August 14 1635" (https://jfboyer.net/wp-content/uploads/2022/07/Thacher-Shipwreck-Rev-3.pdf, 6 pages, read with pypdf 2026-09-27). The transcription modernizes spelling. Quotations in the prose should keep its wording or go back to Young.
  - "We embarked at Ipswich August 11, 1635 with our families, and substances, bound for Marblehead, we being in all 23 souls, viz. 11 in my cousin's family, seven in mine, and one Mr. William Elliot sometime of New Sarum and four mariners." His cousin was the minister (John) Avery, invited to be pastor at Marblehead. The people of Marblehead sent a pinnace (a small ship) for them.
  - "On August 14, 1635 about 10 at night having a fresh gale of wind, our sails being old and done, were split." "Before daylight it pleased the Lord to send so mighty a storm as the like was never known in New England since the English came, nor in the memory of the Indians." The anchor dragged ("came home") and the cable slipped away.
  - The pinnace was driven onto a rock "between two high rocks." A wave washed Thacher onto the rock with his daughter Mary, and Avery and his eldest son joined them. Another wave broke the pinnace "all to pieces" and carried "my wife away in the scuttle as she was, with the greater part of the quarter deck unto the shore." "All the rest that were in the barque were drowned." The next wave swept the four off the rock.
  - He was in the water "a quarter of an hour at least" and crawled ashore. His children: "My little babe (ah poor Peter) sitting in his sister Edith's arms," "my poor William," and his oldest daughter Mary on the rock.
  - Ashore he found "a knapsack ... in which I had a steel and flint and powder-horn," "a drowned goat," "a hat and my son William's coat," and "two cheeses and some butter." The gunpowder was dry, he struck a fire with a piece of his wife's neck cloth, and they boiled some of the goat in a small brass pot. "Our drink was brackish water. Bread we had none."
  - "There we remained till Monday following when about three of the clock in the afternoon, in a boat that came that way, we went off that desolate island, which I named after my name, 'Thatchers Woe' and the rock 'Avery's Fall'." "In the isle lieth buried the body of my cousin's eldest daughter, who I found dead on the shore." They reached Marblehead on the Tuesday.
  - Massachusetts colonial records as given in the same transcription: September 3, 1635, "40 marks given to Mr. Thatcher out of the treasury towards his great losses." March 9, 1636/37: "Mr. Anthony had granted to him the small island at the head of Cape Ann (upon which he was preserved from shipwreck) as his proper inheritance." Winthrop's Journal: the General Court gave him money "and divers good people gave him besides."
- **Secondary:** Gordon Harris, "Wreck of the Watch and Wait, August 24, 1635," Historic Ipswich, August 15, 2025 (https://historicipswich.net/2025/08/15/wreck-of-the-watch-and-wait-august-24-1635/, opened with a script 2026-09-27): the boat was the *Watch and Wait*, left Ipswich "August 21, 1635" (new-style date), wrecked off Rockport "on what is known as Crackwood's Ledge." "Mr. and Mrs. Thatcher were the only survivors." **Disagreements to record:** Harris says "his wife and six children" for both families and "21 passengers." Thacher's own letter gives 23 people, 11 in Avery's family and 7 in his own, and names four of his children (Mary, Edith, William, Peter). Vintagerockport.com (search summary only) says 21 other passengers "including his four children." Follow the letter: 23 aboard, 2 survived, 21 died. Wikipedia names the owner of the boat as Isaac Allerton.
- **His wife's name** is not in the letter or the sources opened. Genealogies give Elizabeth (not opened). The outline leaves her unnamed.
- **Movie:** none found. John Greenleaf Whittier's poem "The Swan Song of Parson Avery" is based on Thacher's account (Wikipedia).

### The earthquake of June 1, 1638
- Bradford, *Of Plimoth Plantation*, 1638 entry (Gutenberg, above): "This year, aboute ye 1. or 2. of June, was a great & fearfull earthquake; it was in this place heard before it was felte. It came with a rumbling noyse, or low murmure, like unto remoate thunder." It "caused platters, dishes, & such like things as stoode upon shelves, to clatter & fall downe." People outdoors "could not stand without catching hould of ye posts & pails." A second, weaker shock came about half an hour later. "Ye Indeans felt it within land; and some ships that were upon ye coast were shaken by it." Bradford read the shaking as "ye signes of his [God's] displeasure" and noted that for years after "ye somers ... were not so hotte & seasonable for ye ripning of corne," but left the cause "to naturallists to judge." No deaths recorded.
