## 01 Before 1500

### Cahokia — the planned city (urban-form angle; `native-nations` leads the nation story)
- Built up rapidly from ~1050 CE; peak ~1100 with roughly 10,000–20,000 residents in the core — about London's size then; metropolitan-area estimates run higher (one to 50,000 — state as the high end of estimates, not settled). Dispersed by ~1350. (Sources: UNESCO World Heritage listing "Cahokia Mounds State Historic Site"; Illinois DNR Historic Preservation; National Geographic. Population figures match the `native-nations` outline — keep the two chapters consistent.)
- **Monks Mound:** largest **prehistoric** earthen structure in the Americas; built in stages (UNESCO says 14 construction stages), four terraces, ~30 m (~100 ft) tall, base ~6 hectares (~14 acres). (UNESCO; Cahokia Mounds SHS.)
  - ⚠ **The word "prehistoric" is load-bearing — never drop it.** Without it the claim is false: modern earthmoving machinery has built far larger earthen structures on this continent. Monks Mound holds about **814,000 cubic yards** of earth (Cahokia Mounds SHS, "Mound 38 – Monks Mound"); Fort Peck Dam in Montana, finished 1940, holds about **125.6 million cubic yards** of fill (ASCE Historic Civil Engineering Landmark listing) — roughly 150 times the volume. Cahokia Mounds SHS's own wording is "the largest prehistoric earthen structure in the Western Hemisphere." A 2026-09-07 verifier pass found the qualifier dropped in `outlines/city-building.md` and sharpened to "ever built" in the prose. Keep "prehistoric," or say plainly instead that every basketload was carried by hand.
- **Grand Plaza:** ~50 acres at the mound's base, deliberately leveled and filled by hand for ceremonies and the game of chunkey. (Cahokia Mounds SHS; Library of History/Illinois DNR summaries.)
- **Woodhenge:** five successive circles of red cedar posts west of Monks Mound; posts align with sunrise at solstices and equinoxes — a working solar calendar in the city plan. (Cahokia Mounds SHS; Wikipedia "Cahokia Woodhenge" corroborated by site literature.)
- **Palisade:** ~2-mile stockade around the central precinct, begun ~1100, rebuilt three times over ~200 years, each build needing 15,000–20,000 oak/hickory logs ~1 ft diameter, ~20 ft tall. (Cahokia Mounds SHS.)
- No named individuals survive — say so plainly.

### Chaco Canyon and Mesa Verde
- Chaco (NW New Mexico): center of Ancestral Puebloan world ~900–1180 CE. Pueblo Bonito: 650+ rooms. (NPS "Pueblo Bonito"; EBSCO research starter "Chaco Canyon.")
  - ⚠ **Two different dates, two different things — do not let them collide.** `~900–1180` is the span of the **great-house era**: the period during which people were building and using the big masonry great houses in the canyon. `~1140s` is when people **began leaving** the canyon in numbers; the last construction and occupation trail off to about 1180–1200. Both are correct and they do not contradict each other. A 2026-09-07 verifier pass found the finished prose carrying both bare ("between about 900 and about 1180" and, four paragraphs later, "People left Chaco in the 1140s") with nothing saying which was which, which reads as a self-contradiction. **Whenever either date is written, say what it measures.**
- Chacoan roads: engineered, remarkably straight, averaging ~9 m (~30 ft) wide; network connects outlying communities across the Four Corners. (Roads of Chaco literature; Science Moab; New Mexico Magazine; 2025 lidar studies of a solstice-aligned road — Archaeology Magazine, Feb 2025.)
- Mesa Verde: Cliff Palace, largest cliff dwelling — 150 rooms, 23 kivas, built 1190–1280, housed 100+ people. (Colorado Encyclopedia; Mesa Verde NP visitor literature.)
- After dispersal — people **began leaving** Chaco in the ~1140s, and had left Mesa Verde by the ~1280s — people regathered into Rio Grande pueblos; continuity, not disappearance (`native-nations` leads).

### PATCH 2026-10-01 (T-454): Cahokia's location and rank; Mesa Verde's setting
- Cahokia Mounds State Historic Site lies in southwestern Illinois, between East St. Louis and Collinsville, directly across the Mississippi River from present-day St. Louis. UNESCO's listing calls it the largest pre-Columbian settlement north of Mexico. (Wikipedia "Cahokia"; UNESCO World Heritage listing 198 via search summary; cahokiamounds.org "About": "located a few miles west of Collinsville, Illinois.")
- Mesa Verde National Park is in southwestern Colorado, 10 miles east of Cortez. The Ancestral Puebloans built its cliff dwellings of sandstone blocks and mud mortar in the sandstone alcoves (hollows under overhanging cliff rock), mostly from the late 1190s to the late 1270s. (NPS "Colorado: Mesa Verde National Park"; Smithsonian Magazine "In the Cliffs of Mesa Verde".)
- "Pueblo" is the Spanish word for a town or village; Spanish colonists used it for the stone and adobe towns of the Southwest and the people who live in them. (Britannica "Pueblo Indians".)

## 02 The 1500s

### St. Augustine and the Laws of the Indies
- Founded September 1565 by Pedro Menéndez de Avilés at the Timucua village site of Seloy; oldest continuously occupied European-founded city in the continental U.S. (City of St. Augustine "Our History"; Smithsonian Magazine; NPS.)
- **Laws of the Indies, 1573:** King Philip II's ordinances standardizing colonial town planning — central plaza "laid to the compass points," length 1.5× width; grid of streets from the plaza; lots designated for church/government; among the first standardized urban-planning codes anywhere. (ArchDaily on the Laws of the Indies; Colonial Havana scholarship; scholarly literature.)
- **Plaza de la Constitución:** laid out under the 1573 royal ordinances — oldest public square in the U.S.; government buildings and church fronted it; market established 1598; National Historic Landmark (Town Plan) 1970; name honors the Spanish Constitution of 1812. (Historical Marker Database; Florida's Historic Coast; Visit St. Augustine.)
- ⚠ **Two specifics that this bank does NOT support — removed from outline and prose 2026-09-07, do not put them back without a source.**
  1. **"a printed rulebook."** The 1573 document was a set of royal ordinances issued by Philip II, not a printed book. The printed compilation of colonial law, the *Recopilación de Leyes de los Reynos de las Indias*, was not published until 1681 — a century later. Write "ordinances," "written rules," or "a written order with the force of law."
  2. **"the same plaza-and-grid pattern for the next 250 years."** No source in this bank carries a 250-year span, and none was found on a 2026-09-07 search. What is sourced is that the ordinances were the basis of grid-and-plaza town layouts across Spanish America and what is now the U.S. Southwest (ArchDaily; Colonial Havana scholarship). Say that; do not attach a number to it.
- Era is thin: one lasting town, no documented town-builder life at story level. Honest sentence, move on.

### PATCH 2026-10-01 (T-454): Seloy and its people, 1565-1566
- Menéndez landed at Seloy's town on September 8, 1565, with about 800 colonists (including 26 women and an unknown number of enslaved Africans). The colonists made their first settlement in Seloy's town and used the chief Seloy's council house as their first fort. Timucua council houses were round, thatched and very large, able to hold up to a thousand people. (Florida Museum of Natural History, "First Contacts," St. Augustine timeline.)
- Relations broke down quickly. The Timucua made repeated attacks to drive the Spaniards away, the fort was "half burned" on April 1, 1566, with all supplies lost (the timeline does not say who set the fire), and in May 1566, about nine months after landing, Menéndez moved the settlement and fort across the bay to Anastasia Island. The Spaniards returned to the mainland, to the site of today's St. Augustine, by about 1572. (Florida Museum of Natural History, St. Augustine Menéndez timeline and "Archaeologists upload the history of North America's oldest colonial city"; Wikipedia "Anastasia Island".)
- "The Indies" (las Indias) was the Spanish crown's name for its lands in the Americas; the Laws of the Indies were the laws for those lands. (Wikipedia "Laws of the Indies"; ArchDaily on the Laws of the Indies, already cited above.)

## 03 The 1600s

### Fort towns, walls, watchmen
- **Jamestown:** triangular James Fort 1607; in the 1620s surveyor William Claiborne laid out "New Towne" east of the fort; row-house foundations excavated there. (NPS Historic Jamestowne "New Towne"; NPS Colonial NHP.)
- **Santa Fe:** founded ~1610 by Governor Pedro de Peralta as Villa Real de la Santa Fé; planned around a central plaza per Spanish practice; Palace of the Governors = longest continuously occupied public building in the U.S. (Britannica "Santa Fe"; EBSCO research starter; santafe.org history.)
- **Boston:** founded/named 1630; no plan — streets formed around the Trimountain's hills, brooks, and marshes near the waterfront. The "cow paths" explanation is a myth, traceable to Ralph Waldo Emerson (1860). (Boston Magazine "Are Boston's Streets Really Paved Over Cow Paths?" 2018; Boston.com; celebrateboston.com.)
- **Boston night watch:** established 1631 (watch to patrol after dark; precursor of the Boston Police). (Wikipedia "Boston Watch" corroborated by Boston PD history pages.)
- **New Amsterdam wall:** built 1653 along the town's northern edge against an English threat — 5–6 ft high plank wall, river to river; the street along it became Wall Street. (NYC Dept. of Records & Information Services; Lower Manhattan Historical Association; New Netherland Institute; MAAP/Columbia.)
  - **Who ordered it:** Director General Petrus Stuyvesant with the Court of Burgomasters and Schepens resolved on **March 13, 1653** to build it, after news of English military preparations in New England. Cost reckoned at **3,166 guilders**, raised by contributions from named citizens. Reported complete by **July 28, 1653**. (NYC Dept. of Records & Information Services, "The Dutch & the English, Part 1: Good Fences," quoting the court minutes.)
  - **Who did the work, from the order itself:** "the soldiers and other servants of the Company, together with the free Negroes, no one excepted, shall complete the work on the fort by constructing a breastwork," and "the citizens without exception, shall work on the constructions… by immediately digging a ditch from the East River to the North River." (Same court minutes, via NYC Dept. of Records.) MAAP/Columbia's account: "A gang of black men labored as long as daylight allowed, digging a three-foot-deep trench from the East River all the way across Manhattan Island to the Hudson River," standing logs 18 inches around and 12 feet long into it and packing dirt and stone around each. MAAP names men who dug it — **Paulo d'Angola, Simon Congo, Anthony Portuguese** — and states they "were owned by the Dutch West India Company, but some had gained a form of half-freedom."
  - **Who held them — say this, it is the mechanism:** the **Dutch West India Company** owned the enslaved Africans of New Amsterdam. On **February 25, 1644**, after 18 or 19 years of service, the Company's Director and Council granted "half-freedom" to eleven men and their wives — among them **Paulo Angola, Sijmon Congo and Antonij Portugies**. They could hold land, but **their children, "at present born or yet to be born," stayed the Company's property**, they owed the Company an annual payment that Dutch colonists did not owe, and they had to work for the Company whenever it called them. (New Netherland Institute, "Half-Freedom"; DPLA transcript of the 1644 ordinance; MAAP/Columbia.) So the 1653 order's phrase "free Negroes" means men under exactly that arrangement.
  - ⚠ **Agency warning (style guide §1.1 / policy §6b).** A 2026-09-07 verifier pass found the finished prose reading "the Dutch colonists … built a wall … Enslaved and free Africans did much of the work of building it" — the colonists as the builders, the enslaved workers arriving as a correction, and no one named as holding them. **Put the people who did the work in the subject of the sentence, and name Stuyvesant and the Company.** Do not write "New Amsterdam built a wall": a town cannot build anything.
- **Rattle watch:** August 12, 1658 — paid night patrol with wooden rattles in New Amsterdam; among the first public police forces in America. (NYC records; Washington Examiner crime-history column — popular but consistent with NYPD histories.)

### Penn's Philadelphia (dated 1682–83 → this era by the boundary rule)
- Penn appointed **Thomas Holme** surveyor-general 1682; Holme laid out the grid December 1682; his map "A Portraiture of the City of Philadelphia" published 1683 — promotional as well as practical. (Historical Society of Pennsylvania; explorePAhistory Holme marker; TCLF "William Penn's Philadelphia Plan.")
- Plan: grid between Delaware and Schuylkill; two wide main streets (Broad and High/Market); one large central square + four quadrant squares — today Logan, Franklin, Washington, Rittenhouse; City Hall sits on Centre Square. Width of streets partly a fire lesson from London 1666. (Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia "Green Country Town"; PhillyVoice on the squares.)
- Penn's phrase, documented: "a greene Country Towne which will never be burnt, and always be wholesome." (Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia, quoting Penn's instructions.)
- **Movie (verified):** *Penn of Pennsylvania* (UK, 1941; dir. Lance Comfort; Clifford Evans as Penn, with Deborah Kerr) — U.S. release title *The Courageous Mr. Penn* (1942). (Wikipedia; IMDb tt0033493; MUBI.) Biopic of Penn generally, not the city plan specifically — noted.

### PATCH 2026-10-01 (T-454): whose land, and details for the 1600s towns
- **Jamestown.** The English built James Fort on Jamestown Island, on the James River, inside the territory of the Paspahegh, a people of the Powhatan paramount chiefdom (Tsenacommacah); the Paspahegh used the island as hunting ground and their nearest town was about 4 miles away. The Paspahegh attacked the new settlement on May 26, 1607. George Percy: "the fifteenth of June, we had built and finished our Fort, which was triangle wise." The fort's wall was "a pallazado or stockade made of upright oak and poplar logs that were about 14 feet high." (NPS, Captain John Smith Chesapeake NHT, "Historic Jamestowne"; Historic Jamestowne / Jamestown Rediscovery "Fort Site"; Wikipedia "Jamestown Island".)
- **Santa Fe.** Peralta and his men laid out Santa Fe at the base of the Sangre de Cristo Mountains "on the site of the ancient Pueblo Indian ruin of O'Ga P'Ogeh" (Tewa: "White Shell Water Place"). The Palace of the Governors stands on the Santa Fe Plaza; in the 1680 Pueblo Revolt the Pueblo people burned most of Santa Fe's buildings but not the Palace. (santafe.org "Santa Fe History"; Wikipedia "Palace of the Governors".)
- **Boston.** The Massachusett people called the peninsula Shawmut. The Trimountain was the largest of its hills, a steep mass with three summits, today's Beacon Hill. (Wikipedia "Shawmut Peninsula"; bostonbasinhills.org.) Thomas Dudley, letter to the Countess of Lincoln, March 1631: "Wee have ordered that noe man shall build his chimney with wood nor cover his house with thatch, which was readily assented unto, for that divers houses have been burned since our arrival (the fire always beginning in the wooden chimneys)." (George Francis Dow, *Every Day Life in the Massachusetts Bay Colony*, 1935, Project Gutenberg.) The night watch: "The people of the town of Boston established a Watch in 1631"; watchmen "patrolled the streets of Boston at night to protect the public from criminals, wild animals, and fire"; the Town Meeting took control of the Watch in 1636. (City of Boston, "Brief history of the Boston Police".)
- **New Amsterdam.** Manhattan was part of Lenapehoking, the homeland of the Lenape people. New Amsterdam stood at the island's southern tip, around Fort Amsterdam (today's Bowling Green area). (Smithsonian NMAI "Lenape (Delaware) Homeland" and "The People of Native New York"; Smithsonian NMAH "Dutch New Amsterdam".) Half-freedom, 1644: the eleven men "petitioned the Council to be freed from their bondage after having served the Company for 18 or 19 years," saying the Company had promised them freedom; the Company gave them plots of land north of New Amsterdam; their yearly dues were 30 skepels (a Dutch measure of dry goods) of grain or beans and one hog. (New Netherland Institute, "Half-Freedom".) Willem Kieft was the Company's director of New Netherland from 1638 to 1647, so the 1644 grant was made by Kieft and his council. (Wikipedia "Willem Kieft"; New Netherland Institute.) No source found that half-freedom gave the right to go to court: SEARCHED, NOT FOUND.
- **Philadelphia.** About 2,500 Lenape and about 3,000 Europeans (Dutch, Swedes, Finns and the English of West New Jersey) lived on the two sides of the Delaware River when Penn's colonists arrived. Penn bought land in southeastern Pennsylvania from the Lenape in 1682-1684, paying at least £1,200 in goods. Penn himself landed on October 28, 1682. "William Penn (1644-1718), gentleman and Quaker." (Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia, "Founding of Pennsylvania".) Quakers are the Religious Society of Friends, a Christian group that began in England in the 1640s-1650s with the preaching of George Fox. (Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia, "Quakers (Religious Society of Friends)".) "Penn's concept set a precedent for planning in many early American cities." (TCLF, "William Penn's Philadelphia Plan".) No source found for a "two hundred years" span of copying: not used.

## 04 1700 to 1750

### Colonial port cities ~1750
- Populations ~1750: Philadelphia ~13,000; Boston ~12,000; New York ~11,000; Charleston ~8,000 (Charleston figure is a 1760 count in some sources — "about 8,000" is safe). Boston was the busiest port before ~1750; New York and Philadelphia passed it after. (Edmaps colonial population maps; History in Charts "Largest Cities in the American Colonies"; Census Bureau history pages.)

### Franklin's Union Fire Company
- **December 7, 1736** — Franklin and fellow citizens sign the Articles of the Union Fire Company, Philadelphia — first formally organized volunteer company in the colonies that protected the whole community (Boston's earlier Mutual Fire Societies protected members only). About 25–26 founding members (sources say 25 co-founders/26 members; state "about 25"). Each member: six leather buckets, two stout linen bags, answer every alarm. (Founders Online, "Articles of the Union Fire Company, 7 December 1736" — primary; USHistory.org; Historical Society of Pennsylvania blog; Benjamin Franklin Historical Society.)
- Franklin's other civic-improvement work (street paving, lighting, the watch) is in his *Autobiography* — not separately verified this pass; the fire company carries the story. Keep the invention angle (`technology`) and press angle (`news-communication`) out.

### PATCH 2026-10-01 (T-454): London's size, Franklin's 1735 letter, the bags, and what came after
- London grew from about 80,000 people in 1550 to over 700,000 by 1750. (Cambridge Group for the History of Population and Social Structure, "Early modern London" project page.)
- Franklin wrote an unsigned letter, "On Protection of Towns from Fire," to his own newspaper, the *Pennsylvania Gazette*, published February 4, 1735. It opens with the advice "an Ounce of Prevention is worth a Pound of Cure," about carrying live coals from room to room. The Union Fire Company followed in December 1736. (Founders Online, "On Protection of Towns from Fire, 4 February 1735"; USHistory.org "Union Fire Company"; Wikipedia "Union Fire Company".)
- The members' gear: "leather buckets, with strong bags and baskets (for packing and transporting goods), which were to be brought to every fire." After the Union, Philadelphians set up the Heart-in-Hand, the Britannia, the Fellowship and several other fire companies, which Franklin encouraged. (USHistory.org, "Franklin's Philadelphia: Union Fire Company".)
- Boston's October 2, 1711 fire "destroyed major buildings and left many families homeless"; "The first Board of Fire Wards would be established a few months later, with 12 men appointed to the board" (the guide does not say who appointed them or list their duties). (Boston Public Library, "Great Fires of Boston" guide.)
- Volunteer companies did the firefighting in American towns until April 1, 1853, when Cincinnati set up the first fully paid, professional fire department in the United States; Miles Greenwood, co-inventor of an early steam fire engine, was its first chief. (Cincinnati Fire Museum; Ohio.org "1st in the Nation"; Wikipedia "Cincinnati Fire Department".)

## 05 1750 to 1800

### The land the capital was built on — ADDED 2026-09-07 (the "empty riverbank" erasure)

**Why this section exists.** Until 2026-09-07 the outline read "it designed its capital city from nothing, on an empty riverbank" and the prose read "on an empty riverbank, before anybody lived there." Both are false, and both are the "empty land" move `control/hard-subjects-policy.md` forbids — the same erasure already corrected once in this chapter at the 1889 Oklahoma land run, and a third time at Oak Ridge (see era 08). The file even contradicted itself: Banneker's own survey camp at **Jones Point is inside Alexandria**, a town that had stood there for forty-two years. Everything below is sourced per fact. Use it; do not write around it.

**Who chose the site, and under what law**
- **Residence Act signed July 16, 1790.** It fixed the capital on the Potomac between the Eastern Branch (the Anacostia) and the Conococheague, in an area "not exceeding ten miles square," and gave the President the power to fix the exact location and to appoint three commissioners and a surveyor. (Library of Congress research guide, "Residence Act: Primary Documents in American History"; Wikipedia "Residence Act" corroborating the statutory text.)
- **George Washington chose the ground himself.** He rode the country, settled on the stretch at and next to Georgetown, and told Congress on **January 24, 1791**. Congress amended the act on **March 3, 1791** so the district could reach south of the Eastern Branch and take in Alexandria. Washington's proclamation of **March 30, 1791** fixed "Jones's point, the upper cape of Hunting Creek in Virginia" as the survey's starting point. (Wikipedia "Residence Act," citing the proclamation; consistent with NPS and boundarystones.org.)
- ⚠ **Agency note.** "Congress picked the land" and "the country designed its capital" are both §1.1 failures and both inaccurate. Congress passed a law setting a stretch of river; **Washington** picked the exact ground; **Andrew Ellicott** and his crew, Banneker among them, measured it; **L'Enfant** drew the city. Name them.

**Who was already there — Native people**
- Three Native villages stood in what became Washington. The largest was **Nacotchtank** (the name behind "Anacostia"), an Algonquian-speaking town on the southeast side of the Anacostia River, in the area between today's Bolling/Joint Base Anacostia-Bolling and Anacostia Park; **Nameroughquena** stood on the Potomac's west bank opposite today's Theodore Roosevelt Island; a third, unnamed, stood on a bluff in the northwest between today's C&O Canal and MacArthur Boulevard. (NPS, "Native Peoples of Washington, DC.")
- Captain John Smith reached the head of Potomac navigation in **1608**. NPS: "After only 40 years of contact with the Europeans, the population of local American Indians was only one-quarter of those that lived in the region prior to 1608" — deaths from introduced diseases and in wars, with others joining nations to the north, south and west. (NPS, same page.) Histories of the National Mall puts it: the "Native American communities that occupied those lands in centuries past had been driven away by war and disease."

**Two towns already standing inside the ten-mile square**
- **Alexandria, Virginia — 1749.** Established by act of the Virginia General Assembly; county surveyor John West laid out the first 60 acres; it grew up around **Hugh West's tobacco warehouse**, in place since 1732. Virginia ceded Alexandria into the ten-mile District of Columbia; it stayed federal until it was **retroceded to Virginia in 1847**. (City of Alexandria, "A Brief History of Alexandria.")
- **Georgetown, Maryland — 1751.** The Maryland General Assembly authorized the town and bought **60 acres from George Gordon and George Beall for £280**; the site already had a tobacco inspection house, and Georgetown grew through the next half-century as a tobacco port. (Georgetown Historic District National Register documentation, period of significance beginning 1751; EBSCO research starter "History of Georgetown.")
- Both stood **inside the district**. Georgetown sat inside the ten-mile square but outside the boundary of L'Enfant's City of Washington. **Banneker's survey base camp at Jones Point is at Alexandria** — the chapter's own story already places a named person in one of these towns.
- Two more towns had been platted on paper on ground the new city took in: **Hamburg** (laid out 1765 by Jacob Funk, east of Rock Creek) and **Carrollsburg** (laid out 1770 by Charles Carroll, at Buzzard's Point). Both were **barely built on** — say "laid out on paper and hardly built," not "towns," so the sentence stays true.

**The rest of it was farms — tobacco, and enslaved people working it**
- The plots that became the National Mall were owned in 1790 by five men — **Daniel Carroll of Duddington, David Burnes, Notley Young, Benjamin Oden and Samuel Davidson** — later called the **Original Proprietors**. Histories of the National Mall: the "Europeans who took over that land owned slaves who raised tobacco, corn, wheat, and cattle." (Histories of the National Mall, RRCHNM/George Mason University, "Who owned the Mall land in 1790?")
- **Notley Young (1736–1802)** held the largest of them: an **800-acre tobacco plantation** running to the Potomac near today's Jefferson Memorial, with his two-story house, outbuildings, fences, gardens, and quarters for the people he enslaved. **The count differs between sources — state the range, do not pick one silently:** the Smithsonian's National Museum of African American History and Culture, whose own five-acre site was part of that plantation, gives **245 people enslaved there in the U.S. Census of 1790**; Histories of the National Mall gives **260**. The government bought Young's land in **1791**. (NMAAHC museum-site history; Histories of the National Mall.)
- **District-wide, at the first census after the city opened:** the 1800 census counted **14,093 people in the District of Columbia**, of whom African Americans were **25 percent**, most of them enslaved. (Government of the District of Columbia, emancipation.dc.gov, "Ending Slavery in the District of Columbia.") A commonly reproduced breakdown of the same count gives 10,066 white, 783 free Black and 3,244 enslaved — attributed to the National Archives' District of Columbia census tables; **not read directly this pass, so use the 14,093 / 25 percent pair in prose** unless someone verifies the breakdown.
- ⚠ **Not verified this pass — do not put in prose yet.** A White House Historical Association page ("Before the White House") is reported to say that **David Burnes**, who farmed the ground the White House and Treasury stand on, sold eight of the twelve people he enslaved to cover debts before the city lots went on sale. whitehousehistory.org returned HTTP 403 to this pass, so this comes from a search summary, not from the page. Verify against the page before using it.

### L'Enfant and the capital
- 1791: Pierre (Peter) Charles L'Enfant, French-born engineer, designs the federal city plan — grid overlaid with grand diagonal avenues meeting in circles/squares. (L'Enfant Plan literature; NPS.)
- **Dismissal:** February 1792 — L'Enfant refused to furnish his original plan for engraving and clashed with the commissioners; Washington dismissed him for insubordination (Washington–Jefferson correspondence, Feb 1792, founders.archives.gov). **Andrew Ellicott**, aided by his brother Benjamin, reconstructed and revised the plan (straightened Massachusetts Avenue, removed some squares); the Thackara & Vallance engraving of March 1792 was the first widely circulated plan and the basis of the built city. (Founders Online; Wikipedia "L'Enfant Plan" corroborated by Library of Congress map notes.)
- L'Enfant died poor 1825; reinterred at Arlington National Cemetery 1909 overlooking the city. (NPS; standard biographies.)

### Banneker — the real role (myth cleared)
- **Verified role:** Benjamin Banneker, free Black tobacco farmer and self-taught astronomer, hired on George Ellicott's recommendation as scientific assistant to Major Andrew Ellicott for the **boundary survey** of the 10-mile square, **February–April 1791**. He made the astronomical observations and calculations to fix the south corner at Jones Point (maintained the regulator clock, observed by night); paid $2/day (Ellicott got $5). Left the survey in spring 1791, aged ~59 and in poor health, before the full 40-mile boundary was run.
  - ⚠ **On the two pay figures: $2 a day was an ordinary rate for an assistant. That is a RATE, not a differential.** Nothing in this bank says a $3 gap, or a $5 figure, was the standard difference between a surveyor and his assistant. A 2026-09-07 verifier pass found the prose had turned the rate into a reassurance — "$5, which was the ordinary difference between a surveyor and his assistant." Write the two rates and stop. (NPS "Benjamin Banneker and the Boundary Stones of the District of Columbia"; Records of the Columbia Historical Society, Silvio Bedini, 1969 — boundarystones.org PDF; White House Historical Association; National Archives Pieces of History blog.)
- **The myth, stated honestly:** the popular claim that Banneker reproduced L'Enfant's city plan from memory after the firing is not supported — Banneker had left the project ~10 months before L'Enfant's Feb 1792 dismissal, and it was Andrew Ellicott who reconstructed the plan. Historians (Bedini, the NPS, LOC's law-library blog) credit Banneker with the boundary-survey astronomy. Do not repeat the memory story as fact; name it as legend.
- Banneker's almanacs (from 1792) and his 1791 letter to Jefferson → `science` and `slavery-freedom` angles.

### Philadelphia's waterworks

**The 1793 yellow fever epidemic — scale and course. ADDED 2026-09-07.** The bank previously carried only "yellow fever epidemics 1793 and 1798," which let the finished prose shrink to "Yellow fever killed people in Philadelphia in 1793" — softer than the outline's own "killed thousands." Numbers and the bodily course now sit here so nobody has to guess.
- **About 5,000 people died between August 1 and November 9, 1793**, in a city of roughly **50,000** — about **one person in ten**. (Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia, "Yellow Fever," "estimated 5,000 lives"; Wikipedia "1793 Philadelphia yellow fever epidemic," giving the Aug 1–Nov 9 window and the ~10 percent figure.)
- **About 20,000 people fled the city** by the end of September, the federal government among them. (Wikipedia, same article; History.com.)
- **Benjamin Rush warned the mayor on August 21, 1793** that an epidemic was under way; daily burials passed twenty by late August and forty by the second week of September. (History.com, "Yellow fever breaks out in Philadelphia"; Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia.)
- **What it does to a person — required by `hard-subjects-policy.md` §3b (clinical-word rule).** Yellow fever is a virus carried by mosquitoes (*Aedes*). It starts as fever, chills, headache, body aches and vomiting. In about one case in seven the fever eases and then returns worse. In that severe stage the virus kills liver cells; with the liver failing, the skin and the whites of the eyes turn yellow, which is what jaundice means and what gives the disease its name. Bleeding starts — from the mouth, the nose, the eyes and into the stomach — and blood mixed with stomach acid is vomited up black, which is why people of the time called it the black vomit. The kidneys fail too. **Among those who reach the severe stage, 30 to 60 percent die.** (CDC, "Symptoms of Yellow Fever"; WHO yellow fever fact sheet; Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia on the "coffee grounds" or "black vomit.")
- **1798:** the fever returned to Philadelphia. **No Philadelphia-specific death toll for 1798 was found in a 2026-09-07 search** — the Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia names the year without a count. **Do not attach a number to 1798.** Say it came back.

### PATCH 2026-10-01 (T-454): the landowners' deal, L'Enfant's reburial, Centre Square, yellow fever recovery
- **The 1791 deal with the landowners.** Washington met the landowners at a dinner in Georgetown and the agreement was signed on March 30, 1791. The owners pooled about 6,000 acres. ### PATCH 2026-10-01 (T-454): whose land, and how the 1800-1850 cities were built
- **Rochester.** The land was Seneca land, part of the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) Confederacy. In 1788 the Seneca ceded about 2,600,000 acres east of the Genesee River to the land dealers Oliver Phelps and Nathaniel Gorham, and granted 200,000 more acres west of the river for a mill yard on condition that a mill be built for their use; Ebenezer Allen built a sawmill and gristmill on the 100-acre tract there in 1789. (Wikipedia "Phelps and Gorham Purchase"; HMDB "The 100 Acre Tract" marker; Rochester Historical Society, "Historic Aspects of the Phelps and Gorham Treaty", 1939.) The Genesee River's falls powered the mills, grain grown in the Genesee Valley came to them, and the canal carried the flour away; before the canal it cost $100 to ship a ton of flour from Rochester, and the canal cut that to $7 a ton. (WHEC-TV, "How the Erie Canal made Rochester the Flour City" [journalism]; Wikipedia "History of Rochester, New York".)
- **Chicago.** The town grew where the Chicago River entered Lake Michigan, beside Fort Dearborn (built 1803 at the river's mouth) and near the Chicago Portage to the Mississippi basin. In the Treaty of Chicago, September 26, 1833, Potawatomi leaders ceded their last lands in Illinois and Wisconsin and their last reservations in Michigan, about 5,000,000 acres, and agreed to move west of the Mississippi. (Encyclopedia of Chicago "Treaties"; Citizen Potawatomi Nation, "September 26, 1833 Treaty of Chicago"; Wikipedia "1833 Treaty of Chicago".) The ground: "Low, flat, and on a clay and loam soil that absorbed little moisture, Chicago lacked natural means of drainage"; for most of the year the streets were "quagmires of mud and water." The streets were raised in two stages, 1855-56 and 1857-58, by an average of four to five feet and in places as much as eight. (Encyclopedia of Chicago "Street Grades, Raising" via search summary; Wikipedia "Raising of Chicago": standing water, typhoid, dysentery, an 1854 cholera outbreak that killed six percent of the people; drains laid and covered with "several feet of soil"; unraised buildings ended up below the street, their ground floors becoming basements; in raised buildings "people came, went, shopped and worked in them" during the work.)
- **San Francisco.** The peninsula is the homeland of the Yelamu, a Ramaytush Ohlone people (Mission San Francisco de Asís was founded in their homeland; see `research-religion.md` era 05, MPDF Table 3). Between December 1849 and June 1851 six great fires burned San Francisco, the May 3-4, 1851 fire destroying as much as three-quarters of the town; people rebuilt after each. (Wikipedia "San Francisco Fire of 1851"; San Francisco Chronicle vault, "When San Francisco burned down six times in a year and a half" [journalism]; Wikipedia "Yelamu".)
- **The 1811 grid on the ground.** The plan covered Manhattan above Houston Street and below 155th Street. Much of that land was "rocky and elevated or swampy and low-lying," with few roads. In 1808 Randel mapped its hills, rocks, swamps, marshes, streams and ponds, and its houses, barns, fences and cleared fields. His crews set 1,549 marble markers and 98 iron bolts to fix the grid on the ground. To build each street, "hills were excavated or hollows filled in, the right of way was leveled and the street was paved"; about 721 of the 1,825 buildings standing north of Houston Street in 1811 had to be moved. Manhattan is Lenape homeland (see era 03 PATCH). (Wikipedia "Commissioners' Plan of 1811"; Wikipedia "John Randel Jr.".)
- **Water before Croton.** New Yorkers drew water from wells, ponds and springs in the city; the Manhattan Company (from 1799) sank wells at the Collect Pond and sent water out through wooden pipes; carters sold "pure" country springwater by the barrel at a penny a gallon, and it too was often polluted; rain was caught in cisterns. With more privies and seepage from old graveyards, downtown wells by the 1830s brought up tainted water, and cholera spread "when the sick used their privies or cesspools and the waste overflowed into the nearby wells and basements." (NYC Department of Environmental Protection, "History of New York City's Drinking Water"; Burrows and Wallace, *Gotham*, ch. "Filth, Fever, Water, Fire"; Fraunces Tavern Museum, "Thirsty New York".)
- **Cholera's speed.** Severe cholera causes so much watery diarrhea that a person can die of dehydration within hours if not treated. (CDC "About Cholera"; CDC Yellow Book; WHO cholera fact sheet.)
- **The 1835 fire.** It began on the evening of December 16, 1835, in a five-story warehouse at 25 Merchant Street (now Beaver Street) near Hanover Street and Wall Street, in lower Manhattan's business district; a gale blew it toward the East River, and water froze in the hydrants and hoses. (Wikipedia "Great Fire of New York"; OUP blog 2022.)
- **Croton.** NYC DEP: by the late 1820s "City leaders realized they had to develop a cleaner and more abundant source of water." The Old Croton Dam stood six miles above the Croton's junction with the Hudson, in Westchester County, north of the city; the aqueduct carried water 41 miles "largely by gravity" to a receiving reservoir (now the Great Lawn in Central Park) and on to the Murray Hill distributing reservoir at 42nd Street and Fifth Avenue. (NYC DEP, same page.) The conduit was a horseshoe-shaped brick tunnel 8.5 feet high and 7.5 feet wide on a stone foundation, falling about 13 inches per mile. (ASCE Metropolitan Section, "Croton Water Supply System" landmark page; Haverstraw Brick Museum; Wikipedia "Croton Aqueduct".)

### Central Park and Seneca Village

### PATCH 2026-10-01 (T-454): details for 1850-1900
- **Otis.** Elisha Graves Otis's brake was a steel wagon spring meshing with a ratchet. At the Crystal Palace, "The inventor ascended in an elevator inside an open-sided shaft. Halfway up, he had the hoisting cable cut with an axe. The platform held fast." (National Inventors Hall of Fame, "Elisha Graves Otis".)
- **Rents by floor.** Before passenger elevators, the higher the floor, the lower the rent; elevators let floors above the fourth or fifth, the limit people would walk, rent for as much as the floors below or more. (Skyscraper Museum, "Ten & Taller"; Building the Skyline, "The Technology of Tall, Part II".)
- **The limit of walls.** Chicago's Monadnock Building (finished 1891), 16 stories and 215 feet, is the tallest building ever held up by load-bearing brick walls; its walls are six feet thick at the base and taper to 18 inches at the top. (WTTW "Monadnock Building"; Wikipedia "Monadnock Building".)
- **Post.** The Produce Exchange architect was George B. Post. (Wikipedia "George B. Post"; "New York Produce Exchange".)
- **The White City.** The exposition buildings were meant to be temporary: steel-framed sheds with fronts of staff, a mix of plaster, cement and jute fiber poured into molds, painted white. More than 27 million people visited during the fair's six-month run. The City Beautiful movement of the 1890s and 1900s, inspired by the White City, aimed to bring beauty and grand public buildings, avenues and parks into American cities. (Wikipedia "World's Columbian Exposition"; Chicago Architecture Center "5 fun facts about the World's Columbian Exposition"; Encyclopedia of Cleveland History "City Beautiful Movement"; Wikipedia "City Beautiful movement".)
- **Tenement laws.** New York State passed a Tenement House Law on May 14, 1867, the nation's first comprehensive housing reform law: no cellar apartments unless the ceiling stood a foot above the street, one water closet (toilet) for every 20 residents, and fire escapes. Enforcement was lax, and builders met its window rule by cutting windows between inner rooms. The 1879 Act (the "Old Law") was a response to that failure. (Encyclopedia.com / Dictionary of American History "Tenements"; Wikipedia "Old Law Tenement".) Riis's work, with other reform groups, led to the Tenement House Act of 1901. (Library of Congress exhibition "Jacob Riis: Revealing How the Other Half Lives," "Riis and Reform"; Wikipedia "New York State Tenement House Act".)
- **Riis at night.** As a police reporter Riis covered the city's poorest districts, including Mulberry Street; the police ran homeless shelters called police lodging houses, which he saw and wrote about. He took up flash powder around 1887 to photograph the tenements, saloons and slums at night. (Smithsonian Magazine, "Pioneering Social Reformer Jacob Riis"; ICP "Jacob Riis".)
- **Swill milk.** Swill milk came from cows kept in stables beside distilleries and fed the distillery's leftover grain mash ("swill"); the milk was thin and dirty, and was blamed for many infant deaths in 1850s New York. (Wikipedia "Swill milk scandal".) The Tenement Museum says the Moores' baby Agnes suffered from marasmus "due to tainted milk." (Tenement Museum blog, "Father's Day at 97 Orchard Street".)
- **Seneca Village taking.** "The City acquired the land through eminent domain, the law that allows the government to take private land for public use with compensation paid to the landowner." Owners were paid, but many argued their land was undervalued. About 1,600 people were pushed off the whole park site. New York required Black men to own $250 in property to vote; of about 100 Black men in the city who qualified in 1845, 10 lived in Seneca Village. (Central Park Conservancy, "Seneca Village".) ### PATCH 2026-10-01 (T-454): details for 1900-1950
- **Immigrants into the cities.** Ellis Island's busiest year was 1907, with 1,004,756 immigrants admitted; about 12 million passed through Ellis Island from 1892 to 1954. (Copied from `research-immigration.md` era 08: Statue of Liberty-Ellis Island Foundation; American Immigration Council.)
- **Chicago's water.** Chicago drew its drinking water from Lake Michigan through intake cribs about two miles offshore; sewage went into the Chicago River, which flowed into the lake; a great storm in 1885 carried river filth far out into the lake and raised fears for the intakes, which led to the Sanitary District's creation. In 1900 Missouri sued the Sanitary District; the case was later dismissed because Missouri could not prove the Mississippi's pollution came from Chicago (St. Louis also dumped its waste in the river). (Wikipedia "Chicago Sanitary and Ship Canal"; Illinois Secretary of State archives, "Photo of the Reversal of the Chicago River (1900)".)
- **San Francisco, 1906.** The earthquake struck on the morning of April 18, 1906. It broke the main water conduits from the Crystal Springs and San Andreas reservoirs and caused more than 300 breaks in the city's mains; firefighters found no water in the hydrants. The fire burned for about 72 hours and charred more than four square miles. (California Geological Survey, "The 1906 Great San Francisco Earthquake"; EBSCO research starter; History.com.) President Woodrow Wilson signed the Raker Act on December 19, 1913; it is the only time a city was allowed to develop a national park for its own use. San Francisco later sold the dam's power to Pacific Gas and Electric, a private company, which led to decades of legal fights over the Act's no-private-profit terms. (Wikipedia "Raker Act"; hetchhetchy.org "The Raker Act"; National Archives "H.R. 7207, The Raker Bill".)
- **The Equitable Building's shadow.** The 40-story building, finished in 1915, cast a shadow of about seven acres on the streets around it and cut off the sun from neighboring buildings, which drew attention to the loss of light and air in lower Manhattan; the 1916 setbacks were meant to keep air and sunlight reaching the sidewalk. (New-York Historical Society blog, "Undesirable Edifices Generally"; Gothamist; RPA "Celebrating 100 years of zoning".)
- **Zoning and exclusion.** In *Buchanan v. Warley* (1917) the Supreme Court unanimously struck down Louisville's ordinance barring Black buyers from white-majority blocks; the case was brought with William Warley, a Black buyer and NAACP lawyer. Afterward local governments turned to rules that named no race. Euclid's 1922 ordinance, upheld in 1926, banned two-family houses and apartment buildings from parts of the village, which kept out families who could not afford a single house. (Wikipedia "Exclusionary zoning"; Boston Fair Housing timeline "1917: Buchanan v. Warley"; Governing, "How the Defeat of a Zoning Law Reshaped Racial Segregation".)
- **The Plan of Chicago's money.** The Commercial Club raised about $80,000 to pay for the plan and spent more than $40,000 printing 1,650 copies of the book; Burnham worked on it for about thirty months and charged nothing. The Commercial Club was a club of Chicago's leading businessmen, founded 1877 (Wikipedia "Commercial Club of Chicago"). The Chrysler architect was William Van Alen (Wikipedia "William Van Alen"). Caughnawaga is the older English spelling of Kahnawake (Wikipedia "Kahnawake"). (NEH *Humanities*, "Taming the Savage City", 2009; Encyclopedia of Chicago, "The Plan Comes Together".)

(Wikipedia "Home Owners' Loan Corporation"; Chicago Fed WP 2022-01; Governing, "Redlining didn't happen quite the way we thought it did".)
- **Oak Ridge.** On September 19, 1942, Brigadier General Leslie Groves, newly put in charge of the Manhattan Project, approved buying about 59,000 acres along the Clinch River, about twenty miles west of Knoxville ("Site X"). - **Colleen Black's work.** She tested for pinhole leaks in the hundreds of miles of pipe being put into K-25, climbing over the pipes; workers were not supposed to say the word "helium." Her words: "I didn't ask. I mean, I didn't know where those pipes were going, I didn't know what was going through them ... I just knew that I had to find the leak and mark it." (Atomic Heritage Foundation, "Colleen Black's Interview (2005)" and "The K-25 Plant".)
- **Hiroshima.** Copied from `research-war.md` era 08: "Little Boy," a uranium bomb, was dropped on Hiroshima on August 6, 1945. ### PATCH 2026-10-01 (T-454): details for 1950-2000
- **How urban renewal worked.** From 1949 the program gave local renewal agencies federal money and the power of eminent domain to condemn neighborhoods labeled slums or "blighted," tear the buildings down and resell the cleared land to private developers at a reduced price; developers paid much less than the city had spent to take, clear and prepare the land (the "write-down"), and federal money covered the gap. The Supreme Court upheld this in *Berman v. Parker* (1954). (Encyclopedia Virginia, "Urban Renewal in Virginia"; Institute for Justice, "History of Eminent Domain"; Digital Scholarship Lab "Renewing Inequality".)
- **The highway law and the routes.** President Dwight D. Eisenhower signed the Federal-Aid Highway Act on June 29, 1956. Highway planners built largely where land was cheap and organized opposition was least likely, which meant poor neighborhoods, mostly of people of color; builders defended taking Black neighborhoods by saying the land was cheapest there, a cheapness that redlining had helped create. (Wikipedia "Federal-Aid Highway Act of 1956"; Eisenhower Presidential Library; History.com "How Interstate Highways Reinforced Segregation"; ASCE *Civil Engineering*, 2021; AARP "Before the Highway".)
- **Marvin Roger Anderson's age, settled.** In AARP's 2023 "Before the Highway" interview Anderson says "I arrived on the scene in 1940" and that he graduated from Central High School in 1958; the Sahan Journal (March 2022) has him "approaching 83" and "now 82", which matches 1940. Rondo Days (1983) was set up for the "preservation and perpetuation of the history, culture and memories of the Rondo that existed before the highway." ReConnect Rondo's land bridge would hold housing, businesses and community space. (AARP Livable Communities, "Before the Highway: Saint Paul, Minnesota", 2023; Sahan Journal via reconnectrondo.com; Wikipedia "Rondo Days"; OpenCommons "Reconnect Rondo".)
- **Levittown's method.** Levitt & Sons broke building into 27 steps, each done by a crew trained for that step; the houses stayed put and the crews moved from house to house; at the peak about 30 houses a day went up. The standard lease for the first houses said they could not "be used or occupied by any person other than members of the Caucasian race," and in 1960 Levittown was still all white. (Wikipedia "Levittown" and "Levitt & Sons"; Construction Physics, "WW2 Era Mass-Produced Housing".)
- **Blockbusting.** Real estate agents and speculators frightened white owners into selling cheaply by telling them Black families were moving in, then resold the houses to Black families at much higher prices, often "on contract," under which one missed payment meant eviction and the loss of everything paid. In Chicago's North Lawndale about 3,000 buildings were sold on contract, and the neighborhood went from 87 percent white in 1950 to 91 percent Black in 1960. (Wikipedia "Contract Buyers League"; Natalie Y. Moore, "This Is Black Chicago," Art Institute of Chicago.)

### PATCH 2026-10-01 (T-454): details for 2000-today
- **Downtown growth.** In the 2000s downtowns grew faster than their home counties and metro areas, breaking decades of the opposite trend; 42 of 53 downtowns studied added people. (Brookings Metro, "Big city downtowns are booming," 2020 brief.) By 2000, people aged 25 to 34 were nearly a quarter of downtown residents, up from 13 percent in 1970, and 44 percent of downtown residents had a bachelor's degree or more. (Brookings, "Who Lives Downtown," 2005, covering 44 cities, 1970-2000.)
- **Why the offices emptied.** Axios (January 2024, reporting Moody's): office vacancies hit a record "as remote and hybrid work stick around"; companies shrank their space after the pandemic, and long leases took years to unwind. The vacancy rate reached 19.8 percent in early 2024 and 20.1 percent in the second quarter, the highest since Moody's began tracking in 1979. (Axios, Jan 9 and Jul 9, 2024; Bloomberg, Apr 2, 2024; Fortune, Jul 2, 2024.)
- **U Street.** In its heyday (about 1900 to the early 1960s) U Street had "hundreds of black-owned and black-friendly businesses, churches, theaters, gyms, and other community spaces," among them the Lincoln Theatre (1921) and the Howard Theatre (1926), where Duke Ellington, Louis Armstrong and Billie Holiday played. After Martin Luther King Jr. was murdered on April 4, 1968, 14th and U was the center of four days of riots in Washington: 13 people died and about 1,200 homes and businesses were damaged. Drug dealing at that corner rose sharply in the mid-1970s. More than 2,000 luxury condominiums and apartments went up nearby from 1997 to 2007, and the neighborhood went from about 60 percent Black and 20 percent white in 2000 to about 20 percent Black and 60 percent white by 2010. (Wikipedia "U Street (Washington, D.C.)"; Wikipedia "1968 Washington, D.C., riots"; National Archives Unwritten Record blog, "April 1968 Washington, D.C. Riots".) King was shot in Memphis, Tennessee; James Earl Ray pleaded guilty to the murder in March 1969 (copied from `research-holidays.md`, which cites the `rights-movements` bank and the US Department of Justice Civil Rights Division report of June 2000). During the riots Stokely Carmichael, head of the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee, asked that Ben's stay open past the curfew, and Ben Ali wrote "Soul Brother" in soap on the front window. (WTOP 2018, "From Black Broadway to Black Power"; Wikipedia "Ben's Chili Bowl".)
- **Why cities run hot.** EPA: roads, sidewalks and buildings absorb and hold much of the sun's heat; cities have fewer trees and plants and less moisture, so they get less shade and less cooling from evaporation; tall buildings and narrow streets block cooling air flow. EPA's listed ways to reduce it: more trees and plants, green roofs, cool (sunlight-reflecting) roofs, cool pavements and smart growth. (EPA, "Reduce Heat Islands"; EPA Heat Island Cooling Strategies.)
- **Miami Beach's water.** At the highest tides, the ocean pushes Biscayne Bay back up the storm drains, and bay water pours out of the drains into the lowest streets; seawater also rises through the porous limestone underground. The city added one-way valves on the drain outlets and pumps. (Penn State EARTH 103, "Miami Beach and Rising Seas"; FORWARD Florida, "Fighting Sunny Day Flooding in Miami Beach".)
- **One World Trade Center's height.** 1,776 feet was chosen for 1776, the year of the Declaration of Independence; the last piece of the spire went on May 10, 2013. (Britannica "One World Trade Center"; Wikipedia "One World Trade Center".)
