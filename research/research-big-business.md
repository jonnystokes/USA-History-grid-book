## 03 · The 1600s

### The Virginia Company of London (Encyclopedia Virginia, "Virginia Company of London", https://encyclopediavirginia.org/entries/virginia-company-of-london/, opened twice on 2026-09-27)

- **1606:** King James I chartered the Virginia Company of London to plant a colony in North America. Jamestown was founded 13 May 1607 (the date is the outline's, and the standard one. Encyclopedia Virginia's company entry was not asked for it.)
- **Second charter, 23 May 1609:** "divested the king of some of his power. Investors now elected a treasurer to lead the company." Investors met in a weekly Court and Assembly and a quarterly Great and General Court. Supports the outline's "moved control from the king's council to the shareholders".
- **Third charter, 12 March 1612:** extended the company's bounds to include Bermuda and authorized lotteries. Power to elect all officers of the company and the colony moved from the council to the General Assembly (the shareholders' meeting).
- **Shares:** £12 10s. a share for a seven-year term. The first sales "may have netted as much as £10,000". Payments in 1616 "were made in land alone".
- **Lotteries:** began 1612. By May 1620 lotteries had brought in "£7,000 in lottery earnings for the previous year out of £9,831 in total cash".
- **CORRECTION (lottery ban):** Encyclopedia Virginia says "The king banned the lotteries soon after" the House of Commons debate of 24 February 1621. The outline says "Parliament banned them after February 1621". The source names the king, not Parliament. Writers must say King James I stopped the lotteries in 1621, after members of the House of Commons attacked them.
- **The deaths, 1619 to 1622.** Virginia's population in 1619, when Sandys took over the company, was 700. "Another 3,570 men, women, and children had entered the colony in the subsequent three years, adding up to a population of 4,270. But after Opechancanough's attacks, which resulted in the deaths of 347 colonists, only 1,240 settlers remained. What, Wrote demanded, had happened to the other 2,683? Most of them, it turns out, had died of disease." The count is the investor **Samuel Wrote**'s, made during the company's internal fight. (Encyclopedia Virginia.) The outline's "One investor's count blamed disease for 2,683 of the other deaths" slightly overstates the source: Wrote asked what happened to the 2,683, and Encyclopedia Virginia's authors say most died of disease. The war and its causes (English expansion onto Powhatan land for tobacco) are told in `native-nations` and `war`.

The headright grants below were grants of Powhatan land.

### The other chartered companies

- **Dutch West India Company.** Charter given at The Hague by the States General on **3 June 1621**. For 24 years, no one from the United Netherlands but the company could sail to or trade with "the coast and countries of Africa from the Tropic of Cancer to the Cape of Good Hope, nor in the countries of America, or the West-Indies" (Avalon Project, Yale Law School, "Charter of the Dutch West India Company: 1621", text from Thorpe, *Federal and State Constitutions*, 1909, opened 2026-09-27). The charter also let the company make alliances with Native rulers, build forts and plant colonies (Encyclopedia.com "Dutch West India Company", via search summary: unconfirmed: search summary only).
- **Fort Orange, 1624:** "Fort Orange was the first permanent Dutch settlement in New Netherland, built as a trading post by the West India Company in 1624 at the location of present-day Albany along the Hudson River." (New York State Museum, exhibition "a small fort, which our people call Fort Orange", opened 2026-09-27.) Land: the upper Hudson was Mohican (Mahican) country. The museum page names "Native Americans" only. The Mohawk-Mohican war of 1625 near the fort is recorded by the New Netherland Institute (unconfirmed: search summary only).
- **Manhattan:** the outline says the company "settled Manhattan in 1625". Sources found: the first settlers (about 30 Walloon families) sailed on the *Nieu Nederlandt* on 29 March 1624, and Director Peter Minuit bought Manhattan "from the Indians for some 60 guilders worth of trinkets" between 4 May and 26 June 1626 (University of Notre Dame, "A Brief Outline of the History of New Netherland", unconfirmed: search summary only). **Writer's note:** 1625 is the usual date for the laying out of Fort Amsterdam, and 1626 for Minuit's purchase. Use "1625 to 1626" or name both. **Land:** Manhattan (Mannahatta) was Lenape land, part of the Lenape homeland Lenapehoking. The Dutch claimed they "purchased" it in 1626 for 60 guilders' worth of trade goods and likely misread what the Lenape meant by the exchange (Pratt Institute Libraries, *Lenape Peoples' History* guide, as recorded in `research/research-america-world.md` lines 214 to 224).
- **The company owned people.** Men enslaved by the Dutch West India Company dug the New Amsterdam wall's trench in 1653. The Mapping the African American Past project (Columbia University) names Paulo d'Angola, Simon Congo and Anthony Portuguese and says they "were owned by the Dutch West India Company, but some had gained a form of half-freedom" (recorded in `research/research-city-building.md` line 45). The company was a slaveholder as well as a trader.
- **Massachusetts Bay Company.** Charter granted by Charles I and dated **4 March 1628 Old Style**, which is 4 March 1629 by the modern calendar (the English year then began on 25 March) (Wikipedia, "Charter of the Massachusetts Bay Company", opened 2026-09-27). The outline's "chartered 1629" is correct in modern reckoning.
- **Cambridge Agreement.** Signed at Cambridge, England. Twelve signers, among them John Winthrop, Richard Saltonstall and Thomas Dudley, promised to sail for New England by 1 March next "Provided alwayes that before the last of September next the whole governement together with the Patent for the said plantacion bee first by an order of Court legally transferred and established to remayne with us". (Teaching American History, full text, opened 2026-09-27.) **Date disagreement:** the document is headed 26 August 1629, and its closing line reads "This was done by order of Court the 29th of August. 1629." The date **23 October 1684** is given by History of Massachusetts Blog ("Massachusetts Bay Colony Charter of 1629", opened 2026-09-27), which lists the reasons as trade with other countries against the Navigation Acts of 1651 and 1660, a colonial mint that made coins without the king's image, and laws against Quakers and Anglicans. The charter was a company's founding paper and became the colony's government until the Dominion of New England in 1686. Land: the charter granted land already lived on by Native nations. Which nations, around Massachusetts Bay, is `native-nations`' to source. It was not sourced this session.
- **Hudson's Bay Company.** "The Hudson's Bay Company (HBC) was chartered on 2 May 1670." The charter named the "Governor and Company of Adventurers of England trading into Hudson Bay" and gave it exclusive trading rights over all lands drained by rivers flowing into Hudson Bay, which it named Rupert's Land. Prince Rupert, cousin of Charles II, persuaded the king, merchants and noblemen to back it. (The Canadian Encyclopedia, "Hudson's Bay Company", Arthur J. Ray, last edited 10 July 2025, opened 2026-09-27.) In 1821 the HBC absorbed its rival the North West Company and took over its Pacific Northwest posts. On **19 March 1825** HBC Governor George Simpson, with Chief Factor Dr. John McLoughlin, formally opened **Fort Vancouver** on the north bank of the Columbia River, in what is now Vancouver, Clark County, Washington. ### PATCH 2026-09-27 (T-256a): the Royal African Company (hard subject the outline's 1600s cell omits)

The checkpoint names the Royal African Company as a hard subject for this chapter. The outline mentions it only once, in era 04, as a monopoly that ended in 1698. It belongs in era 03 as a chartered company whose business was carrying enslaved Africans, many of them to English North America.
- **Who chartered it:** King Charles II granted a charter in 1660 to the Company of Royal Adventurers Trading to Africa, led by his brother James, Duke of York (later King James II). That company collapsed under debt in 1667 and was re-chartered in **1672** as the Royal African Company, with a monopoly on English trade with West Africa, including gold, silver and enslaved people (History.com, Sarah Pruitt, opened 2026-09-27).
- **How many people, whose count:** Between 1672 and 1731 ships the company invested in carried **186,748 enslaved people on 652 voyages** to English colonies in the Americas. **16,077** of them died on the way. The earlier Company of Royal Adventurers (1662 to 1672) carried **27,489** people on 105 voyages, of whom 670 died. (Wikipedia, "Royal African Company", citing its ref 23, which is the Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade Database, opened 2026-09-27.) History.com gives an average of about 5,000 people a year from 1680 to 1686, "most of which were shipped to colonies in the Caribbean and Virginia".
- **Branding (clinical word, must be defined in the prose):** "Thousands of slaves arrived in the New World with the company's initials branded on their chests" (History.com). Wikipedia adds that many were branded with "DoY" for the Duke of York, and others with "RAC" on their chests. To brand a person means to press a red-hot metal stamp against the skin, which burns the letters into the flesh and leaves a permanent raised scar. (The method is the standard meaning of branding. Neither source describes the iron itself. A writer who needs a source for the method should cite a dictionary or `slavery-freedom`'s bank.)
- **The historian's judgment:** William Pettigrew (*Freedom's Debt*, 2013) writes that the company "shipped more enslaved African women, men and children to the Americas than any other single institution during the entire period of the transatlantic slave trade", and that its investors knew what the company did and meant to profit from it (quoted in Wikipedia, "Royal African Company").
- **The monopoly's end:** the company itself admitted in 1689 that it had lost its monopoly when James II was overthrown (Wikipedia). History.com: "the RAC effectively lost its monopoly in 1689". Parliament's Trade with Africa Act 1697 (dated 1698 in most accounts, 9 Will. 3 c. 26) opened the African trade to every English merchant who paid the company a 10 percent levy on goods exported from Africa (Wikipedia). The company stopped slave trading in 1731 and was dissolved by Parliament in 1752 (History.com). The outline's era 04 line "ended in 1698" is supported, with the 1689 loss noted.
- **Owner:** the Middle Passage itself is `slavery-freedom`'s (director ruling). This chapter's angle is that a chartered company, run by a future king and financed by shareholders, ran the trade for profit.

### PATCH 2026-09-27 (T-256a): Hancock and Apthorp's ships in the expulsion of the Acadians, 1755 (hard subject missing from the story)

The outline's Hancock story says the partners supplied "ships" to British forces and stops there. The sources show what some of those ships carried.
- **What happened:** in the autumn of 1755 British officials in Nova Scotia (Governor Charles Lawrence) and Massachusetts (Governor William Shirley) forced the Acadians, French-speaking Catholic farmers, out of Nova Scotia and shipped them to the other British colonies. The Acadians' houses and farms were taken. (Acadian Genealogy, "The Ships of the Acadian Expulsion", acadian.org, opened 2026-09-27.)
- **Hancock's part:** "Governor Shirley and Colonel Lawrence had contracted, or chartered these vessels, by the month, for a flat fee per head, from Charles Apthorp and Thomas Hancock of the Boston Mercantile firm of Apthrop and Hancock." The fleet was "about 24 sailing vessels", converted in Boston "to hold 2 persons per ton (in some cases 300 to 500 persons)". The transports were ready on 11 October 1755. The firm charged **40 to 48 pounds a month** per vessel. The ship list comes from "accounts transmitted by Apthorp & Hancock of Boston, to Governor Lawrence", printed in *Selections from the Public Documents of the Province of Nova Scotia* (1869), pages 285 to 289. (acadian.org, citing that primary record and Basil Sollers, *Maryland Historical Magazine* vol. III no. 1, March 1908.)
- **Examples:** the sloop *Endeavor*, 96 tons, master James Nichols, left Chignecto on 13 October 1755 with 121 exiles for South Carolina, arriving 19 November 1755. The sloop *Ranger*, 90 tons, master Francis Piercy, chartered from Apthorp and Hancock 20 August 1755 to 30 January 1756, carried 208 people, 81 more than the two-per-ton limit. (Search result quoting blupete.com and acadian.org transport lists: unconfirmed: search summary only.)
- **Deaths at sea:** "Because of the lack of manifests, or passenger lists, there is no record of those Acadians who died at sea." (acadian.org.) Wikipedia ("Charles Apthorp") states that the poor quality of the ships Apthorp and Hancock supplied "led to instances of malnutrition, disease and death among the Acadians on board", and that the two merchants also lent money to finance the operation. The historian Mark Peterson describes the sloops as "fitted out in the manner of slave ships for the large number of expected deportees" (quoted in Wikipedia, "Thomas Hancock (merchant)").
- **CORRECTION to a source:** Wikipedia's Hancock article says 17 sloops carried Acadians "to French Louisiana". The 1755 transports went to British colonies on the Atlantic coast (the *Endeavor* to South Carolina, others to Maryland per Sollers). Do not repeat "to Louisiana".

Name Lawrence and Shirley as the officials who ordered the removal, and Hancock and Apthorp as the merchants who were paid by the head to carry the people. ## 05 · 1750 to 1800

- **Wright's count:** "Of the 319 business corporations tracked in the table" (Table 7.1, "Corporations chartered before 1801"), 15 never began operations. Of the 319, 8 date from the colonial period and 21 from 1776 to 1789. The rest date from 1790 to 1800. (Wright, NBER chapter c11744, opened 2026-09-27.) Supports the outline's "319 business corporations chartered in the United States before 1801, almost all of them after the Revolution". Banks, bridges, canals, turnpikes, insurance and water utilities make up most of the table. Manufacturing is 7 of the 319.
- **CORRECTION (limited liability, whose count):** the outline says "only 16 of the pre-1801 charters said plainly that shareholders could not be made to pay the company's debts (Wright)". Wright's text reports this as the finding of Oscar and Mary Handlin (1945): because most early charters had no clause limiting liability, "sixteen expressly provided unqualified limited liability for shareholders, while another sixteen imposed full liability on directors or shareholders only if certain financial ratios were exceeded". Wright then argues against the Handlins' conclusion: corporations "could not assess stockholders for more than the par value of their shares so liability for solvent corporations was inherently limited", and limited liability became established "in subterranean fashion". Writers must attribute the 16 to the Handlins, as reported by Wright, and not present it as a count of all 319.
- **Toll bridges:** "From 1786 to 1798, fifty-nine private toll bridge companies were chartered in the northeast, beginning with Boston's Charles River Bridge, which brought investors an average annual return of 10.5 percent in its first six years (Davis 1917, II, 188)." ### The Tea Act and the East India Company

- **Tea Act, passed by Parliament on 10 May 1773.** It let the East India Company export tea straight to the American colonies. It imposed no new tax. The Townshend duty of 1767 on tea stayed. With the Act, "the seventeen million pounds of unsold surplus tea the British East India Company owned could be sold to markets in the American colonies" at a reduced price. "The direct sale of tea by agents of the British East India Company to the American colonies undercut the business of colonial merchants." (Boston Tea Party Ships & Museum, "The Tea Act", opened 2026-09-27.)

## 06 · 1800 to 1850

### From special charter to general law

- **New York, 22 March 1811.** "The Act relative to incorporations for manufacturing purposes passed by the 34th New York State Legislature on March 22, 1811, was the first law in the US giving a general authorization for formation of corporations." Groups of five or more could form a manufacturing corporation for 20 years, with no more than nine trustees and capital of no more than $100,000. It covered "woolen, cotton, or linen goods" and iron, steel, copper, lead and similar metal goods. Section 7 made shareholders liable for the company's debts at dissolution "to the extent of their respective shares of stock ... and no further". (Wikipedia, "Act Relative to Incorporations for Manufacturing Purposes of 1811", citing Seavoy, *Business History Review* 46(1), 1972, and Kessler, *Journal of Political Economy*, 1940, opened 2026-09-27.) Seavoy: "the first effective general incorporation statute for business corporations passed by any state ... essentially an emergency measure" to get thread made for home weaving when European textiles were cut off by the Embargo of 1807 and the Non-Intercourse Act of 1809. Supports the outline, which says the charter term was limited: the limit was 20 years.
- **Connecticut, 1837.** The act allowed incorporation "for the purpose of engaging in and carrying on any kind of manufacturing or mechanical or mining or quarrying or any other lawful business". Eric Hilt: "in 1837, Connecticut passed a general incorporation act that was the first to not specifically enumerate the industries that could be pursued, or to limit the duration of the existence of the corporations it created." Banks still needed a special act. (Texas Law Review, "The History and Revival of the Corporate Purpose Clause", quoting 1837 Conn. Pub. Acts 49 and Hilt, opened 2026-09-27.) Supports the outline's "generally counted as the first that let people incorporate for any lawful business".

The outline does not say this. It is part of this era's story: a Black American in those states could not form a corporation under the general law.

### Andrew Carnegie (story `andrew-carnegie-big-business`, marked verified)

- **Vertical integration:** "In the 1890s Carnegie pushed for greater and greater vertical integration, as a way to cut costs and increase profits. He had previously acquired a dominant stake in Frick's coke business and now leased recently-discovered ore deposits in Minnesota's Mesabi Range, purchased lake steamers to handle his ore traffic and bought and extended a railroad to link his steel works to Lake Erie" (EH.Net Encyclopedia, "Carnegie, Andrew", opened 2026-09-27). By the end of the 1890s Carnegie Steel was "the world's largest steel producer, manufacturing a quarter of the nation's soaring steel output", with profits of $40 million in 1900. **Vertical integration** means one company owning each step of making a product, from raw material to finished goods.

Frick closed the deal in March 1894 (search summary of Wikipedia "MinnTac": unconfirmed: search summary only). The outline's "until it held about three-quarters of that range's output" was **not found** on an opened page. Drop it or find a source.

EH.Net's "bought and extended a railroad to link his steel works to Lake Erie" is the opened source. It supports the Lake Erie line. The Union Railroad line in the outline rests on the search summary.

### Homestead, 1892, from the company's side

- **Frick's moves:** the Amalgamated Association's contract expired 1 July 1892. Carnegie, in Scotland, gave Frick "carte blanche to break the union". Frick cut wages, and in late June locked the workers out and built "a massive barbed-wire-topped fence around the plant", which the workers called "Fort Frick". On 2 July he fired all 3,800 workers. (Britannica, "Homestead Strike", opened 2026-09-27.) The outline's "locked the workers out on 30 June 1892" is consistent with "late June".
- **The Pinkertons:** Frick "contracted definitely with William and Robert Pinkerton for an armed force of three hundred men (at five dollars per day per man) to be towed up the river in two barges early on July 6". The agency collected "a total of 316 men in New York and Chicago. Mostly unemployed or drifters, with a few college lads trying to earn a little money between semesters, a hard core of Pinkerton regulars, some hoodlums and out-and-out criminals on the run". Their crates held 250 Winchester rifles and 300 pistols. The captains were Frederick H. Heinde, 42, and Charles Nordrum, 35. (Leon Wolff, "Battle at Homestead", *American Heritage* vol. 16 no. 3, April 1965, opened 2026-09-27.) Britannica: "a force of 300 Pinkerton agents". The range 300 to 316 is supported.
- **Frick's officers:** Superintendent John Potter. Deputy Sheriff Joseph H. Gray, sent by Allegheny County Sheriff William H. McCleary. The Carnegie company's lawyers Knox & Reed. (Wolff.)
- **The dead, sources disagree, and PATCH 2026-09-27 (T-256a): the named dead.** Britannica: "at least three Pinkertons and seven workers were killed during the battle and its aftermath". Wolff: "Bullets, beatings, drowning, and suicide brought the death toll to approximately nine strikers and seven Pinkertons. Some forty strikers and twenty Pinkertons were shot", and "When the strike finally collapsed in November, thirty-five men were dead as a result of the July 6 battle and subsequent violence." **Named by Wolff:** a Pinkerton guard named **Klein**, "killed instantly by a bullet through the head" on the gangplank. **Joseph Sotak**, who went to help the wounded roller Martin Murray, "was killed by a bullet in the mouth". A worker named **Streigle**, "shot through the throat", "died instantly" (Wolff gives no first names for Klein or Streigle). Pinkerton Captain Heinde was shot in the thigh and the shoulder. The crewman John McCurry of the tug *Little Bill* was shot in the groin. After the Pinkertons surrendered, the crowd beat them as they walked to the town: "One striker carefully slugged one captive after another behind the ear with a large stone wrapped in leather", and a woman pushed an umbrella into a Pinkerton's eye "and poked it out" (Wolff). This is `work-workers`' to tell in full. This chapter needs the company-side facts: Frick hired the armed men, and men on both sides died.
- **The state:** "Frick asked Pennsylvania Gov. Robert Emory Pattison for help; he responded by sending in 8,500 soldiers of the state National Guard. The plant was turned over to the militiamen on July 12. By July 15 the plant was again operational but with replacement workers." The union gave up on 21 November. (Britannica.) Wolff: Pattison ordered the militia out on 11 July. `research/research-work-workers.md` line 88 has 8,000. Use 8,500 (Britannica) and note the difference.
- **After:** criminal charges against "scores of union leaders and workers", almost all acquitted (Britannica). Wages in all Carnegie mills were cut by about half on a tonnage basis: a heater's helper earning $0.0485 a ton in February 1892 earned $0.0222 in February 1894. The twelve-hour day was enforced, and the union's advisory committee was blacklisted for life. (Wolff.) Many strikebreakers were African Americans brought from the South, because the union barred Black workers. In November 1892 about 2,000 white workers attacked Black workers and their families, and several people were badly wounded by gunfire (Britannica). The racial riot is `work-workers`' and `rights-movements`'.
- **Congress:** Frick testified to the congressional investigation into the use of Pinkerton detectives at Homestead in 1892 (DPLA primary source set "The Homestead Strike", excerpt from Frick's testimony. House Judiciary Committee, *Investigation of the Employment of Pinkerton Detectives*, 1892, on Internet Archive: both seen in search results).

### John W. Holway (story `john-w-holway`, marked verified)

All from Wolff, *American Heritage*, April 1965 (opened 2026-09-27), which quotes his sworn testimony to the congressional committee:

### The first federal rules

- **Interstate Commerce Act:** "Approved on February 4, 1887, the Interstate Commerce Act created an Interstate Commerce Commission to oversee the conduct of the railroad industry. With this act, the railroads became the first industry subject to Federal regulation." It set up "a five-member enforcement board". (National Archives, Milestone Documents, "Interstate Commerce Act (1887)", opened 2026-09-27.) The outline's claim that the ICC "was the model for every federal regulatory commission that followed" is **not stated** by the National Archives page. It is a common historians' judgment. Writers should say "the first federal regulatory commission" (which the source supports) and drop "model for every".
- **Sherman Antitrust Act:** "Approved July 2, 1890, The Sherman Anti-Trust Act was the first Federal act that outlawed monopolistic business practices." Named for Senator John Sherman of Ohio. Every contract or combination "in restraint of trade or commerce among the several States, or with foreign nations" was declared illegal, with fines of $5,000 and a year in jail, and triple damages for those harmed. "It was loosely worded and failed to define such critical terms as 'trust,' 'combination,' 'conspiracy,' and 'monopoly.'" (National Archives, Milestone Documents, "Sherman Anti-Trust Act (1890)", opened 2026-09-27.)
- **United States v. E. C. Knight Co., 1895:** "The Court ruled that the American Sugar Refining Company, one of the defendants in the case, had not violated the law even though the company controlled about 98% of all sugar refining in the United States. The Supreme Court reasoned that the company's control of manufacture did not constitute a control of trade." (National Archives, same page.) Supports the outline exactly.
- **The Act against unions:** "Claiming that the strike violated the Sherman Antitrust Act, Edwin Walker, a special attorney acting for U.S. Attorney General Richard Olney, obtained an injunction that U.S. Pres. Grover Cleveland used to dispatch federal troops" (Britannica, "Pullman Strike", opened 2026-09-27). Encyclopedia.com: the Act "had been passed to break up monopolies rather than unions, but was invoked mainly against unions" (search summary: unconfirmed: search summary only). The injunction of 2 July 1894 was issued by federal judges William Woods and Peter S. Grosscup, and about 30 people were killed nationwide (`research/research-work-workers.md` lines 89 and 112). **An injunction** is a court order telling people to stop doing something, and breaking it can mean jail for contempt of court. **"First decade ... more often against unions":** supported as a characterization by the Encyclopedia.com line. No count of cases was found. Say "in its first years, federal lawyers used the Act against a railroad union to end the Pullman boycott, while the Supreme Court let the sugar trust stand".
- **Pullman company-side facts:** the Pullman Palace Car Company cut more than 2,000 workers and cut wages by 25 percent in the 1893 depression without cutting rents in its company town. Company president George M. Pullman refused to meet the workers' delegation. 125,000 to 250,000 railroad workers in 27 states joined the boycott. (Britannica, "Pullman Strike".) `work-workers` leads.
