## 1. Before 1500 — captive-taking, not chattel slavery (THIN)

- Captive-taking existed in some Native societies, and in most of them it was different in kind from hereditary chattel slavery: captives taken in raids could be adopted into families and nations to replace the dead (the Haudenosaunee "mourning war" pattern is the best-described case), enslavement was a status that could end, and it did not automatically pass to children. (Sources: standard ethnohistory summarized in Smithsonian and academic treatments; framing cross-checked against Alan Gallay, *The Indian Slave Trade*, and reviews of it.)
- **Honest exception:** on the Northwest Coast (Tlingit, Haida, and neighbors), slavery was real slavery by any cross-cultural definition — slaves were property, were traded, and **slave status was hereditary**. (Source: Leland Donald, *Aboriginal Slavery on the Northwest Coast of North America*, U. of California Press, 1997, and its academic reviews.)
- Write the difference plainly and carry the exception; do not flatten either way.
- No documented named individual — no story block for this era.

## 2. The 1500s — the first enslavement here was Spanish (THIN)

- **Native people were the first victims.** Spanish raiders took captives from the Florida and Carolina coasts from the early 1500s; under the **encomienda** system Spain forced Native labor and tribute in its colonies. The reform "New Laws" of 1542 curbed the encomienda on paper; forced Native labor continued in practice. (Sources: Britannica "encomienda"; Smithsonian Magazine on Indigenous enslavement.)
- **The first enslaved Africans in what is now the United States, 1526.** Lucas Vázquez de Ayllón's colony of **San Miguel de Gualdape** (on the Georgia or Carolina coast; the exact site is not known) included about **100 enslaved Africans** among 600–700 colonists. In **October 1526**, after Ayllón died and the colony collapsed into infighting, the enslaved Africans rebelled and escaped — **the first documented rebellion of enslaved Africans in what became the United States.** Historians believe the escapees found refuge among nearby Native communities (likely Guale). The colony failed within months. (Sources: BlackPast "San Miguel de Gualdape Slave Rebellion (1526)"; Zinn Education Project TDIH; Wikipedia cross-checked.)
- **St. Augustine, founded September 8, 1565**, included Africans, both free and enslaved, among its first residents — craftsmen, farmers, and domestic workers under Spanish law, which (unlike later English law) recognized certain rights for enslaved people. (Sources: NPS Castillo de San Marcos "African Americans in St. Augustine 1565–1821"; Museum of Florida History.)
- **Estevanico** — enslaved North African (from Azemmour, Morocco); survived the wrecked Narváez expedition (1528) and the eight-year crossing to Mexico with Cabeza de Vaca (1528–1536); later guided the Niza party north and was killed at a Zuni town in 1539. His **journey** is `exploration`'s (verified in `research-exploration.md`); his **status** — an enslaved man doing the advance work of empire — is this chapter's. No known film about him. (Sources: research-exploration.md; NPS.)

## 3. The 1600s — a system built piece by piece, then written into law (FULL)

### 1619 and the Middle Passage (this chapter owns the full telling)
- **Late August 1619:** the English privateer ***White Lion*** landed "20. and odd Negroes" (colonist John Rolfe's words) at **Point Comfort** (Old Point Comfort, Hampton), Virginia — people taken from a Portuguese slave ship, originally from the **Ndongo/Kongo region of West Central Africa**. **Date disagreement:** NPS and most accounts say "late August"; History.com gives August 20; Hampton historian Beth Austin argues about August 25. State the range. (Sources: NPS; National Geographic; Beth Austin/Hampton History Museum — all previously verified in `research-immigration.md`.)
- Whether the 1619 arrivals were treated as servants or slaves is debated; the legal category "slave" did not yet exist in Virginia law. That question belongs here (the system side), and the honest answer is that the records do not settle it.
- **The Middle Passage, the numbers (SlaveVoyages / Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade Database):** about **12.5 million Africans were embarked** on Atlantic slave ships over the whole trade; about **10.7 million survived** the crossing — roughly **1.8 million people died at sea**. **Fewer than 4 percent** of survivors came to mainland North America: roughly **305,000 landed directly from Africa**, close to **388,000 in total** counting arrivals by way of the Caribbean. Most captives went to the Caribbean and Brazil.
- **The conditions:** captives were chained below decks in spaces too low to stand, on voyages that commonly took one to three months; disease, dehydration, and suicide killed steadily; the dead were thrown overboard. (Sources: SlaveVoyages; NMAAHC Middle Passage materials; Equiano's and later survivors' accounts as primary sources.)

### From servitude to hereditary slavery — the legal hinge
- **John Punch, 1640 (Virginia):** three servants of Hugh Gwyn ran away together and were caught. The court sentenced the two European men to four added years of service; **Punch, an African, was sentenced to serve "for the time of his natural Life"** — the first documented lifetime slavery sentence in Virginia, and the first recorded legal distinction by race among the three. (Sources: Encyclopedia Virginia; the July 9, 1640 court record; 1619education.org teaching documents.)
- **Johnson v. Parker, 1654–1655 (Northampton County, Virginia):** **Anthony Johnson** — an African who had arrived about 1621, gained freedom, and become a landowner on the Eastern Shore — sued his white neighbor Robert Parker over the labor of **John Casor**, a Black man who said his indenture had expired. In **1655 the court ruled Casor was Johnson's servant for life** — the first judicial ruling in the English mainland colonies that a person who had committed no crime could be held for life. The complexity is real and should be told: a free Black landowner used the hardening system, and the system then hardened around men like him. (Sources: Britannica Kids "John Casor"; Wikipedia "Anthony Johnson (colonist)" and "John Casor," cross-checked; 1619education.org.)
- **Massachusetts, 1641:** the **Body of Liberties** (passed December 10, 1641) made Massachusetts Bay **the first English mainland colony to give slavery statutory recognition** — its liberties clause carved out "lawful captives taken in just wars, and such strangers as willingly sell themselves or are sold to us." Slavery was a New England institution too. (Sources: Massachusetts Historical Society; NPS "Slavery and Law in 17th Century Massachusetts"; mass.gov.)
- **Virginia, 1662 — *partus sequitur ventrem*:** the House of Burgesses ruled that a child's status **follows the mother** — a deliberate break from English common law, which traced status through the father. A child born to an enslaved mother was enslaved for life, whoever the father was. Slavery now reproduced itself. (Sources: Encyclopedia Virginia; Wikipedia "Partus sequitur ventrem," cross-checked.)
- **Virginia, 1705 — the slave code:** the General Assembly consolidated decades of piecemeal laws into one sweeping code: enslaved people were real property; baptism did not free anyone; masters who killed an enslaved person while "correcting" them faced no penalty. The ambiguities of the 1620s were gone. (Sources: Encyclopedia Virginia; LegalClarity summary cross-checked against the statute text.)
- **Anti-literacy thread — 1600s finding (clears the seed's VERIFY):** no colony passed a statute against teaching enslaved people in the 1600s. **The first statutory ban came in South Carolina in 1740** (see era 4). The 1600s codes controlled bodies and status, not yet books.

## 4. 1700 to 1750 — rice wealth, harder codes, the first mass revolt, and a place to run TO (FULL)

### Rice and the Lowcountry
- South Carolina's rice economy was built on the knowledge and labor of West Africans from rice-growing regions; planters paid premiums for captives from those coasts. Charleston became the largest mainland arrival port. (Sources: SlaveVoyages; Lowcountry Digital History Initiative; previously verified in `research-immigration.md`.)
- **Sullivan's Island** in Charleston harbor served as the quarantine ("pest house") station for arriving slave ships. A widely repeated figure calls it the entry point for about 40 percent of enslaved Africans brought to mainland North America; **some historians (e.g., Nic Butler, Charleston County Public Library) argue the Sullivan's-Island-specific share is overstated** since many ships landed elsewhere in the harbor. Carry Charleston's leading role; flag the island-specific percentage as disputed. (Sources: Wikipedia "Sullivan's Island"; Charleston Time Machine podcast/essays.)

### The Stono Rebellion, September 9, 1739
- About **20 enslaved men led by "Jemmy"** (described in a contemporary account as Angolan) seized weapons at the Stono River south of Charleston and marched south — **toward Spanish Florida and its promise of freedom** — gathering recruits, with banners and drums. **About 25 white colonists were killed; the militia killed roughly 35 to 50 of the rebels** in the fighting and the hunt that followed (counts vary; state the range). It was the largest slave uprising in the mainland colonies before the Revolution. (Sources: LOC Today in History Sept. 9; Britannica; BlackPast; CCPL Charleston Time Machine.)
- **The Negro Act of May 10, 1740** answered Stono: it barred enslaved people from assembling, growing their own food, earning money, moving freely — and made it illegal to teach an enslaved person **to write** (reading was not named). This is the **first anti-literacy statute** in the future United States; the reading/writing distinction matters and should be stated. (Sources: Wikipedia "Negro Act of 1740" cross-checked; Zinn Education Project TDIH May 10; the academic literature on slave literacy and the 1740 act.) **Clears the seed's VERIFY on the 1740 act.**

### Fort Mose, 1738 — freedom by running south
- Spain's king had decreed (1693) freedom for enslaved people escaping British colonies who reached Florida, accepted Catholicism, and (for men) served in the militia. In **1738** Florida's governor Manuel Montiano established **Gracia Real de Santa Teresa de Mose** — **Fort Mose** — about two miles north of St. Augustine: **the first legally sanctioned free Black town in what is now the United States**, roughly 100 people in more than 20 households. Destroyed in the British siege of 1740 (the battle of "Bloody Mose"), rebuilt in 1752, evacuated to Cuba with the Spanish in 1763. Today it is Fort Mose Historic State Park. (Sources: NPS; Fort Mose Historical Society; BlackPast; Encyclopedia.com.)
- **Francisco Menéndez** — the fort's militia captain: a **Mandinga man born in the Gambia region of Africa**, carried to Carolina in slavery, escaped to Florida, baptized Francisco Menéndez, commanded the free Black militia at Mose. Documented in Spanish colonial records. The best-documented named resident; no known film. (Sources: enslaved.org profile; Fort Mose Historical Society; BlackPast.)

### Native people were enslaved too (shared with `native-nations`)
- English Carolina ran a large **trade in enslaved Native people**: between 1670 and 1715, an estimated **24,000 to 51,000 Native people of the Southeast** were enslaved (Alan Gallay's range), and in that period **more Native slaves were exported from Charleston than Africans were imported there**. Many were shipped to the Caribbean. (Sources: Alan Gallay, *The Indian Slave Trade* (2002) and academic reviews; EBSCO; Lowcountry Digital History Initiative.) `native-nations` carries what this did to the nations; this chapter states plainly that the system enslaved Native people as well as Africans.

## 5. 1750 to 1800 — liberty declared, property defended (FULL)

### The Revolution's two promises
- **Crispus Attucks**, a sailor of African and Wampanoag descent, was the first person killed at the **Boston Massacre, March 5, 1770**. Most accounts say he had escaped slavery in Framingham around 1750; **historians disagree over whether he was legally free or a fugitive** — say so. (Sources: Boston.gov; Crispus Attucks Museum; Wikipedia cross-checked.)
- **Dunmore's Proclamation, November 7, 1775:** Virginia's royal governor offered freedom to enslaved men of rebel masters who joined the British. About 300 joined his "Ethiopian Regiment" within a month (it grew to about 800); the proclamation's news carried far beyond Virginia. Over the whole war an estimated **20,000 Black people sided with or fled to the British**; an estimated **9,000 served the Patriot side** (about 5,000 of them as combat soldiers). Both promises of freedom were kept unevenly. (Sources: Gilder Lehrman; Encyclopedia Virginia; Mount Vernon; American Battlefield Trust "10 Facts: Black Patriots"; Wikipedia "Black Patriot"/"Black Loyalist" cross-checked.)
- **Freedom suits:** enslaved people sued. Massachusetts cases — Elizabeth Freeman's (below) and the Quock Walker cases (1781–1783) — ended slavery there through the courts, using the 1780 state constitution's "all men are born free and equal." (Sources: Massachusetts Historical Society; Boston Bar Association.)

### The first abolitions — gradual, partial, real
- **Vermont's 1777 constitution** (an independent republic until 1791) banned holding adults in slavery — the first such constitutional ban. **Pennsylvania's Act for the Gradual Abolition of Slavery, March 1, 1780**, was the first abolition statute passed by an elected legislature anywhere: children born after the act would be freed at 28. Massachusetts ended slavery by court decision (1781–1783); Connecticut and Rhode Island passed gradual laws in 1784; New York in 1799 (fully free 1827); New Jersey last in 1804. Gradual meant slow: people remained enslaved in the North for decades. The **Northwest Ordinance (July 13, 1787)** banned slavery north of the Ohio River. (Sources: NPS "PA Gradual Abolition Act"; EBSCO; verified Northwest Ordinance facts in `research-migration.md`.)

### The Constitution's compromises, 1787
- Three protections for slavery, without ever using the word: the **three-fifths clause** (Art. I, §2 — enslaved people counted as three-fifths of a person for a state's representation), the **fugitive clause** (Art. IV, §2 — escapees "held to Service or Labour" must be delivered up), and the **slave-trade clause** (Art. I, §9 — Congress barred from ending importation before **1808**). (Sources: National Archives; National Constitution Center; previously verified in `research-immigration.md`.)
- The 1808 clock ran out on schedule: the **Act Prohibiting Importation of Slaves**, signed March 2, 1807, took effect **January 1, 1808**. (Verified in `research-immigration.md`.)

### People (verified)
- **Elizabeth Freeman ("Mum Bett"):** enslaved in Colonel John Ashley's household in Sheffield, Massachusetts; sued with a man named Brom, argued by attorney Theodore Sedgwick that the 1780 Massachusetts constitution's "all men are born free and equal" outlawed slavery. **In August 1781 a Great Barrington jury freed them** and awarded 30 shillings damages — the case (*Brom and Bett v. Ashley*) helped end slavery in Massachusetts. She took the name Elizabeth Freeman and worked as a paid housekeeper and nurse; died 1829. No known feature film — leave the Movie line out. (Sources: National Constitution Center; National Women's History Museum; Massachusetts court records via Digital Commonwealth.)
- **Phillis Wheatley:** kidnapped from West Africa as a child of about seven, landed in Boston in 1761 on the slave ship *Phillis* (her imposed name). Her ***Poems on Various Subjects, Religious and Moral*** was published in **London on September 1, 1773** — **the first book of poetry published by an African American**. The Wheatleys freed her by **November 1773**, shortly after the book appeared. She married John Peters (1778) and **died in poverty in 1784**, about age 31. No known feature film. (Sources: Museum of the American Revolution; National Women's History Museum; Poetry Foundation; Wikipedia cross-checked.)
- **Ona Judge:** enslaved attendant to Martha Washington. On **May 21, 1796**, while the Washingtons were at dinner in the President's House in Philadelphia, she walked out and took a sloop, the *Nancy*, to Portsmouth, New Hampshire. A **runaway advertisement ran in the Philadelphia Gazette on May 24, 1796** offering ten dollars for her. George Washington pursued her for years; she refused an offer to return, married, and lived free — poor but, as she said, free — until **1848**. Her own words survive in **interviews she gave in the 1840s** (1845, *The Granite Freeman*). No feature film (the 2017 book *Never Caught* is the standard account). (Sources: Mount Vernon; White House Historical Association; NPS "1796 Ona Judge ad"; Zinn TDIH.)

## 6. 1800 to 1850 — cotton makes slavery bigger, richer, more brutal (FULL)

### The scale
- Census counts: **697,624 enslaved people in 1790 → 3,953,760 in 1860** — growth mainly by birth after the 1808 import ban. By 1860 the South grew about **75 percent of the world's cotton**, and cotton made up **more than half of U.S. exports by value**. (Sources: U.S. Census (1790, 1860); census.gov history pages; Wikipedia census articles cross-checked.)

### The domestic slave trade (this chapter leads on the system; `migration` tells the journey — ruling 2)
- **More than 1 million people** were moved from the Upper South to the Deep South cotton lands between about 1790 and 1860 — about 200,000 a decade from 1820 — overland in chained **coffles** and by coastwise ship to **New Orleans**, the country's largest slave market. (Movement numbers verified in `research-migration.md`: EJI; PBS "Many Rivers to Cross.")
- **What the system did to families — our lead:** people were property, and sale broke families as a matter of routine. The Equal Justice Initiative's accounting: the domestic trade **separated nearly half of all the people it moved from a spouse or a parent**; estimates for the Upper South run to more than half separated from a parent or child, and about **one marriage in three destroyed** by forced sale. (Sources: EJI "Domestic Slave Trade" and "Black Families Severed by Slavery.")
- Children were sold away from mothers; the "Information Wanted" ads of the 1860s–1900s (era 7) are the after-record of these separations.

### Daily life under slavery (labor, food, housing, family, worship, music, the threat of sale)
- **Labor:** sunup to sundown, six days a week; **gang system** on cotton and sugar (driven in rows under an overseer), **task system** in Lowcountry rice (a set task, then your own time). (Sources: NMAAHC; Lowcountry Digital History Initiative.)
- **Food and clothing, from a primary source:** Frederick Douglass's *Narrative* (1845) records the monthly food allowance on the Maryland plantation of his childhood — **eight pounds of pork or the equivalent in fish, and one bushel of corn meal** — and a yearly clothing issue so thin that children too young for field work often had only a long shirt. Enslaved people stretched rations with gardens, fishing, and trapping where allowed. (Source: Douglass, *Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass* (1845), chs. 1–2 — primary.)
- **Housing:** one-room cabins, dirt floors, board beds; the WPA narratives (era 8) describe them from memory. (Sources: WPA narratives, LOC "Born in Slavery"; NMAAHC's preserved Point of Pines cabin.)
- **Family and the threat of sale:** marriages had no legal standing; the auction block could end any family any day — the fact that organized daily life more than any other. (Sources: EJI; WPA narratives.)
- **Worship and music:** independent Black churches in the free states — **Richard Allen**, born enslaved, founded the **African Methodist Episcopal Church in Philadelphia, formally organized 1816**, the first Black denomination in the country. In the slave states, worship out of the owners' sight in brush arbors ("hush harbors"); the spirituals carried both faith and coded meaning. (Sources: Britannica "African Methodist Episcopal Church"; Mother Bethel AME; NMAAHC on sacred music.)

### Reading made a crime (this chapter leads; `education` notes the schooling side)
- **First ban: South Carolina 1740** (writing only — see era 4). **Georgia followed in 1755.** Then the wave after Nat Turner and the abolition press: **Georgia 1829** (December 19); **Louisiana and North Carolina 1830**; **Virginia 1831**; **Alabama 1832**; **South Carolina tightened again in 1834**. **Most, but not all, slave states had such laws.** Penalties differed: white teachers faced fines (North Carolina $200; Georgia and others fines and jail); Black teachers and learners faced the whip (North Carolina: 39 lashes; Virginia: up to 20 lashes for those caught at a school). Several laws covered free Black people as well as enslaved. (Sources: Encyclopedia.com "Literacy and Anti-Literacy Laws"; Encyclopedia Virginia "Literacy and Education of the Enslaved in Virginia"; state statute texts via the anti-literacy-law literature.) **Clears the seed's VERIFY on states/years/penalties.**
- **Why:** literate people could forge travel passes, read abolition newspapers, and read the Bible without an owner's editing; after 1831 the fear was organized revolt. (Sources: History.com "How Literacy Became a Powerful Weapon"; Heather Andrea Williams, *Self-Taught*.)
- **How many learned anyway:** historians estimate roughly **5 to 10 percent** of enslaved people could read by 1860 (some estimates for the late period run higher, up to 20 percent — state the spread). (Sources: History.com citing the scholarship; Encyclopedia Virginia.)
- **How (verified methods only; the "pit school" claim could not be verified and is dropped):**
  - **Frederick Douglass's own account** (*Narrative*, 1845, chs. 6–7): Sophia Auld in Baltimore taught him the alphabet until her husband Hugh forbade it — Douglass heard the prohibition itself as "the first decidedly antislavery lecture"; he then traded bread with hungry white boys for lessons and, at about twelve, bought *The Columbian Orator*. **This is the best-known first-person source; use the *Narrative* passage, 1845 edition.** (Clears the seed's VERIFY on the passage and edition.)
  - **Milla Granson (Lilly Ann Granderson)**, enslaved in Natchez, Mississippi, ran a **"midnight school"** — about twelve pupils at a time, taught between roughly midnight and 2 a.m., graduating one group and taking another; accounts credit her with teaching about 200 people, some of whom wrote their own passes and escaped. **Sourcing note:** the account traces mainly to abolitionist Laura Haviland's memoir (1881) and later compilations — one primary source; tell it with that honesty. (Sources: Notable Kentucky African Americans Database (U. of Kentucky); Natchez Democrat.)
  - **Susie King Taylor** (era 7 story) learned in **secret schools in Savannah** as a child in the 1850s — documented in her own 1902 memoir.

### Resistance — from slow-downs to revolt
- Everyday resistance is documented across the WPA narratives and planters' own complaints: slowed work, broken tools, feigned illness, food taken from smokehouses, and above all **running away** — the runaway advertisements that fill Southern newspapers are the system's own record of it (the Freedom on the Move project at Cornell is digitizing thousands; ads often note that the person "can read" or "can write and may forge a pass" — the literacy thread in the system's own words). (Sources: WPA narratives; Freedom on the Move / freedomonthemove.org; NMAAHC.)
- **Gabriel's conspiracy, 1800 (Richmond):** an enslaved blacksmith planned a march on Richmond; betrayed hours before, and a storm scattered the gathering; **26 men were hanged**, including Gabriel. (Sources: Encyclopedia Virginia "Gabriel's Conspiracy (1800)".)
- **Denmark Vesey, 1822 (Charleston):** a free Black carpenter's planned rising was betrayed; **35 men were executed** and dozens deported, and Charleston's independent **AME church was torn down** — the congregation that later became Mother Emanuel. (Sources: PBS Africans in America; Wikipedia "Denmark Vesey" cross-checked.)
- **Nat Turner's rebellion, August 21–23, 1831 (Southampton County, Virginia):** Turner, an enslaved preacher, led the deadliest slave revolt in U.S. history; **at least 55 white people were killed**. In the panic afterward militias and vigilantes **killed more than 100 Black people, many of whom had no part in it** (some estimates run to 200 — state the range). About 30 were tried in Southampton; **19 of those convicted were executed**. Turner evaded capture until October 30 and was **hanged November 11, 1831**. His jailhouse account was published by lawyer Thomas R. Gray as *The Confessions of Nat Turner* (1831). Virginia and other states answered with the anti-literacy and anti-assembly wave above. (Sources: Encyclopedia Virginia "Nat Turner's Revolt"; Gilder Lehrman; Wikipedia cross-checked.)
- ***The Amistad*, 1839–1842:** Spanish slavers shipped kidnapped Africans (mostly Mende, taken from the Sierra Leone region) to Cuba; 53 of them, bought for a Cuban plantation, **seized the schooner *Amistad* on July 1, 1839**, led by **Sengbe Pieh (called Joseph Cinqué)**. Captured off Long Island, they were jailed and tried; **the U.S. Supreme Court freed them March 9, 1841** (argued in part by former president John Quincy Adams), and 35 survivors **returned to Sierra Leone in 1842**. They were never enslaved on U.S. soil — the story here is the freedom fight in U.S. courts. (Sources: National Archives "The Amistad Case"; NPS "Sengbe Pieh"; Britannica.)

### The Underground Railroad and the abolitionists
- The Underground Railroad — the informal network of guides, safe houses, and above all Black communities and churches — is currently estimated to have helped about **100,000 people** escape over its decades; the number is an estimate, and many more tried and were caught. (Sources: NPS Network to Freedom materials; NCpedia; the estimate is widely used and should be labeled an estimate.)
- **Harriet Tubman:** escaped from Maryland's Eastern Shore **September 1849**; returned repeatedly — **about 13 trips, bringing out about 70 people**, mostly family and friends, per her own statements and the documented record (**the old "19 trips, 300 people" figure is a legend; state the correction**). Later a Union scout (era 7). (Sources: NPS Tubman fact sheet "Myths and Facts"; Britannica; Zinn TDIH.)
- **Frederick Douglass:** born **February 1818** in Talbot County, Maryland (the exact date unrecorded; he kept February 14); escaped **September 3, 1838**, by train through Baltimore in a sailor's clothes with borrowed seaman's papers, helped by **Anna Murray**, the free Black woman he married. *Narrative* published **1845**; founded the *North Star* **1847**; the century's most photographed and most heard abolitionist voice; died 1895. (Sources: LOC Frederick Douglass Papers timeline; History.com; Britannica.)
- **Free Black institutions:** ***Freedom's Journal*** (New York, first issue **March 16, 1827**) — the first Black-owned newspaper in the U.S., founded by Samuel Cornish and John Russwurm. The AME church (1816, above). Northern free Black communities ran the vigilance committees that made the Underground Railroad work. (Sources: LOC; Britannica; BlackPast.)
- **Sojourner Truth:** born Isabella Baumfree about 1797 in Ulster County, New York; **walked away from her enslaver with her infant daughter in 1826**, the year before New York's emancipation took effect (July 4, 1827). When her five-year-old son **Peter was illegally sold south to Alabama**, she sued — and **won his return in 1828**, becoming (per the Historical Society of the New York Courts) **the first Black woman to win a lawsuit of this kind against a white man in a U.S. court**. Took the name Sojourner Truth in 1843; her *Narrative* published 1850; her 1851 Akron women's-rights speech belongs to `rights-movements` — here she is the woman who sued slavery for her son and won. No known feature film. (Sources: Historical Society of the New York Courts; NPS; NYPL/Schomburg on the court records.)
- **Solomon Northup:** born free in Minerva, New York, 1808; a farmer and fiddler; **drugged and kidnapped in Washington, D.C., in 1841**, sold in New Orleans, held twelve years on Louisiana cotton and sugar plantations; **freed January 4, 1853**, after a letter reached his friends; his memoir ***Twelve Years a Slave* (1853)** sold about 30,000 copies. His kidnappers were never convicted. **After about 1857 he disappears from the record; where and when he died is not known — say so.** Film: ***12 Years a Slave* (2013)**, dir. Steve McQueen — Academy Award for Best Picture. (Sources: Documenting the American South (UNC); Britannica; Smithsonian Magazine; History.com.)

## 7. 1850 to 1900 — THE HEART: the system's peak, the break, and what freedom actually looked like (FULL — longest section in the book)

### The crisis
- **Fugitive Slave Act, September 18, 1850:** federal commissioners decided cases with **no jury** and the accused **could not testify**; the commissioner was paid **$10 for ruling a person a slave, $5 for ruling them free**; bystanders could be compelled to help catch people. It reached into the free states — free Black Northerners fled to Canada. (Sources: LOC (act text); American Battlefield Trust; Social Welfare History Project.)
- **Anthony Burns, 1854 (Boston):** escaped from Virginia by stowing away; arrested May 24, 1854; after a week's hearing, on **June 2, 1854, federal troops marched him through Boston to a ship back to Virginia** past crowds draped in mourning black. The rendition radicalized New England. (Sources: Encyclopedia Virginia; Massachusetts Historical Society; Boston.gov archives.)
- ***Dred Scott v. Sandford*, March 6, 1857:** the Supreme Court ruled **7–2** against Dred and Harriet Scott — Black Americans **were not and could never be citizens**, and Congress could not ban slavery in the territories; Chief Justice Roger Taney wrote that Black people "had no rights which the white man was bound to respect." (Sources: National Archives milestone document; Britannica; EBSCO.)

### The war — freedom taken, not merely given
- **Fort Monroe, May 23–24, 1861:** three enslaved men — **Frank Baker, Shepard Mallory, and James Townsend** — rowed to the Union-held fort at Old Point Comfort (the same ground as 1619) rather than be taken south to build Confederate batteries. **General Benjamin Butler refused to return them, calling them "contraband of war."** Word spread; people began walking, rowing, and riding to Union lines everywhere. **By war's end roughly half a million people had freed themselves by reaching the lines.** The fort got a new name among the freedom-seekers: **"Freedom's Fortress."** (Sources: NPS "Fort Monroe and the Contrabands of War"; House Divided (Dickinson); Mariners' Museum.)
- **Robert Smalls, May 13, 1862 (Charleston):** an enslaved harbor pilot, age 23, took the Confederate transport ***Planter*** before dawn with an enslaved crew and their families aboard, gave the correct signals past Fort Sumter wearing the captain's straw hat, and surrendered the ship to the Union blockade. Later the first Black captain of a U.S. Army vessel? — accounts style it differently; safest verified line: he became a ship's captain in U.S. service, then a South Carolina legislator and **five-term U.S. congressman**. (Sources: Post and Courier; PBS "Many Rivers to Cross"; Essential Civil War Curriculum.)
- **Emancipation Proclamation:** preliminary September 22, 1862; effective **January 1, 1863**. What it did: declared free the enslaved people of the states in rebellion, turned the Union army into an army of liberation wherever it advanced, and **opened enlistment to Black men**. What it did NOT do: it **exempted the loyal border states (Delaware, Maryland, Kentucky, Missouri), Tennessee, and Union-held parts of Virginia and Louisiana** — freedom there waited for state action or the 13th Amendment. (Sources: National Archives featured document; LOC "Abraham Lincoln and Emancipation.")
- **Black soldiers: about 180,000 men served in the army's U.S. Colored Troops — roughly one Union soldier in ten by war's end — and about 19,000 more in the navy.** Fourteen Black soldiers earned the Medal of Honor in one battle, Chaffin's Farm / New Market Heights (September 29, 1864). (Sources: American Battlefield Trust "The Role of the USCT"; Essential Civil War Curriculum.)
- **Harriet Tubman at Combahee Ferry, June 2, 1863:** guided Union gunboats and the 2nd South Carolina Volunteers up the Combahee River; **more than 700 enslaved people were carried to freedom in one night** — she is regularly described as the first woman to lead a U.S. armed raid (shared with `war`: they own the operation; we own the freeing). (Sources: NMAAHC "The Combahee Ferry Raid"; BlackPast.)
- **Juneteenth:** **June 19, 1865** — Major General **Gordon Granger**, with about 2,000 federal troops, issued **General Order No. 3** at Galveston: "all slaves are free," with "absolute equality of personal rights." **More than 250,000 people were still enslaved in Texas**, two and a half years after the Proclamation, because the army had not been there to enforce it. The date became the freedom anniversary. (Sources: National Archives; Britannica; Texas State Historical Association.)
- **The 13th Amendment:** passed Congress January 31, 1865; **ratified December 6, 1865** — slavery abolished everywhere, **"except as a punishment for crime whereof the party shall have been duly convicted."** The exception clause matters; convict leasing (below) walked through it. (Sources: National Archives milestone document; NMAAHC.)
- **The *Clotilda*'s people:** about 110 Africans smuggled into Mobile in July 1860 (52 years after the ban) were freed five years later; unable to afford passage home, about 30 of them founded **Africatown** north of Mobile — see the Kossola story write-up below. (Verified in `research-immigration.md`; Encyclopedia of Alabama; Smithsonian.)
- **The Five Tribes too (shared with `native-nations`):** the Cherokee, Muscogee (Creek), Choctaw, Chickasaw, and Seminole nations had also held enslaved Black people (the Cherokee Nation as many as 2,500 at emancipation). Their **1866 treaties with the U.S. freed the enslaved and promised the freedpeople citizenship** — the Cherokee treaty's Article 9 promised Freedmen "all the rights of native Cherokees." The modern fight over that promise is in era 10. (Sources: CNN/court coverage of *Cherokee Nation v. Nash*; the 1866 treaty text via NARF.)

### Freedom, day to day — the searches, the marriages, the names, the churches
- **Searching for family:** freedpeople placed **"Information Wanted" advertisements** in Black newspapers for decades, searching for mothers, children, husbands, and wives sold away. The **"Last Seen: Finding Family After Slavery" project (Villanova University, with Mother Bethel AME)** has digitized **more than 5,000** of them at informationwanted.org. Example, verified in the database (item 918): **Thornton Copeland's ad in the *Colored Tennessean* (Nashville), October 1865** — "INFORMATION is wanted of my mother, whom I left in Fauquier county, Va., in 1844, and I was sold in Richmond, Va., to Saml. Copeland." Searches ran into the 1900s; reunions were rare and are recorded with joy when they happened. (Sources: informationwanted.org; Villanova; NHPRC.)
- **Legalizing marriages:** the Freedmen's Bureau recorded thousands of marriages that slavery had refused to recognize — couples married for decades stood up to be married in law. The Bureau's **marriage registers** survive in the National Archives and are a core genealogy source (era 10). (Sources: FamilySearch/NARA Freedmen's Bureau record descriptions.)
- **Choosing names:** freedpeople chose and registered surnames — some kept a former owner's name to stay findable by sold-away family, others chose new names (Freeman among them). Documented across Bureau records and the WPA narratives. (Sources: Bureau records; WPA narratives.)
- **Churches:** congregations that had met in hush harbors built their own buildings and joined or founded Black denominations; the AME and AME Zion churches grew across the South within a decade of the war. The church became the freedpeople's school hall, meeting hall, and political hall. (Sources: NMAAHC; Britannica AME.)
- **The Freedman's Savings Bank — a promise broken early:** chartered 1865 for freedpeople's savings; mismanaged and wrecked by speculation; **failed in June 1874, taking about $3 million belonging to some 61,000 depositors**. (Sources: OCC history "The Freedman's Savings Bank"; NPR; NMAAHC Searchable Museum.)

### Schools, the moment freedom came (this chapter: what schooling meant to freedpeople; `education` leads on the school system)
- The demand came **with** freedom, immediately: schools opened in churches, army camps, and confiscated buildings as soon as Union lines arrived — **Susie King Taylor was openly teaching freed children on St. Simons Island, Georgia, in 1862, at age 14**, while the war still ran (see her story below). Grandparents and grandchildren learned in the same rooms; adults filled night classes after full workdays. (Sources: Taylor's memoir (1902, primary); Freedmen's Bureau education records.)
- Freedpeople paid for it themselves where they could — school fees out of first wages, land and labor for schoolhouses — alongside Northern aid societies (the **American Missionary Association** foremost) whose teachers, many of them women and a growing number of them Black, came south by the thousands. (Sources: Bureau education-division records (NARA/Smithsonian SOVA); AMA history via Fisk founding accounts.)
- **The Freedmen's Bureau's schools: by 1870, more than 2,600 schools with about 150,000 pupils** (a count of day and night schools; Bureau-era reports that counted every kind of school give higher totals — state the count and its scope rather than one flat number). (Sources: Bureau records as summarized in standard references; John W. Alvord's semi-annual reports on schools for freedmen, archive.org.)
- **The first Black colleges:** a few predated the war (Cheyney's forerunner 1837; Lincoln, Pa., 1854; Wilberforce 1856 — `education` carries these as institutions); the emancipation wave founded **Fisk (Nashville, opened January 1866)**, **Howard (chartered by Congress, 1867)**, and about **25 institutions of higher learning for freedpeople by 1872**, most with Bureau and AMA support. (Sources: Fisk founding accounts; Encyclopedia.com; Freedmen's Bureau histories.)
- **Schools burned:** in the **Memphis Massacre, May 1–3, 1866**, white mobs and police killed 46 Black residents and burned **91 homes, 4 churches, and 8 schools — every Black church and school in the city** (the counts are from the congressional investigating committee). School burnings and attacks on teachers recur through Bureau records. (Sources: congressional committee report figures via Britannica/Tennessee Encyclopedia/Zinn TDIH.)
- **Freedpeople wrote schooling into law:** the Reconstruction state constitutions of 1868–1869 — drafted by the first public bodies in U.S. history with substantial Black membership — **created the South's first statewide free public school systems**, funded by earmarked taxes and open to white children too. (Sources: Yale Law Journal, "Reconstruction State Constitutional Conventions and the Rebirth of American Schooling"; Facing South; American Yawp.) **Clears the seed's VERIFY on this claim.**

### Black political power
- In the decade after the 15th Amendment (ratified February 3, 1870), **16 Black men served in Congress — 14 representatives and 2 senators**, all Southern Republicans. **Hiram Revels** (Mississippi) was sworn in as **the first Black U.S. senator on February 25, 1870**; **Joseph Rainey** (South Carolina) became the first Black U.S. representative December 12, 1870; **Blanche K. Bruce** (Mississippi) was the first Black senator to serve a **full term (1875–1881)**. **Robert Smalls** served five terms in the House. (Sources: U.S. House history office "Black Americans in Congress"; Senate.gov; govinfo.)
- Across the South, **about 2,000 Black men held elective or appointive office during Reconstruction; Eric Foner's directory *Freedom's Lawmakers* documents more than 1,500 of them by name** — state both the estimate and the documented count. (Sources: LSU Press/Foner, *Freedom's Lawmakers* (1993); H-Net review.)

### The promises broken (handoff to `rights-movements` at 1877)
- **"Forty acres":** Sherman's **Special Field Orders No. 15 (January 16, 1865)** set aside about **400,000 acres** of coastal land from Charleston to northern Florida for freedpeople in forty-acre plots; about **40,000 people** settled. **President Andrew Johnson reversed the order in fall 1865** and returned the land to the former owners. The phrase "forty acres and a mule" carries the memory. (Sources: New Georgia Encyclopedia; BlackPast; Freedmen & Southern Society Project (order text).)
- **Black Codes:** Mississippi first, **November 1865**, then other states — vagrancy laws requiring proof of employment, apprenticeship laws binding Black children, licenses controlling work; arrest and hired-out labor for violators. Congress answered with the Civil Rights Act of 1866 and the 14th Amendment (ratified July 9, 1868). (Sources: Mississippi Encyclopedia; American Yawp primary texts.)
- **Sharecropping:** without land, freedpeople farmed shares of landlords' fields against advances for tools, seed, and food at plantation-store prices — a debt that rolled over year after year and bound families to the land. (Sources: EJI; standard Reconstruction references.)
- **Convict leasing:** through the 13th Amendment's crime exception, states arrested Black men under the new codes and **leased them to planters, mines, and railroads** — labor without wages, discipline without limit, profitable to the states for decades. (Sources: PBS/Blackmon, *Slavery by Another Name* (Pulitzer-winning book; PBS film 2012); 13th Amendment text.)
- **Terror:** the **Ku Klux Klan, founded December 1865 in Pulaski, Tennessee** by Confederate veterans, ran campaigns of whipping, arson, and murder against freedpeople and their allies; the **Colfax Massacre (Louisiana, April 13, 1873)** killed between **62 and 153 Black men**, most after surrender — Foner calls it the bloodiest single act of Reconstruction-era racial violence (the count itself is uncertain; state the range). The Enforcement Acts (1870–71) briefly broke the Klan; the will to enforce faded. (Sources: NPS; EJI calendar; Britannica; Journal of the Civil War Era on the Colfax count.)
- ***Plessy v. Ferguson*, May 18, 1896** (7–1, Harlan alone in dissent): "separate but equal" blessed the segregation statutes; the decision's date sits in this era, its shadow over the next. (Sources: National Archives; Britannica.)
- **The end:** the disputed 1876 election; the **Compromise of 1877**; federal troops withdrawn from the last Southern statehouses (New Orleans, April 24, 1877). Reconstruction ended with the amendments on paper and the power to enforce them surrendered. **From this point the campaigns to win the rights back belong to `rights-movements`.** (Sources: EBSCO; EJI calendar; 64 Parishes.)

## 8. 1900 to 1950 — the replacement system at full strength; the last survivors speak (FULL)

- **Jim Crow as a working system:** segregation ordered daily life — schools, railroad cars, waiting rooms, water fountains, courtrooms, cemeteries — under the shelter of *Plessy* (1896). This chapter tells the system as slavery's successor; the campaigns against it are `rights-movements`'.
- **Disfranchisement by design:** Mississippi's 1890 constitution wrote the model (poll tax, literacy test, understanding clause); Louisiana's 1898 constitution added the grandfather clause. **Louisiana's registered Black voters fell from 130,344 in 1896 to 1,342 by 1904.** (Sources: EJI calendar May 12; 64 Parishes "Louisiana Constitution of 1898.")
- **Lynching as terror:** the Equal Justice Initiative documents **more than 4,400 racial terror lynchings between 1877 and 1950**; other counts using different periods and definitions differ — name the source with the number. Lynching enforced the labor and caste system and drove the Great Migration (the migration itself is `migration`'s; the terror is ours). (Sources: EJI, *Lynching in America*.)
- **Forced labor continued:** convict leasing and debt peonage ran deep into the 20th century — documented in Douglas Blackmon's Pulitzer-winning *Slavery by Another Name* and the 2012 PBS film of the same name. (Sources: Blackmon; PBS.)
- **The last survivors' voices:**
  - The **WPA Federal Writers' Project, 1936–38**, interviewed formerly enslaved people across 17 states: **more than 2,300 first-person accounts and 500 photographs**, held at the Library of Congress as **"Born in Slavery."** (Source: LOC digital collection introduction.)
  - Separately, the Library of Congress preserves **actual sound recordings** of people born in slavery ("Voices Remembering Slavery," recordings made 1932–1975) — among them **Fountain Hughes** (story below).
  - **Zora Neale Hurston interviewed Oluale Kossola in 1927**; her book *Barracoon* waited until **2018** for publication. (Verified in `research-immigration.md`; the Kossola story block sits in era 7 where his enslavement, freedom, and Africatown belong.)

## 9. 1950 to 2000 — making the amendments mean what they say (FULL, kept narrow by design)

- **The frame (keep to it):** the movement's own story belongs to `rights-movements`. This chapter's angle: the 13th, 14th, and 15th Amendments were on the books for a century before the country enforced them.
- ***Brown v. Board of Education*, May 17, 1954** — unanimous; "separate educational facilities are inherently unequal," overturning *Plessy*'s doctrine in schooling. (Sources: National Archives.)
- **Civil Rights Act, July 2, 1964** and **Voting Rights Act, August 6, 1965** — the 14th and 15th Amendments finally given enforcement machinery, ninety-nine years after the 13th. (Sources: National Archives; standard references.)
- **Mississippi and the 13th:** the state legislature voted to ratify the 13th Amendment only in **1995** — and the paperwork was never filed; after two residents discovered the omission in 2012, the ratification was **officially recorded February 7, 2013**. (Sources: EJI calendar Mar. 16; CBS/ABC News, journalism — labeled.)
- **Finding the families — Roots:** Alex Haley's *Roots* (book, 1976; Pulitzer special award 1977) and the **ABC miniseries (January 1977), watched by an estimated 130 million people** — an audience record for a series — set off a boom in Black family genealogy. **The disputes, told honestly:** Haley settled a plagiarism suit by **Harold Courlander for $650,000 (1978)** over passages taken from Courlander's novel *The African*, and professional genealogists have challenged links in the documentary chain of the family story. The book's power and its problems are both part of the record. (Sources: EBSCO; Washington Post retrospective; Tennessee Encyclopedia — journalism/reference, labeled.)

## 10. 2000 to today — memory, argument, and the records opened (FULL; current through 2026)

- **Memory built in stone:** the Smithsonian's **National Museum of African American History and Culture opened September 24, 2016** (about 400,000 square feet; its history galleries begin with the slave trade and hold objects including a preserved slave cabin). The **International African American Museum opened June 27, 2023, in Charleston at Gadsden's Wharf**, a principal disembarkation site. (Sources: Smithsonian newsdesk; IAAM/NPR.)
- **Juneteenth:** the **Juneteenth National Independence Day Act was signed June 17, 2021** (Public Law 117-17), making June 19 a federal holiday. `holidays` carries how it is celebrated; this chapter carries what is being remembered. (Sources: govinfo; White House archive.)
- **Reparations debated:** **H.R. 40** (a commission to study reparations, named for the forty acres) was introduced by John Conyers every Congress from 1989 and continues under successors; the House Judiciary Committee advanced it for the first time in 2021. **Evanston, Illinois** created the first city-funded program (2019 resolution; first $25,000 housing grants approved March 2021, from a $10 million fund, for victims of the city's 1919–1969 housing discrimination). **California's task force** delivered its final report **June 29, 2023** — more than 115 recommendations, including a formal apology. (Sources: Congress.gov; FCNL/ACLU; Evanston city records/CNN; California DOJ — mix of government and journalism, labeled.)
- **Universities and their ledgers — the GU272:** in **1838 the Maryland Jesuits sold 272 enslaved people** to Louisiana plantations, in part to keep Georgetown College solvent — among the largest and best-documented mass sales in U.S. history. Georgetown announced descendant admissions preference and building renamings in **2016**; **more than 8,000 descendants** have been identified; in **2021** the Jesuits and descendant leaders created the **Descendants Truth & Reconciliation Foundation** with a **$100 million pledge** — the largest such commitment by a U.S. religious institution. (Sources: GU272 Memory Project (American Ancestors); Georgetown; CNN/Boston Globe — labeled.)
- **The records opened:** FamilySearch volunteers indexed the **Freedmen's Bureau records — about 1.8 million names** (project begun 2015; database given to the NMAAHC; searchable at discoverfreedmen.org). For millions of Black Americans the Bureau's marriage registers, labor contracts, and school rolls are the first documents where ancestors appear by name. (Sources: FamilySearch newsroom; Smithsonian.)
- **The *Clotilda* found:** the wreck was located in the Mobile River and **confirmed in 2019**; Africatown's descendant community is the subject of the documentary ***Descendant* (2022)**. (Verified in `research-immigration.md`; Alabama Historical Commission; Netflix/Sundance record.)
- **The Freedmen of the Five Tribes:** a **2017 federal court ruling (*Cherokee Nation v. Nash*)** held the Cherokee Nation bound by its 1866 treaty — descendants of Cherokee Freedmen are entitled to full tribal citizenship; the Cherokee Nation accepted the ruling, and its supreme court struck "by blood" language in 2021. Shared with `native-nations` (they lead on sovereignty and citizenship politics). (Sources: NARF case records; Cherokee Phoenix; CNN.)

- U.S. cotton production: under 9,000 bales/year around 1790 → over 200,000 by 1800 → about 4 million by 1860. (Sources: DPLA primary-source set "Cotton Gin and the Expansion of Slavery"; EH.net "Cotton Gin"; American Yawp ch. 11.)

## Parked from science research (2026-08-08) — the Agassiz daguerreotypes, 1850
Science's era 07 tells this as "science's authority misused"; the people photographed and their descendants' fight are yours to lead. Web-verified 2026-08-08:
- In 1850 Harvard zoologist Louis Agassiz — promoter of polygenism, the doctrine that races were separately created — commissioned photographer Joseph T. Zealy to make daguerreotypes of enslaved people in South Carolina as "evidence" of Black inferiority. Seven people were photographed stripped or partly stripped: Jack, Drana, Delia, Renty, Fassena, Alfred (Foulah), and Jem. Fifteen plates survive — the earliest known photographs of enslaved Americans.
- Shown once (Cambridge Scientific Club, 1850), never published, forgotten; rediscovered in Harvard's Peabody Museum attic in 1976.
- Tamara Lanier, who identifies Renty and Delia as her ancestors, sued Harvard over ownership of the images; the Massachusetts SJC allowed her emotional-distress claims to proceed (2022) and the case settled in the 2020s with the images to be transferred to a South Carolina museum per settlement coverage — confirm final disposition when writing. (Sources: Harvard Magazine "See their faces"; Peabody Museum/Harvard Gazette coverage; Cultural Property News on Lanier v. Harvard — journalism, label.)

## Parked from `landmarks` (2026-08-08) — enslaved builders of the capital, by name
`landmarks` features the buildings and carries these people as builder stories; the hiring-out system, ownership, and emancipation context are yours to lead. All web-verified 2026-08-08, full sourcing in `research/research-landmarks.md`.

### 5. 1750 to 1800
- Enslaved people worked at every stage of the White House (cornerstone Oct 13, 1792) and Capitol (Sept 18, 1793): quarrying Aquia Creek sandstone, timber, bricks, sawing, carpentry; the commissioners hired them from owners who kept the pay. (White House Historical Association, "Did enslaved people build the White House?"; WHHA "Enslaved labor and the construction of the U.S. Capitol.")
- **Named from the record: Tom, Peter, Ben, Harry, and Daniel** — five enslaved carpenters on the May 1795 President's House payroll; four held by architect James Hoban, one by his assistant Pierce Purcell (WHHA pages differ on which man Purcell held). Days worked that month recorded: Peter 28, Ben 28, Daniel 25, Harry 9; Hoban signed for their wages. (WHHA payroll image and "Building the President's House with Enslaved Labor.")

### 6. 1800 to 1850
- **John Hemmings (1776–1833)**, enslaved master joiner at Monticello: fine woodwork of the rebuilt house and Poplar Forest; freed by Jefferson's 1826 will (one of five freed) and given his shop's tools. (Monticello.org, "John Hemmings.") `landmarks` features him as a builder; the Hemings family's larger story is yours.

### 7. 1850 to 1900
- **Philip Reid** (c. 1820–1892), enslaved foundry craftsman at Clark Mills' foundry: solved the separation of the Statue of Freedom's plaster model with a pulley and tackle when the paid foreman held out for more money; paid $1.25/day of which only Sunday was his; freed by the D.C. emancipation act April 16, 1862 — a free man before Freedom was set on the Capitol dome December 2, 1863; later signed his name Reed. (Architect of the Capitol blog; U.S. Senate history office; WHHA "Philip Reed.")
