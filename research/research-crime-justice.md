## Parked from `native-nations` (2026-08-07)
- **The Osage murders / Reign of Terror (c. 1920–1925, Osage County, Oklahoma):** more than **60 murders and suspicious deaths** of Osage headright holders during the oil boom. Mechanism: the Osage kept mineral rights collective (1906 Osage Allotment Act; per-capita "headrights"), and a 1921 guardianship law let local courts appoint white "guardians" over Osage adults' money — guardianships handed to cronies; killings by husbands and guardians to inherit headrights. The Bureau of Investigation's case on the Kyle-family murders (Mollie Burkhart's family; William Hale convicted) became one of the young FBI's first major homicide cases. `native-nations` tells the nation's-wealth angle; this chapter gets the crime and the investigation. Film: *Killers of the Flower Moon* (2023, Scorsese; Lily Gladstone as Mollie Burkhart). (Sources: Osage Nation "Did You Know? Osage Murders"; History.com "How Marriage and Murder Were Used to Steal Osage Oil Riches"; Voices of Oklahoma. Note: Hale conviction detail is standard but verify specifics when this chapter is worked.)
- ***McGirt v. Oklahoma* (July 9, 2020)** — criminal-jurisdiction earthquake: the Muscogee (Creek) reservation (3.25M acres incl. much of Tulsa) was never disestablished, so the state lacks jurisdiction over crimes by Native people there — federal and tribal courts take them; follow-on rulings extended this to the other Five Tribes. Gorsuch's opening line: "On the far end of the Trail of Tears was a promise." (Sources: Supreme Court slip op. 18-9526; Oklahoma Bar Association primer; History.com.)
- **Wounded Knee occupation siege (February 27 – May 8, 1973):** 71 days; U.S. Marshals, FBI, and other agencies cordoned the village; two occupiers died. The Marshals Service history office documents the operation. `native-nations` leads on the event's meaning. (Sources: U.S. Marshals "Incident at Wounded Knee"; SDPB.)
- **The Dakota trials, 1862:** 392 prisoners tried by military commission — some trials under five minutes, no defense counsel, no interpreters; 38 hanged December 26, 1862 (largest mass execution in U.S. history); two hanged in error. A justice-system story as much as a war story. (Sources: Minnesota Historical Society "The Trials & Hanging"; Death Penalty Information Center.)

## Parked from `rights-movements` era 8 (2026-09-09) — federal anti-lynching bills, 1900 to 1922

`rights-movements` carries the campaign; `crime-justice` owns killing outside the law and may want
the legislative record. All fetched from the U.S. House of Representatives, History, Art & Archives.

- **The first federal anti-lynching bill was H.R. 6963, introduced by Representative George Henry
  White of North Carolina on 20 January 1900.** White was the last Black member of Congress of his
  generation.
  (https://history.house.gov/Exhibitions-and-Publications/BAIC/Historical-Essays/Temporary-Farewell/Anti-Lynching-Legislation/)
- **Representative Leonidas Dyer of Missouri introduced his bill on 8 April 1918**, "to protect
  citizens of the United States against lynching in default of protection by the States." It would
  have charged lynch mobs with capital murder, tried the cases in federal court, fined the county
  where a lynching happened, jailed and fined up to **$5,000** any law officer who failed to prevent
  one or surrendered a prisoner to a mob, and barred mob participants from juries.
- **The House passed it on 26 January 1922**, with several hundred Black spectators in the gallery.
  **The Senate never voted.** Southern Democrats obstructed debate and threatened a filibuster;
  Senate Republicans dropped it.
- The NAACP published ***Thirty Years of Lynching in the United States, 1889-1919*** to push
  Congress and sent **James Weldon Johnson** to Washington as its lobbyist. Johnson: "I tramped the
  corridors of the Capitol and the two office buildings so constantly that toward the end, I could,
  I think, have been able to find my way about blindfolded."
  (https://history.house.gov/Exhibitions-and-Publications/BAIC/Historical-Essays/Temporary-Farewell/A-Generation-Lost/)
- **Not used, because not verified:** the House vote count of **230 to 119** (search summary only),
  and the NAACP total of **3,224 people lynched 1889-1918** (loc.gov PDF returns 403).

**The Springfield, Illinois riot of August 1908**, which produced the NAACP, is also `crime-justice`
material: mob violence over nearly three days, two men lynched (**Scott Burton and William
Donegan**), dozens of homes destroyed, nearly 2,000 Black residents driven out for good, state
militia called, **about 150 arrests and few convictions**.
(NPS, https://www.nps.gov/articles/illinois-1908-springfield-race-riot-archaeological-site.htm)

## Era 1: Before 1500 (T-265a, 2026-09-27)

**What can and cannot be known.** No nation north of Mexico kept written law before 1500. Every
account of how the nations handled wrongdoing comes from oral tradition written down later, or from
European and Native observers after about 1600. The book should say so, and should not date any
of these customs to a year before 1500.

### Cherokee clan law (the "law of blood")
- Primary source: "Indian Clans," *Cherokee Phoenix*, vol. 1 no. 49, 18 February 1829, p. 2 col. 5
  (Western Carolina University digital transcription,
  https://www.wcu.edu/library/DigitalCollections/CherokeePhoenix/Vol1/no49/indian-clans-page-2-column-5a.html).
  The paper was the Cherokee Nation's own newspaper. It lists the rules "previous to" the change:
  - "The Cherokee as a nation, had nothing to do with murder."
  - "Murder was punished upon the principle of retaliation."
  - "It belonged to the clan of the murdered to revenge his death."
  - "If the murderer fled, his brother or nearest relative was liable to suffer in his stead."
  - "If a man killed his brother, he was amenable to no law or clan."
  - "To kill, under any circumstance whatever, was considered murder, and punished accordingly."
    (So an accident counted as murder.)
  - The Cherokee had seven clans (Wolf, Deer, Paint and others). A child took the mother's clan.
  - The Cherokee "about twenty years ago in a solemn council, abolished it. From that time, murder
    has been considered a governmental crime." (Twenty years before 1829 = about 1809.)
  - The 1829 editor calls the old rules "savage and barbarous." That is the editor's judgment
    (the book must not adopt it, and may attribute it if used).
- Encyclopedia.com, "Native American Law: Blood Revenge" (American Eras; cites Charles Hudson, John
  Phillip Reid, Daniel K. Richter): the killing was "a private matter between the clans of the
  victim and the assailant"; the killer's relatives had "a sacred obligation to produce a life in
  exchange"; "The lex talionis also did not make a distinction between an accidental death or a
  premeditated homicide"; communities sometimes accepted "compensation payments in wampum, furs, or
  other goods instead of retaliating against the killer."
  (https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/news-wires-white-papers-and-books/native-american-law-blood-revenge)
- Date of the Cherokee abolition: Cherokee Nation's own social-media post says 1810; other summaries
  give a council at Broom's Town on 11 September 1808 and an act of 1810 (unconfirmed: search summary
  only). This is era 6 (T-265b). For era 1 use "about twenty years before 1829" from the *Phoenix*.
- Chota as a Cherokee "town of refuge" where no one could be harmed (unconfirmed: search summary
  only, Wikipedia-derived). Not used.

### Haudenosaunee: condolence and the Great Law
- NYSED, "The Great Law of Peace" teaching unit (New York State Education Department, Indigenous
  Education; https://www.nysed.gov/sites/default/files/programs/indigenous-education/unit-the-great-law-of-peace.pdf):
  the five nations (Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, Seneca) tell that before the Peacemaker they
  were caught in feuds. Hiawatha, grieving his murdered daughters, made strings "forming in his mind
  words of compassion and consolation, rather than vengeance," then shell wampum strings for "the
  words of condolence" that became "central to the Great Law of Peace."
- The Great Law in English: Arthur C. Parker, *The Constitution of the Five Nations*, New York State
  Museum Bulletin 184 (Albany, 1916), from manuscripts collected at Six Nations of the Grand River
  (Ontario) in 1910, one by Seth Newhouse (Mohawk) and one by the Six Nations chiefs (LOC record
  https://www.loc.gov/item/16027201/). Text read at Portland State's transcription,
  https://web.pdx.edu/~caskeym/iroquois_web/html/greatlaw.html:
  - Art. 20: if a Lord (chief) "should commit murder," the other Lords depose him. The War Chief
    says: "I now depose you and expel you and you shall depart at once from the territory of the
    Five Nations Confederacy and nevermore return again." The title passes to a sister family.
  - Art. 74: an adopted outsider who does wrong is first reprimanded by a war chief, and "if a
    similar offense is again committed the offending party or parties shall be expelled from the
    territory of the Five United Nations."
  - Caution for the writer: these are written versions from about 1900 of an older oral law. The
    book should say the law was told aloud for generations and written in English about 1900.
- "Twenty strings of wampum were given for murder as the penalty for homicide" (unconfirmed: search
  summary only). Not used.

### Southeastern nations: forgiveness at the Green Corn
- Eric E. Bowne, "Green Corn Ceremony," *Encyclopedia of Alabama*
  (https://encyclopediaofalabama.org/article/green-corn-ceremony/): the busk (from the Creek word
  for "to fast") was held in midsummer when the early corn ripened. People rekindled the sacred fire
  in the town square, and "the societies at large forgave all crimes except murder." Written
  accounts come from trader James Adair (mid-1700s), naturalist William Bartram and agent Benjamin
  Hawkins. Detailed descriptions date from the late 1800s and early 1900s.

## Era 2: The 1500s (T-265a, 2026-09-27)

**Thin, honestly.** European law reached this land only at a few Spanish outposts: St. Augustine
(1565) and Oñate's New Mexico colony (1598). English Roanoke (1585-1587) left no court record of
its own that this search found. No sheriff, jail or court of the later kind existed yet.

### Who judged, under Spanish law
- Geoffrey Pivateau, "Alcalde," *Handbook of Texas* (TSHA, https://www.tshaonline.org/handbook/entries/alcalde):
  the alcalde's "primary duties was to act as a judge. Though his judicial authority was limited to
  lesser civil and criminal issues, in practice he handled most judicial matters occurring within
  the limits of his jurisdiction." Appeals went to the *justicia mayor* "or to the governor himself."
  So in a Spanish colony the same officials governed, commanded soldiers and judged.
  (Encyclopedia.com and Britannica "alcalde" agree in search summaries.)

### The Acoma trial, February 1599 (justice angle; `native-nations` leads the event)
All from `research/research-native-nations.md`, section "Acoma Pueblo, December 1598 - February
1599" (verified 2026-09-07), which cites: Rebecca Carlson 2008 thesis (citing Simmons, *The Last
Conquistador*, 1991, and the trial record in Hammond and Rey, *Don Juan de Oñate*, 1953); EBSCO
"Battle of Acoma"; NPS "Acoma Pueblo"; NPS "Traditional Groups along El Camino Real"; AP (June 27,
2020); NPR (July 13, 2020); All Pueblo Council of Governors (Oct. 6, 2023); Sherry Robinson (2023)
quoting Kessell.
- December 1598: Acoma people killed Juan de Zaldívar and eleven (or twelve) of his men after the
  soldiers demanded food. The Acoma account says the soldiers also assaulted (raped) an Acoma woman.
- Oñate opened a judicial inquiry on 28 December 1598 and asked the Franciscan friars whether war
  would be just. They said yes. In January 1599 Vicente de Zaldívar led about 70 soldiers, who
  stormed and burned the pueblo. Acoma dead: about 600 (NM State Historian), 800 (All Pueblo
  Council), about 1,500 (NPS). Some 500 captives (EBSCO), 507 tried (NLM).
- The captives were tried before Oñate himself, the man who had ordered the attack. The trial began
  9 February 1599 and lasted three days (Carlson, citing Simmons); the sentence is dated 12 February
  1599 in other sources. Location: Santo Domingo Pueblo (Simmons) or San Juan (Wikipedia): not settled.
- Oñate appointed a defender, who asked for mercy. Captain Alonso Gómez Montesinos is named as the
  defender in some accounts (Wikipedia, uncited: treat as unconfirmed).
- Acoma witnesses said many had been working in their fields during the first fight, and that
  "some wanted to surrender but some refused, and so they fought" (trial record via Carlson).
- Sentence (no death sentences): men over 25, one foot cut off and twenty years of "personal
  servitude"; males 12-25 and females over 12, twenty years of servitude; children under 12 taken
  from their families and given to the friars (NPS: some 70 girls sent to convents in Mexico, "in
  all probability, none ever returned home"; All Pueblo Council: 60 children); two Hopi men had the
  right hand cut off and were sent home to spread word.
- Servitude, in plain words: the prisoners were handed to Spanish colonists to work for them, with
  no pay and no right to leave, for twenty years.
- The number whose feet were cut: 24, reported by AP, NPR and the Acoma oral tradition (NYT 1998).
  Historians Marc Simmons (who later changed his view) and John L. Kessell doubt the cutting was
  carried out. Kessell: "the historical record makes no mention of a one-footed Acoma slave."
  **Settled:** the order, the ages, the twenty-year terms, the taking of the children. **Not
  settled:** how many men were actually cut, and whether it was the whole foot.
- Method, stated honestly: the record gives the order to cut off a foot. It does not describe how.
  (Clinical-word rule: say what the sentence was, and that the record does not say how, or whether,
  it was done to each man.)
- Oñate's own trial (1612-1614) is era 3.

## Era 3: The 1600s (T-265a, 2026-09-27)

### Jamestown under martial law, 1610-1619
- Brent Tarter, "Lawes Divine, Morall and Martiall," *Encyclopedia Virginia*
  (https://encyclopediavirginia.org/entries/lawes-divine-morall-and-martiall/): Sir Thomas Gates
  issued the first orders on 24 May 1610. Thomas West, baron De La Warr, confirmed and added to them
  on 12 June 1610. Sir Thomas Dale issued military rules on 22 June 1611. William Strachey published
  the collection in 1612. Death for "blasphemy, uttering treasonous words or words critical of the
  company, murder, sodomy, robbery," and trading with Indians without permission. Whipping for
  "gambling, failing to attend church, fornication, adultery, unnecessarily killing livestock, or
  stealing agricultural implements or other people's crops." Government under the Lawes ceased when
  Sir George Yeardley arrived, 18 April 1619.
- Tarter, "Sir Thomas Dale (d. 1619)," *Encyclopedia Virginia*: Dale condemned "a man who stole food
  to be tied to a tree and left to starve to death as a warning to others."
- George Percy, "A Trewe Relacyon" (written 1625, printed 1922), transcription in *Colonial
  Williamsburg Journal*, Winter 2007
  (https://research.colonialwilliamsburg.org/Foundation/journal/Winter07/A%20Trewe%20Relation.pdf),
  on the building of the fort at Henricus (1611): men who "did Runne away unto the Indyans," when
  caught, Dale "cawsed to be executed. Some he apointed to be hanged some burned some to be broken
  upon wheles others to be Staked and some to be shott to deathe all theis extreme and crewell
  tortures he used and inflicted upon them To terrefy the reste." Men who robbed the storehouse
  he had "bownd faste unto Trees and so sterved them to deathe." No count and no names.
  Percy wrote in part to defend his own record. Attribute to him. "Staked" is not explained in
  the text: the prose must say the account does not explain it.

### Salem, 1692
- Britannica, "Salem witch trials" (fetched with curl 2026-09-27): trials June 1692 to May 1693.
  "19 people had been hanged and 5 others had died in custody. Additionally, a man was pressed
  beneath heavy stones until he died." First accusers: Betty Parris (9), Abigail Williams (11),
  Ann Putnam Jr. (about 12). First accused: Tituba (enslaved in minister Samuel Parris's household),
  Sarah Good, Sarah Osborn. Magistrates John Hathorne and Jonathan Corwin held the first
  examinations on 1 March 1692. Tituba denied it, then, "after being repeatedly badgered,"
  confessed. Putnam family members and in-laws were accusers "in dozens of cases." After his own
  wife was accused, Governor William Phips set up a new court that would not allow spectral
  evidence. "By May 1693 everyone in custody ... had been pardoned by Phips."
- Jess Blumberg, "A Brief History of the Salem Witch Trials," *Smithsonian* (updated 24 Oct 2022,
  https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/a-brief-history-of-the-salem-witch-trials-175162489/):
  "more than 200 people" accused. "At least five" died in jail. Court of Oyer and Terminer
  established 27 May 1692. Spectral evidence = "testimony about dreams and visions." Increase
  Mather: "It were better that ten suspected witches should escape than one innocent person be
  condemned." Court dissolved 29 October 1692. The Superior Court of Judicature that replaced it
  "condemned just 3 out of 56 defendants." Fast day 14 January 1697. Trials declared unlawful 1702.
  £600 to heirs 1711. Formal apology 1957. Giles Corey was 71 (Smithsonian).
  Correction note: the fetch summary gave "20 were executed by hanging." The count is 19 hanged plus
  Corey pressed (Britannica). Use 19 + 1.
- (unconfirmed: search summary only) William Stoughton, lieutenant governor, presided over seven
  judges. The 1711 act named 22 people.
- Giles Corey: Heather Rockwood, "Giles Corey, pressed to death," Massachusetts Historical Society
  *Beehive* blog, 17 Sept 2021 (https://www.masshist.org/beehiveblog/2021/09/giles-corey-pressed-to-death/):
  pressed 19 September 1692 (Samuel Sewall's diary: "pressed to death for standing mute"). He was
  made to "strip naked and lay down, face up, on the ground. A wooden board was then placed on top
  of him, and on top of the board, one by one, Sheriff George Corwin placed large rocks." It went on
  two days. He died on the third. He had refused to plead guilty or not guilty. The MHS post gives
  the reason as keeping his estate for his children (attribute it). "More weight" as his last words:
  NOT used. The UVA archive index lists his death as 16 September. MHS and Sewall give 19 September.
- Rebecca Nurse, case file, *Salem Witchcraft Papers* No. 94, University of Virginia Salem Witch
  Trials Documentary Archive (https://salem.lib.virginia.edu/n94.html):
  - 23 March 1692: warrant on the complaint of Edward Putnam and Jonathan Putnam, for afflicting Ann
    Putnam Sr., Ann Putnam Jr. and Abigail Williams, signed by magistrates John Hathorne and
    Jonathan Corwin. Marshal George Herrick arrested her 24 March.
  - 24 March examination: "I can say before my Eternal father I am innocent, & God will clear my
    innocency." "I am as clear as the child unborn." When she moved her hands, the accusers were
    recorded as falling into fits. She said she had been sick and not able to leave the house
    "these 8. or 9. dayes."
  - Four indictments: afflicting Ann Putnam Jr., Mary Walcott, Elizabeth Hubbard, Abigail Williams.
  - Petition of 39 neighbors (signers include Israel and Elizabeth Porter and several Putnams): "we
    never had Any: cause or grounds to suspect her of Any such thing as she is nowe Acused of."
  - 28 June 1692, her petition to the court: women had searched her body for a "Supernaturall
    Marke." The most experienced of them saw nothing but what "might Arise from A naturall Cause."
    She asked for other women, two of them midwives, to examine her before her trial. She signed
    with her mark (she could not write her name).
  - Juror Thomas Fisk's declaration, 4 July 1692 (printed by Robert Calef, 1700): the jury first
    found her not guilty. "The honoured Court was pleased to object against it," pointing to her
    words about Goodwife Hobbs and her daughter ("they used to come among us"). The jury went out
    again. In court she "made no reply," and Fisk took that as "a principal Evidence against her."
  - Her appeal: she meant only that they were fellow prisoners. "I being something hard of hearing,
    and full of grief, none informing me how the Court took up my words."
  - UVA summary (https://salem.lib.virginia.edu/minis/nurse1.html): tried June 1692. "Governor Phips
    pardoned her, but was later persuaded to reverse his decision by several men from Salem."

### Massachusetts Bay court records, 1631-1635: the watch, whipping, stocks, ears, branding
Source for every item here: Nathaniel B. Shurtleff, ed., *Records of the Governor and Company of the
Massachusetts Bay in New England*, vol. 1 (Boston, 1853), full text at
https://archive.org/details/recordsofgoverno01mass (read from the djvu text, 2026-09-27). Spelling as printed.
- **The first night watches.** Court of Assistants, Boston, 12 April 1631: "there shalbe a watch of 4
  kept evy night att Dorchester, & another of 4 att Waterton, the watches to begin att sunsett."
  Anyone who fired a gun after the watch was set forfeited 40 shillings or, if unable to pay, "to be
  whipped." Same court: Richard Norman fined for "negligence in watching" and Daniel Abbott fined
  "for refuseing to watch." Court of 5 July 1631: "there shalbe a watch of sixe & an officer kept
  evy night att Boston, 2 whereof are to be of Boston, 2 of Charlton, and 2 of Rocksbury." So the
  watchmen were ordinary settlers taking turns, and a man could be fined for refusing.
- **Philip Ratcliffe (spelled "Phillip Ratliffe" in the index, p. 88).** Court at Boston, 14 June
  1631: "It is ordered, that Phillip Ratliffe shalbe whipped, haue his eares cutt of, fyned 40£, &
  banished out of ye lymitts of this jurisdiccon, for vttering mallitious & scandulous speeches
  against the govmt & the church of Salem, &c., as appeareth by a particul[ar] thereof, proved vpon
  oath." (The printed OCR shows "40*"; Winthrop's journal, per standard secondary accounts, gives
  forty pounds. The record does not say how the ears were cut or by whom.) Same court: Philip
  Swaddon to be whipped "for running away from his maister, Robt Seely, intending to goe to Virginia."
- **Stocks.** Earlier 1630-31 court (same volume, before the March 1631 courts): "John Goulworth
  shalbe whipped, and afterwards sett in the stocks, for fellony committed by him, whereof hee is
  convicted by his owne confession"; Henry Lyn whipped "for the like offence"; John Boggust and John
  Pickryn "to sitt in the stocks 4 howers togeather, att Salem, for being accessary."
  Also: Bartholomew Hill "adiudged to be whipt for stealeing a loafe of breade from John Hoskins";
  John Baker "whipped for shooteing att fowle on the Sabboth day."
- **Bilboes** (iron shackles for the ankles; definition STILL TO SOURCE if used): William James for
  "incontinency" set "in the bilboes at Boston" and "in the stocks at Salem"; Edward Seale, for
  drunkenness, "set in the bilboes till the end of the Court, & then to bee severely whiped" (1630s).
- **Branding.** Court at Boston, 4 September 1632: "Richard Hopkins shalbe severely whipt, &
  branded with a hott iron on one of his cheekes, for selling peeces & powder & shott to the
  Indeans." Court at Newtown, 1 September 1635: Robert Scarlett, "a knowen theife," to be "severely
  whipt & branded in the forehead with a T," then sent back to his master Benjamin Felton, who must
  send him out of the colony. (Branding, in plain words, from the record itself: a hot iron pressed
  into the skin burned a mark or a letter that stayed for life. T stood for thief.)
- Also in this volume (1631, Chickatabut): the Massachusett sachem Chickatabut was fined a beaver
  skin for shooting a swine of Sir Richard Saltonstall. Native people were brought under the
  colony's court from its first year.

### A Native murder trial with a mixed jury: Plymouth, June 1675
Source: Nathaniel B. Shurtleff, ed., *Records of the Colony of New Plymouth*, vol. 5, Court Orders
1668-1678 (Boston, 1856), pp. 159, 167-168, full text https://archive.org/details/recordsofcolonyo05newp.
- 1 March 1674/5: the court required "an Indian called Tobias" to answer questions about "the
  suddaine and violent death of an Indian called John Sassamon." Tobias, Tuspaquin "the black
  sachem" of Namassakett, and Tuspaquin's son William pledged their lands, worth £100, that Tobias
  would appear at the June court.
- June 1675: "three natiues were araigned, vizt, Tobias, & Wampapaquan, and Mattashunannamo," accused
  of having, on 29 January 1674/5 (1675 in today's calendar), at Assowamsett Pond, murdered John
  Sassamon, "an other Indian, by laying violent hands on him and striking him, or twisting his necke,
  vntill hee was dead," and of casting "his dead body through a hole of the ice into the said pond."
  They denied it.
- The jury: twelve English colonists (William Sabine, William Crocker, Edward Sturgis, William
  Brookes, Nathaniel Winslow, John Wadsworth, Andrew Ringe, Robert Vixon, John Done, Jonathan Banges,
  Jonathan Shaw, Benjamin Higgins). The court added "some of the most indifferentest, gravest, and
  sage Indians" to "consult and advice" with the jury: Hope, Maskippague, Wannoo, George, Wampye and
  Acanootus, who "fully concurred." The six Native men advised. They were not sworn jurors.
- Verdict: "Wee, of the jury, one and all, both English and Indians, doe joyntly and with one
  consent agree" that Tobias, "his son Wampapaquan," and Mattashunannamo were guilty.
- Sentence: to be "hanged by the head vntill theire bodies are dead." Tobias and Mattashunannamo
  were hanged 8 June 1675. Wampapaquan was reprieved for a month, "But afterwards shott to death
  within the said month."
- What followed: King Philip's War began in June 1675 at Swansea, after Plymouth executed the three
  men (`research/research-war.md` §3.4, citing NPS, "The Unexpected Start to Metacom's War").
  `war` and `native-nations` lead the war. DEFECT NOTED (not ours to fix): `outlines/war.md` says
  Plymouth "hanged three Wampanoag men." The record says two were hanged and the third was shot a
  month later. Listed in the checkpoint TO PARK.
- Not in this record, and not used: what Sassamon had told Plymouth's governor before his death, and
  which nation each of the three men belonged to (later histories call them Wampanoag).

### Killing an enslaved person during "correction" made no crime: Virginia, October 1669
- William Waller Hening, ed., *The Statutes at Large ... of Virginia*, vol. 2 (1823), p. 270, full
  text https://archive.org/details/statutesatlargeb02virg: "An act about the casuall killing of
  slaves," assembly at James City, session prorogued to 20 October 1669, Sir William Berkeley
  governor. Text: "if any slave resist his master (or other by his masters order correcting him) and
  by the extremity of the correction should chance to die, that his death shall not be accompted
  felony, but the master (or that other person appointed by the master to punish him) be acquit from
  molestation, since it cannot be presumed that prepensed malice (which alone makes murther felony)
  should induce any man to destroy his owne estate."
  Plain words: "correction" meant a beating. If a master, or someone he ordered, beat an enslaved
  person to death, it was not treated as murder, because the lawmakers said no one would mean to
  destroy his own property. `slavery-freedom` leads slave law and tells the 1705 code. This chapter
  uses the act for who could kill without being punished.

### PATCH 2026-09-27 (T-265r): what each punishment was (clinical-word rule, policy 3b)
- **Pillory.** *Britannica Kids / Britannica Student*, "pillory" (https://kids.britannica.com/students/article/pillory/333196,
  read with curl 2026-09-27): "A wooden frame known as the pillory was used to confine criminals during the
  American colonial period. It was a T-shaped wooden stock with holes in the top bar." Offenders "were
  forced to stand with their heads and hands stuck through the holes; the frames clamped around the
  culprit's neck and wrists." The pillories stood on platforms in public places "to expose the criminals
  to public scorn and ridicule as well as to cause bodily discomfort." US federal law provided for the
  pillory until 1839. Delaware, the last state, did not abolish it until 1905. (Delaware 1905 is era 8.)
  Britannica main site (search summary, same wording): the pillory's public use "enabled both verbal and
  physical abuse by other citizens."
- **Stocks.** Britannica Dictionary (search summary): "a wooden frame with holes in it for a person's
  feet, hands, or head that was used in the past as a form of punishment." Wikipedia "Stocks" (API
  extract): "a feet restraining device." Plain words: the person sat on the ground or a bench with the
  ankles locked in holes in a wooden board, in a public place, for the hours the court set (Salem 1631:
  "4 howers").
- **Whipping.** Wikipedia "Flagellation" (API extract): beating the body with a whip, rod or switch, "The
  strokes are typically aimed at the unclothed back of a person." The Massachusetts records above give
  "whipped" and "severely whipt" and do not state the number of strokes or the tool in these cases.
  The prose: a whipping meant being struck on the bare back with a whip, often at a post in a public
  place. The records do not say how many strokes each person here received.
- **Bilboes.** Wikipedia "Bilboes" (API extract): "iron restraints normally placed on a person's ankles,"
  leg shackles. Plain words: iron rings locked around the ankles and joined to a bar, so the person could
  not walk away.
- **Breaking on the wheel** (Percy's "broken upon wheles," Jamestown 1611). Wikipedia "Breaking wheel" (API
  extract): a method of public execution in Europe "by breaking the bones of a criminal or bludgeoning them
  to death." Plain words: the executioner broke the person's arms and legs with a heavy bar or club, and
  the person was tied to a large wooden wheel. Percy's account does not describe how it was done at
  Jamestown. The prose should give the general method from the definition and say that Percy gives no detail.
- **Pressing** (Giles Corey). Wikipedia "Peine forte et dure" (API extract): English common law let a court
  put "heavier and heavier stones" on the chest of a person who refused to plead guilty or not guilty,
  "until a plea was entered, or death resulted." A person who refused to plead and died this way was never
  convicted, so the heirs kept the property. This matches the MHS account of Corey above.
- **Ear-cropping.** In the cases above the Massachusetts records give the order ("haue his eares cutt of")
  and not the method. The religion bank (T-260 PATCH, era 3, from George Bishop, *New England Judged*, 1661) records that in September 1658 the hangman's deputy cut off the right
  ear of each of three Quakers, Christopher Holder, John Copeland and John Rous, in the Boston prison
  with the door shut. Plain words: a court officer cut the outer ear off with a knife. The Holder text in
  `research/research-religion.md` says "cut off by the Hangman." It does not name the tool. The prose may say
  "cut off" and must not name a knife as fact. (Correction to the religion bank's "with a knife": that
  word is its agent's paraphrase, not Bishop's.)
  Shared with: `religion` (lead on the Quakers, who does not tell the ears in its outline).

### The county court and the sheriff
- Emily Salmon/Encyclopedia Virginia, "County Formation during the Colonial Period"
  (https://encyclopediavirginia.org/entries/county-formation-during-the-colonial-period/, curl
  2026-09-27): by 1622 Virginia settlements had local courts of "commissioners of the peace (later known
  as justices of the peace)." They "met, usually monthly." In 1642 the assembly named them "countie
  courts." Serving on them was "the logical stepping stone" to the House of Burgesses. At the close of
  the colonial period Virginia had 61 counties.
- Search summary (Colonial Williamsburg research report on the courthouse, page blocked by Cloudflare):
  the governor appointed the justices, usually eight to ten per county, and the sheriff "had the unpleasant
  duty of seizing a debtor's property in execution of a judgment" (unconfirmed: search summary only).
  The outline says only that the sheriff carried out the court's orders, which the 1741 New York journal
  below shows directly (the sheriff took Quack and Cuffee to the stake).

### PATCH 2026-09-30 (T-319): what the Virginia county courts did

### Imprisonment for debt
- Robert C. Voigt, "Imprisonment for Debt," *Encyclopedia of North Carolina* (2006), NCpedia
  (https://www.ncpedia.org/debt-imprisonment, curl 2026-09-27): "From the time of the earliest colonial
  settlements until the mid-nineteenth century, imprisonment for debt was common in North Carolina and
  throughout British North America." English law, brought to the colonies, "allowed individuals to be
  jailed at their own expense in the event of default." Its purpose: "to provide creditors a forceful
  means of collecting payment on a debt." North Carolina abolished it (except for fraud) in 1867.
- Search summaries only: "The earliest American debtors' prison was located in Salem, Massachusetts in
  1678"; "Some debtors were locked up for as many as 30 years" (unconfirmed: search summary only; not used).

### Convicts shipped from Britain
- Emily Jones Salmon, "Convict Labor during the Colonial Period," *Encyclopedia Virginia* (7 Dec 2020,
  https://encyclopediavirginia.org/entries/convict-labor-during-the-colonial-period/, curl 2026-09-27):
  English courts sent about 2,300 convicts to the colonies 1615-1699. "Between 1700 and 1775,
  approximately 52,200 convicts sailed for the colonies, more than 20,000 of them to Virginia." 80
  percent went to Maryland and Virginia. Parliament's Transportation Act of 1718 set up a paid contract
  system. Jonathan Forward was the first merchant with a Treasury contract. Convicts were sold on arrival
  and served "seven- to fourteen-year terms," mostly in tobacco fields, some to tradesmen, shipbuilders
  and ironworks. Virginia passed a law in 1722 to make shipping convicts too costly. Forward complained
  and the Board of Trade overturned it. Benjamin Franklin objected in 1751. The last convict ship reached
  the James River in 1776. (Historian Lyda C. Bitto: at least 20,000 felons to Virginia 1718-1775.)
  Shared with: `immigration` / `work-workers` (check before prose; not checked here).

### Slave patrols (who enforced the slave codes)
- Encyclopedia of South Carolina, "Slave Patrols" (https://www.scencyclopedia.org/sce/entries/slave-patrols/,
  curl 2026-09-27; bibliography Sally E. Hadden, *Slave Patrols*, Harvard 2001): the earliest patrol
  systems were "based on Barbadian models." White South Carolinians "provided for official slave patrols
  beginning in 1704." Patrollers came from the same men as the militia, and "in times of invasion
  patrollers were to stay away from combat and police the slave population." Early on "gentlemen served
  alongside poorer white farmers." 1734-1737: patrollers were paid. After Stono, the Negro Act of 1740
  "provided for constant, regular patrols," and "the Charleston night watch patrolled the streets." That
  system lasted "until the end of the Civil War." All plantation owners were called to serve. Patrol beats
  ran 10 to 15 miles. The typical patrol (antebellum description): a handful of men on horseback who
  searched slave quarters, broke up gatherings of enslaved people, and guarded roads.
- Search summary (SCE and others): South Carolina was the first colony with formal patrols. Virginia and
  North Carolina followed in the next decades (unconfirmed: search summary only for the dates of the other two).

### Trials without a jury for enslaved people: Virginia 1692 and 1723
- Search summary (several sources incl. Commonwealth Institute, Library of Virginia): Virginia's
  assembly in 1692 set up special "courts of oyer and terminer" to try enslaved people for capital crimes
  without a jury, and Virginia kept that system until 1865 (unconfirmed: search summary only; the UNB law
  page returned nothing to curl).
- Primary, fetched: "An Act directing the trial of Slaves, committing capital crimes ..." (May 1723),
  Hening, *Statutes at Large* 4:126-134, transcribed by Encyclopedia Virginia
  (https://encyclopediavirginia.org/primary-documents/an-act-directing-the-trial-of-slaves-committing-capital-crimes-and-for-the-more-effectual-punishing-conspiracies-and-insurrections-of-them-and-for-the-better-government-of-negros-mulattos-and-in/):
  justices named in a commission try the accused at the county courthouse, taking as evidence "the
  confession of the offender, the oath of one or more credible witnesses, or such testimony of Negroes,
  Mulattos, or Indians ... as to them shall seem convincing, without the solemnity of a jury," and on a
  guilty finding "award execution." A Black or Native witness found to have lied was to "have one ear
  nailed to the pillory, and there to stand for the space of one hour, and then the said ear to be cut
  off," then the other ear the same way an hour later, and "thirty-nine lashes, well laid on, on his or
  her bare back, at the common whipping-post." The court read that warning aloud to each such witness.
  The master "may appear at the trial, and make what just defence he can for such slave."
  Plain words: a person nailed through the ear to the wooden frame, left standing an hour, then the ear
  cut off, twice, then 39 strokes of the whip on the bare back. The act names the punishment and not who
  carried it out, or how often it was done.

### The New York trials of 1741 from the court's side: Quack and Cuffee
`slavery-freedom` gives the totals (13 burned, 17 hanged, 4 white people hanged, 70 to 84 shipped away).
- Douglas O. Linder, "The New York Conspiracy ('Negro Plot') Trials: An Account," Famous Trials, UMKC
  (https://famous-trials.com/newyorkplot/358-home, curl 2026-09-27): fires began 18 March 1741 at
  Lieutenant Governor George Clarke's house in Fort George. The first grand jury witness was Mary Burton,
  "the sixteen-year-old servant of tavern owner John Hughson," who at first refused and began talking as
  she was being taken to the City Hall dungeon. Linder's totals: 34 executed, 13 Black men burned, 17
  hanged, and four white people (two men, two women) hanged. John and Sarah Hughson and Peggy Kerry were
  hanged 12 June. Attorney General Richard Bradley prosecuted Quack and Cuffee.
- Primary: Justice Daniel Horsmanden, *A Journal of the Proceedings in the Detection of the Conspiracy*
  (1744), trial of Quack and Cuffee, transcribed at Famous Trials
  (https://famous-trials.com/newyorkplot/354-journalcuffee, curl 2026-09-27):
  - Quack was enslaved by a painter named Roosevelt. Cuffee was enslaved by Frederick Philipse.
  - 12 May: the undersheriff put Cuffee in a cell with Arthur Price, a white prisoner, and gave them
    "a tankard of punch now and then" so Price could draw talk out of him. Price then swore Cuffee had spoken
    of the fires. (Linder quotes Jill Lepore: "from the prosecutor's point of view, Price was priceless.")
  - Other enslaved witnesses (Sandy, Fortune) testified after being promised their lives. The grand jury told
    Sandy "the negroes which confessed the truth and made a discovery, were certainly pardoned, and shipped off."
  - At trial, "the Prisoners being asked what they had to offer in their defense, they offered nothing but
    peremptory denials ... and protestations of their innocency." William Smith Sr. spoke for the
    prosecution. Linder: the prosecution called eleven witnesses. "Quack and Cuffee, forced to conduct
    their own defense, called ten witnesses, who mostly presented alibi evidence." "The defendants had no
    lawyers." Horsmanden questioned suspects himself before sending them to the grand jury, which Linder
    notes would violate due process today. White defendants (the Hughsons) had more protections under
    New York law, such as twenty peremptory challenges of jurors.
  - Sentence, by Horsmanden: "chained to a stake, and burnt to death," set for Saturday 30 May 1741,
    between one and seven in the afternoon.
  - 30 May: about three o'clock "the criminals were brought to the stake, surrounded with piles of wood."
    Horsmanden: the crowd "were very impatient" for the fire to be lit. The deputy secretary, Mr. Moore,
    and Quack's owner, Mr. Roosevelt, took confessions at the stake by telling each man the other had
    already confessed. The confessions were taken "in the midst of great noise and confusion." Moore asked
    the sheriff to delay while the governor was asked about a reprieve. The sheriff said taking the men back
    through the crowd could not be done without a strong guard. The governor's reprieve was "conditional and
    discretionary," and "the execution proceeded."
  - Plain words for burning at the stake: the person was chained to an upright post with wood piled around
    it, and the wood was set on fire, so the person burned to death.
  - The confessions at the stake named others and fed the next trials. Whether any plot existed is disputed
    (Linder: "grave questions about the contours of the suspected conspiracy").
- Quack's and Cuffee's own nations or birthplaces: not in these sources.
- Cross-bank notes (from `research/research-slavery-freedom.md` era 4, NY Courts Historical Society):
  about 200 tried, judges James De Lancey, Daniel Horsmanden and Frederick Philipse. Two disagreements to
  carry: (1) the first fire: slavery-freedom's bank says 8 March 1741, Linder says 18 March 1741. The
  outline gives no day. (2) Sarah Hughson the daughter: slavery-freedom's bank (NY Courts) lists her among
  those put to death. Linder says her execution "was postponed" on 12 June. Not resolved here, and not in
  the outline. The outline's "historians still disagree whether any plot existed" rests on Linder ("grave
  questions about the contours of the suspected conspiracy") and on the Salem comparison in the

### Two kinds of prison: Auburn and Pennsylvania

- **Auburn, New York.** The Auburn system "evolved during the 1820s at Auburn Prison." Prisoners "worked during the day in groups and were kept in solitary confinement at night, with enforced silence at all times." "In 1821 a new principal keeper, Elam Lynds, was appointed to run the prison. He believed in the disciplinary power of the lash, and used flogging to punish even minor infractions." Lynds and John D. Cray made prisoners march "in unison" with locked arms (the lockstep). Lynds brought in the striped uniform. New York used the plan for Sing Sing, its third state prison. "By 1829, Connecticut, Massachusetts, Maryland, and Washington, D.C. had adopted the Auburn system," and it "quickly replaced" the Pennsylvania plan in most of the US (Wikipedia, "Auburn system").
- **Flogging (policy 3b):** the lash was a whip. A keeper struck the prisoner's bare back with it, which cut the skin and left welts and scars (see era 3 PATCH on whipping for the plain definition). The Auburn sources above do not give the number of blows or name who swung the whip.
- **Pennsylvania plan, Eastern State Penitentiary, Philadelphia.** Designed by the British architect John Haviland, first prisoner admitted 1829. "A strict system of solitary confinement with labor." Haviland said the design was for "watching, convenience, economy and ventilation." Built for 250 prisoners, it held about 1,700 by the mid-1920s. Officials gave up the Pennsylvania system in 1913 (Eastern State Penitentiary Historic Site, https://www.easternstate.org/research/history-eastern-state).
- Wikipedia ("Eastern State Penitentiary"): opened 25 October 1829. Seven cell blocks off a central hub. Cells 8 by 12 feet, 10 feet high, each with a water tap, a toilet and its own small exercise yard. "When a prisoner left his cell, an accompanying guard would wrap a hood over his head to prevent him from being recognized by other prisoners." Guards punished prisoners with cold showers, once apparently outdoors in winter, and with the iron gag: an iron piece over the tongue, chained to the prisoner's hands held behind his back. Closed 1971.
- Smithsonian (Abigail Tucker, "Eastern State Penitentiary: A Prison With a Past", https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/eastern-state-penitentiary-a-prison-with-a-past-14274660/): prisoners lived in "complete isolation, with a Bible their only possession," did shoemaking and weaving, and spoke to guards through "a small feeding hole."
- **Charles Dickens, 1842:** "I believe it, in its effects, to be cruel and wrong" (easternstate.org). Also: "I hold the slow and daily tampering with the mysteries of the brain to be immeasurably worse than any torture of the body" (quoted by Smithsonian). Source: Dickens, *American Notes*, 1842.

### Allan Pinkerton and his detective agency (story re-sourced)

- Wikipedia, "Allan Pinkerton" (tertiary): born in the Gorbals, Glasgow, Scotland, 21 August 1819. A barrel maker (cooper). Settled in Dundee Township, Illinois, in 1843. ### Convict leasing (`slavery-freedom` leads; our slice is the punishment)

- Mary Ellen Curtin, "Convict-Lease System," *Encyclopedia of Alabama* (https://encyclopediaofalabama.org/article/convict-lease-system/): Alabama leased prisoners from 1875 to 1928 and was "the last state in the nation to abolish convict leasing." ### PATCH 2026-09-27 (T-265b, bank check): the first electric chair, William Kemmler, 1890 (clinical-word rule)
- HISTORY.com, "First execution by electric chair" (This Day in History, 6 August 1890; https://www.history.com/this-day-in-history/august-6/first-execution-by-electric-chair): at Auburn Prison, New York, William Kemmler, "convicted of murdering his lover, Matilda Ziegler, with an axe," was the first person executed by electricity. The Buffalo dentist Dr. Albert Southwick first suggested electrocution in 1881 as more humane than hanging, in which the condemned could hang "for up to 30 minutes" before dying. The chair had two electrodes (metal disks covered with a damp sponge) placed on the head and the back. "A charge of approximately 700 volts was delivered for only 17 seconds before the current failed. Although witnesses reported smelling burnt clothing and charred flesh, Kemmler was far from dead, and a second shock was prepared. The second charge was 1,030 volts and applied for about two minutes, whereupon smoke was observed coming from the head of Kemmler." "An autopsy showed that the electrode attached to his back had burned through to the spine." Southwick: "We live in a higher civilization from this day on." George Westinghouse: "They would have done better with an axe."
- Wikipedia, "William Kemmler": he killed Tillie Ziegler with a hatchet on 29 March 1889 in Buffalo. His lawyers argued the electric chair was a "cruel and unusual punishment" under the Eighth Amendment. The US Supreme Court turned the appeal down (*In re Kemmler*, 1890). - **Electrocution (policy 3b):** killing a person by sending a strong electric current through the body. The current stops the heart and burns the skin where the metal touches it. Kemmler's first shock did not kill him, and a second one of about two minutes did.

### Ed Johnson, lynched in Chattanooga after the Supreme Court stayed his execution, 1906 (story; wrongful conviction)

- **Primary: *United States v. Shipp*, 214 U.S. 386 (1909)** (Cornell LII, https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/214/386). The charge as the Court states it: on 11 February 1906 "Ed Johnson, a negro, was convicted of rape by the criminal court of Hamilton county, Tennessee, held in Chattanooga, and was sentenced to death." He petitioned the US circuit court for habeas corpus (a court order to review whether a prisoner is held lawfully). It was denied on 10 March. "On March 19 an order was made by the Supreme Court allowing said appeal," which "stayed all proceedings against Johnson" and required Sheriff Joseph F. Shipp to keep him safe. The order was in Chattanooga's evening papers before 6 p.m. that day. The sheriff "withdrew from the jail early in the evening of the 19th the usual and customary guard, and left in charge thereof only the night jailer," Jeremiah Gibson. Between 9 and 12 that night the mob "broke into the jail, took Johnson out by force, and lynched him." Shipp arrived "but made no effort to prevent the mob." Findings in the opinion: the mob took Johnson "to the county bridge over the Tennessee river," "put a rope around his neck, threw it over a beam, and swung him up. ... The first time Johnson was swung up, the rope broke or slipped and he fell. He was swung up a second time and shot." Shipp "made no effort to send anyone for help." Nevada Taylor is called "a white schoolgirl" in the 1909 opinions; she was attacked 23 January 1906. Johnson was arrested 25 January (opinion) or 24 January (Wikipedia).
- Wikipedia, "United States v. Shipp" and "Lynching of Ed Johnson" (tertiary): Nevada Taylor was attacked from behind and choked with a leather strap while walking home from a streetcar stop. She said she recognized Johnson by his voice, face and size. Thirteen defense witnesses said Johnson was at the Last Chance Saloon, where he worked as a porter, at that time. A witness, Will Hixson, may have given police Johnson's name to collect a $375 reward. His court-appointed lawyers dropped the appeal. Two Black lawyers, Noah Parden and Styles Hutchins, took it up. Parden met Justice John Marshall Harlan in Washington on 17 March. On the bridge, a deputy sheriff fired five more shots into Johnson's head after he fell (Wikipedia). His tombstone reads "God Bless you all. I AM A Innocent Man." The case "remains the only criminal trial in the history of the Supreme Court." In May 1909 Shipp, Luther Williams, Nick Nolan, Jeremiah Gibson, Henry Padgett and William Mayes were found guilty of contempt. On 15 November 1909 Shipp, Williams and Nolan got 90 days, and Gibson, Padgett and Mayes 60 days. Shipp was welcomed home as a hero.
- Death Penalty Information Center, "Chattanooga Dedicates Memorial to Ed Johnson..." (https://deathpenaltyinfo.org/chattanooga-dedicates-memorial-to-ed-johnson-an-innocent-man-sentenced-to-death-on-false-rape-charges-and-lynched-after-u-s-supreme-court-stayed-his-execution): convicted by an "all-white, all-male jury" despite a dozen alibi witnesses. The mob took him to the Walnut Street Bridge, tried to hang him, then shot him to death. Justice Harlan issued the stay on 18 March 1906 (DPIC) (the Supreme Court's order is dated 19 March in the 1909 opinion: record both). In the contempt case "twenty-five men were tried"; six convicted. Memorial dedicated 19 September 2021. In 2000 Pastor Paul McDaniels petitioned to overturn the conviction.
- **February 2000:** Hamilton County Criminal Court Judge Doug Meyer set aside Johnson's conviction because of the all-white jury and the trial judge's refusal to move the trial out of Chattanooga (Wikipedia; search summary of several sources). Book: Mark Curriden and Leroy Phillips Jr., *Contempt of Court* (1999).
- **Word "stay":** a court order that stops something, here the hanging, until the court decides.
- **Contempt of court:** the crime of disobeying or defying a court's order.

### Tulsa, 31 May to 1 June 1921: the justice angle (DECISIONS #23; `rights-movements` leads the event)

- **US Department of Justice, Civil Rights Division, Cold Case Unit, *Review and Evaluation: Tulsa Race Massacre*** (Emmett Till Unsolved Civil Rights Crime Act review), released January 2025 (the DOJ resource page gives Friday 17 January 2025; news reports give 10 and 13 January 2025: record the month), 123-126 pages, PDF https://www.justice.gov/crt/media/1383756/dl (read in full text 2026-09-27). Facts:
  - "On the night of May 31, 1921, a violent attack by as many as 10,000 white Tulsans destroyed the thriving Black community of Greenwood." The attack "lasted into the afternoon of June 1" and "was so systematic and coordinated that it transcended mere mob violence. White men murdered hundreds of Black residents, burned businesses and homes to the ground."
  - The trigger: "The allegations of a white man led police to arrest 19-year-old Dick Rowland for allegedly assaulting a white woman who operated an elevator he used," Sarah Page, in the Drexel building. Police Chief John Gustafson told a federal agent that Page "bore no scratches, bruises, or disarranged dress and that she stated the boy made no bad remark of any kind," and she declined to press charges.
  - "Local police deputized hundreds of white residents, many of whom had been advocating for a lynching and had been drinking. Law enforcement officers helped organize these special deputies ... into the martial forces that ravaged Greenwood." They "looted, burned, and destroyed 35 city blocks." Special deputies were told to "get a [n-word]." (Ellsworth, OHS: police told them to "get a gun and get a nigger." Quote only in paraphrase.)
  - Guard: Governor James Robertson declared martial law at 11:30 a.m. on 1 June. National Guardsmen took Black men into "protective custody" and held them in internment camps. "The Guard arrested some white residents for looting but did not confine any white resident to the camps." No white Tulsans were held in camps.
  - Deaths: "The massacre killed as many as 300 Tulsans, perhaps even more." Bodies were dumped in the Arkansas River, carried off on flatbed rail cars, and buried in unmarked mass graves.
  - Dr. A. C. Jackson, a Black surgeon, walked out of his home with his hands up, surrendering to armed white men, and a young white man shot him. He died of his wounds. At Gustafson's trial the witness John A. Oliphant named a police officer, Brown, and a civilian, "Cowboy Long," as chief arsonists, and said police "were the chief fellows setting fires."
  - Airplanes: white newspapers reported planes supporting the invasion, likely Curtiss JN-4 "Jenny" biplanes. The report examined claims that men in planes dropped fire bombs and fired on Greenwood (it treats these as allegations with some support).
  - **The grand jury.** "The state quickly summoned a grand jury that subpoenaed almost 200 witnesses. The grand jury returned indictments against about 70 men, including many Black leaders of Greenwood, whom the grand jury accused of inciting the 'riot.' Most cases were eventually dismissed, including the assault case against Dick Rowland ... Some white men were accused of looting, but ultimately none were ever sent to prison." On 26 June 1921 the grand jury's report ran in the *Tulsa Daily World* under the headline "Grand Jury Blames Negroes for Inciting Race Rioting: Whites Clearly Exonerated." It named the armed Black men at the courthouse as a "direct cause," and as "indirect causes" "agitation among the [N]egroes of social equality" and "laxity of law enforcement." "The grand jury charged Police Chief Gustafson with dereliction of duty and with other corruption alleged to have occurred before the massacre. Gustafson was subsequently convicted and removed from his position." A *Tulsa Tribune* headline of 17 June 1921: "Roundup of 64 Indicted Blacks Is On: No Warrants Issued for Whites."
  - Less than a year before the massacre, a mob took a white prisoner, Roy Belton, from the same courthouse jail and lynched him. "It is undisputed that Police Chief John Gustafson ordered police not to intervene."
  - **Why no one can be tried now:** "no avenue of prosecution now exists for these crimes, the youngest potential defendants would today be more than 115 years old, and the relevant statutes of limitations have long since expired." "Were present-day civil rights laws on the books at the time, offenders could have been prosecuted and convicted" (DOJ resource page). The DOJ calls it "the federal government's first thorough reckoning" with the massacre.
  - In 1921 a Bureau of Investigation agent, T. F. Weiss, wrote a report in less than a week that called the lynching attempt "half-hearted," said the "riot" was not the result of "racial feeling," and concluded no federal law was broken.
- **Oklahoma Historical Society, Scott Ellsworth, "Tulsa Race Massacre,"** *Encyclopedia of Oklahoma History and Culture* (https://www.okhistory.org/publications/enc/entry?entry=TU013): "credible estimates of deaths range from fifty to three hundred." "An all-white grand jury blamed Black Tulsans for the lawlessness." "Despite overwhelming evidence, no whites were ever sent to prison for the murders and arson that occurred." "Dick Rowland was exonerated." "A state commission was formed in 1997 to investigate. The report recommended that reparations be paid to the remaining Black survivors." Scientists and historians found evidence of unmarked graves.
- **The 2001 report:** *Tulsa Race Riot: A Report by the Oklahoma Commission to Study the Tulsa Race Riot of 1921*, February 2001 (cited by DOJ). It estimates about 10,000 white Tulsans took part (DOJ).
- Grand jury count disagreement: "about 70 men" (DOJ), "more than 85 people, with the majority being Black" (search summary, unconfirmed), and 64 warrants all against Black men (*Tulsa Tribune*, 17 June 1921, quoted by DOJ). Record all.

### The Bureau of Investigation becomes the FBI, 1908-1935

### The "third degree" and *Brown v. Mississippi*, 1931-1936

- Office of Justice Programs abstract, Wickersham Commission Report No. 11, *Report on Lawlessness in Law Enforcement* (1931), by M. M. Lemann, Zechariah Chafee, Walter H. Pollak and Carl S. Stern (https://ojp.gov/ncjrs/virtual-library/abstracts/us-national-commission-law-observance-and-enforcement-report-no-11): the third degree is "the unlawful use of cruel treatment by police to extort confessions of guilt from prisoners." "The use of physical brutality, protracted questioning, threats, prolonged illegal detention" was "widespread in 1931." The report studied 15 cities. President Herbert Hoover set up the commission in May 1929 (FBI history page).
- ***Brown v. Mississippi*, 297 U.S. 278 (1936)** (Cornell LII, https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/297/278), quoting the dissent in the Mississippi Supreme Court: "One Raymond Stewart" died on 30 March 1934 (the opinion does not describe him further). That night Deputy Sheriff Dial and other white men seized Arthur Ellington, "and with the participation of the deputy they hanged him by a rope to the limb of a tree, and, having let him down, they hung him again, and when he was let down the second time, and he still protested his innocence, he was tied to a tree and whipped." Days later the deputy arrested him again and "severely whipped" him until he agreed to confess. On 1 April 1934 the same deputy, other white men and the jailer made Ed Brown and Henry Shields "strip and they were laid over chairs and their backs were cut to pieces with a leather strap with buckles on it," until they confessed in the words demanded. They were indicted 4 April, tried the next day and sentenced to death. The deputy admitted the whippings in court and said of Ellington's whipping: "Not too much for a negro; not as much as I would have done if it were left to me." Chief Justice Charles Evans Hughes for a unanimous Court: "The rack and torture chamber may not be substituted for the witness stand." The Court threw out the convictions because they "rest solely upon confessions shown to have been extorted by officers of the state by brutality and violence."

### The Scottsboro cases, 1931-1937

- Daren Salter, "Scottsboro Trials," *Encyclopedia of Alabama* (https://encyclopediaofalabama.org/article/scottsboro-trials/): on 25 March 1931 nine Black youths aged 13 to 20 were taken off a freight train in Alabama and accused of raping two white women, Victoria Price and Ruby Bates. They were Olen Montgomery, Clarence Norris, Haywood Patterson, Ozie Powell, Willie Roberson, Charlie Weems, Eugene Williams, and the brothers Andy and Roy Wright. Eight were sentenced to death. The jury in 13-year-old Roy Wright's trial split over death or life, and the judge declared a mistrial. In *Powell v. Alabama* (1932) the US Supreme Court ordered new trials because the youths had not had real lawyers. Ruby Bates later said Price "concocted the story to avoid charges for vagrancy" and testified for the defense. Judge James Horton set aside a guilty verdict against Patterson. In *Norris v. Alabama* (1935) the Supreme Court overturned the convictions again because Black people had been kept off Jackson County's jury lists. Seven years of trials followed. The men were released between 1943 and 1976. In 2013 Governor Robert Bentley signed a law that led to pardons for all nine.

### The rights of the accused: *Mapp* (1961), *Gideon* (1963), *Miranda* (1966), *Gault* (1967), *Terry* (1968)

- **Mapp v. Ohio, 367 U.S. 643** (WP "Mapp v. Ohio"): on **23 May 1957** Cleveland police forced
  the door of **Dollree Mapp**'s home, waved a paper they called a warrant (it was never produced in
  court), searched without one and found books they called obscene. She was sentenced to one to
  seven years. On **19 June 1961** the Court ruled **6 to 3** that evidence police get by an illegal
  search cannot be used in a state trial (the "exclusionary rule").
- **Clarence Earl Gideon** (WP "Clarence Earl Gideon", "Gideon v. Wainwright"): born 30 August 1910,
  Hannibal, Missouri. On **3 June 1961** someone broke into the Bay Harbor Pool Room in Panama City,
  Florida, and took coins from the jukebox and cigarette machine, beer, wine and soda. A witness,
  **Henry Cook**, 22, said he saw Gideon leave with his pockets full of coins. Gideon could not pay a
  lawyer. Judge **Robert McCrary Jr.** refused to appoint one, because Florida gave free lawyers
  only in death-penalty cases. Gideon defended himself, was convicted **4 August 1961**, and was
  sentenced to **five years**. From Florida State Prison at Raiford he wrote a petition to the US
  Supreme Court **in pencil**. The Court assigned **Abe Fortas**. On **18 March 1963** the Court
  ruled **9 to 0** (opinion by Justice **Hugo Black**) that a state must give a lawyer to a person
  charged with a felony who cannot pay. Retrial **5 August 1963**: local lawyer **W. Fred Turner**
  showed Cook had lied about his own record. The jury acquitted Gideon after **one hour**. He died
  18 January 1972. **Film:** *Gideon's Trumpet* (1980, TV film, Henry Fonda as Gideon; from Anthony
  Lewis's 1964 book).
- **Miranda v. Arizona** (WP "Miranda v. Arizona", "Ernesto Miranda"). Clears the checkpoint
  **[VERIFY] Miranda 1966**: decided **13 June 1966**, **5 to 4**, opinion by Chief Justice **Earl
  Warren**. Arrested **13 March 1963** by Phoenix officers **Carroll Cooley** and **Wilfred Young**,
  Miranda confessed after **two hours** of questioning to the kidnapping and rape of an
  18-year-old woman ten days earlier. No one told him he could stay silent or have a lawyer. What
  it changed in police work: "police departments throughout the U.S. started to issue Miranda
  warning cards for their officers to recite." Warren pointed to the FBI, which already warned
  suspects. Arizona retried Miranda without the confession and convicted him again on **1 March
  1967** (20 to 30 years). Paroled 1972. Stabbed to death in a Phoenix bar **31 January 1976**, aged
  34. *Dickerson v. United States* (2000) upheld *Miranda* against a 1968 law that tried to undo it.
  `government-politics` era 9 tells the ruling itself. Our slice is the police station.
- **In re Gault, 387 U.S. 1 (1967)** (WP "In re Gault"): in **June 1964** the Gila County, Arizona,
  sheriff took **Gerald Gault**, 15, into custody without telling his parents, after a neighbor,
  **Ora Cook**, complained of an obscene phone call. Judge **Robert McGhee** sent him to the State
  Industrial School "for the period of his minority," which meant until he turned 21 (about six
  years). An adult could have been fined for the same act. In **1967** the Court ruled **8 to 1** (Justice **Abe Fortas**)
  that children in juvenile court have the right to notice of the charges, to a lawyer, to confront
  witnesses and to stay silent. Children thread; follows the 1899 juvenile court (era 7).
- **Terry v. Ohio, 392 U.S. 1** (WP "Terry v. Ohio"): **31 October 1963**, Cleveland detective
  **Martin McFadden** stopped and patted down **John W. Terry** and found a gun. On **10 June 1968**
  the Court ruled **8 to 1** that police may stop and "frisk" (pat the outside of the clothes for
  weapons) a person they reasonably suspect is armed and about to commit a crime, even without
  grounds to arrest. This is the "stop and frisk."

### The death penalty: the last executions, *Furman* (1972), *Gregg* (1976), and the return

- **WP "Furman v. Georgia"**: decided **29 June 1972** (408 U.S. 238), 5 to 4. "There were over 600
  inmates on death row when Furman was decided." Justice **Potter Stewart**: "These death
  sentences are cruel and unusual in the same way that being struck by lightning is cruel and
  unusual." In the next four years **35 states** and the federal government wrote new death
  penalty laws.
- **WP "Gregg v. Georgia"**: decided **2 July 1976**. **Gary Gilmore** (WP "Gary Gilmore") murdered
  gas station worker **Max Jensen**, 25, on 19 July 1976 and motel manager **Bennie Bushnell**, 26,
  the next night, in Orem and Provo, Utah. He asked to be executed. A Utah firing squad (five
  shooters, one with a blank per prison officials) shot him at **8:07 a.m. on 17 January 1977** at
  the Utah State Prison. First execution after *Gregg*.

  1985 18, 1986 18, 1987 25, 1988 11, 1989 16, 1990 23, 1991 14, 1992 31, 1993 38, 1994 31, 1995 56,

  2005 60, 2006 53, 2007 42, 2008 37, 2009 52, 2010 46, 2011 43, 2012 43, 2013 39, 2014 35, 2015 28,
  2016 20, 2017 23, 2018 25, 2019 22, 2020 17, 2021 11, 2022 18, 2023 24, 2024 25, **2025 47**,
  **2026 so far 27**. Total **1,681** executions since 1977. New death sentences peaked at **325 in
  1986**. Methods since 1977: lethal injection 1,490, electrocution 163, gas 19, firing squad 6,
  hanging 3. "Across all executions since 1976, 76% of victims in the underlying crimes were white."
  (Year list read from the chart labels in the order printed. The sum of the list matches the

### Attica, 9-13 September 1971

- **WP "Attica Prison riot"**: on **9 September 1971** about **1,281** of about **2,200** men held in
  the Attica Correctional Facility, New York, took over the prison and held **42** staff hostage.
  Officer **William Quinn** was beaten and died. The men had presented demands in July about food,
  mail, medical care and parole. Governor **Nelson Rockefeller** refused to go to the prison. At
  **9:46 a.m. on 13 September** tear gas was dropped into the yard and state troopers, deputies and
  guards fired into the smoke, with shotguns loaded with buckshot among the weapons. Deaths:
  **43** in all (33 prisoners, 10 officers and employees), counting the whole four days. At least
  **39** were killed in the retaking (29 prisoners and 10 hostages). Medical examiners found all but
  one officer and three prisoners were killed by police and guards' gunfire. Rockefeller and
  officials first said prisoners had cut the hostages' throats. Examiners found the hostages died
  of gunshot wounds from the officers. Survivors were tortured afterwards (pages of the Meyer
  Report released in 2015 describe torture, burning and sexual abuse by prison authorities).
  **Charges:** **62 prisoners** were indicted (1,289 counts). **One** state trooper was indicted, for
  reckless endangerment. In December 1976 Governor **Hugh Carey** pardoned the prisoners who had
  pleaded guilty and dropped discipline against 20 officers. Settlements: **$8 million** to
  prisoners and families in **2000**, **$12 million** to employees and families in **2005**.
- **Films (WP):** *Ghosts of Attica* (Icarus Films, 2019 per WP) and *Betrayal at Attica* (HBO Max, 1 August 2021).

### The War on Drugs: who wrote the laws

- **Nixon, 17 June 1971:** called drug abuse "public enemy number one" (standard quotation;
  unconfirmed: not opened here. `drugs-alcohol` leads the War on Drugs).
- **New York, 1973 (WP "Rockefeller Drug Laws"):** Governor **Nelson Rockefeller**'s laws set **15
  years to life** for selling two ounces or possessing four ounces of heroin, cocaine or cannabis.
  New York removed the mandatory minimums in April 2009.
- **Anti-Drug Abuse Act of 1986 (WP):** after basketball player **Len Bias** died of cocaine on
  **19 June 1986**, House Speaker **Tip O'Neill** had Democratic leaders write the bill. The
  Republican Senate passed its own. President **Ronald Reagan** signed it **27 October 1986**. It set
  a **5-year** mandatory minimum for **5 grams of crack** and the same for **500 grams of powder**
  cocaine: the **100 to 1** ratio. A mandatory minimum is a shortest sentence written into the law,
  which a judge cannot go below. Average federal drug time rose from 22 to 33 months (WP).
- **US Sentencing Commission, *Report to the Congress: Cocaine and Federal Sentencing Policy*, May
  2007** (PDF downloaded and read, Table 2-1): Black people were **91.4%** of federal crack
  offenders in **1992**, 84.7% in 2000, 81.8% in 2006. White people were 3.2%, 5.6%, 8.8%. Same
  report, citing NIDA director Nora Volkow: among people admitted to treatment for crack in 2004,
  **53% were Black and 38% white**. (So most people treated for crack were not all Black, but most
  people federally sentenced for it were.)
- **1994: Violent Crime Control and Law Enforcement Act** (WP): largest crime bill in US history.
  Sponsored in the House by Rep. **Jack Brooks** (Texas). Senate version drafted by Senator **Joe
  Biden** (Delaware), chairman of the Judiciary Committee. Signed by President **Bill Clinton** in
  1994. Money for **100,000** new police officers and **$9.7 billion** for prisons. Created **60** new
  federal death-penalty offenses. Included a federal "three strikes" rule and ended Pell Grants
  (college money) for people in prison. A majority of the Congressional Black Caucus voted for it.
  The COPS office it set up has given about **$30 billion** for community policing (WP).

### Native nations and criminal law: *McGirt* (2020) and *Castro-Huerta* (2022)

- **McGirt v. Oklahoma, 591 U.S. 894** (WP): decided **9 July 2020**, **5 to 4**, opinion by Justice
  **Neil Gorsuch**. The Muscogee (Creek) reservation set aside by Congress in the 1800s was never
  ended, so Oklahoma has no power to prosecute Native people for major crimes there. Federal and
  tribal courts take those cases. Oklahoma's appeals court then applied the same reasoning to other
  nations, so almost all of eastern Oklahoma is Indian country for criminal law. **Jimcy McGirt**, a
  Seminole citizen, had been convicted in state court of sexually abusing his wife's granddaughter.
  A federal jury convicted him in November 2020. Gorsuch's opening line: "On the far end of the
  Trail of Tears was a promise" (parked from native-nations).
- **Oklahoma v. Castro-Huerta** (WP): in **2022** the Court ruled that states may also prosecute
  non-Native people for crimes against Native victims in Indian country.
