## Parked from `native-nations` (2026-08-07)
- **The Osage murders / Reign of Terror (c. 1920–1925, Osage County, Oklahoma):** more than **60 murders and suspicious deaths** of Osage headright holders during the oil boom. Mechanism: the Osage kept mineral rights collective (1906 Osage Allotment Act; per-capita "headrights"), and a 1921 guardianship law let local courts appoint white "guardians" over Osage adults' money — guardianships handed to cronies; killings by husbands and guardians to inherit headrights. The Bureau of Investigation's case on the Kyle-family murders (Mollie Burkhart's family; William Hale convicted) became one of the young FBI's first major homicide cases. `native-nations` tells the nation's-wealth angle; this chapter gets the crime and the investigation. Film: *Killers of the Flower Moon* (2023, Scorsese; Lily Gladstone as Mollie Burkhart). (Sources: Osage Nation "Did You Know? Osage Murders"; History.com "How Marriage and Murder Were Used to Steal Osage Oil Riches"; Voices of Oklahoma. Note: Hale conviction detail is standard but verify specifics when this chapter is worked.)
- ***McGirt v. Oklahoma* (July 9, 2020)** — criminal-jurisdiction earthquake: the Muscogee (Creek) reservation (3.25M acres incl. much of Tulsa) was never disestablished, so the state lacks jurisdiction over crimes by Native people there — federal and tribal courts take them; follow-on rulings extended this to the other Five Tribes. Gorsuch's opening line: "On the far end of the Trail of Tears was a promise." (Sources: Supreme Court slip op. 18-9526; Oklahoma Bar Association primer; History.com.)
- **Wounded Knee occupation siege (February 27 – May 8, 1973):** 71 days; U.S. Marshals, FBI, and other agencies cordoned the village; two occupiers died. The Marshals Service history office documents the operation. `native-nations` leads on the event's meaning. (Sources: U.S. Marshals "Incident at Wounded Knee"; SDPB.)
- **The Dakota trials, 1862:** 392 prisoners tried by military commission — some trials under five minutes, no defense counsel, no interpreters; 38 hanged December 26, 1862 (largest mass execution in U.S. history); two hanged in error. A justice-system story as much as a war story. (Sources: Minnesota Historical Society "The Trials & Hanging"; Death Penalty Information Center.)

## Parked from `rights-movements` era 8 (2026-09-09) — federal anti-lynching bills, 1900 to 1922

`rights-movements` carries the campaign; `crime-justice` owns killing outside the law and may want
the legislative record. All fetched from the U.S. House of Representatives, History, Art & Archives.

- **The first federal anti-lynching bill was H.R. 6963, introduced by Representative George Henry
  White of North Carolina on 20 January 1900.** White was the last Black member of Congress of his
  generation.
  (https://history.house.gov/Exhibitions-and-Publications/BAIC/Historical-Essays/Temporary-Farewell/Anti-Lynching-Legislation/)
- **Representative Leonidas Dyer of Missouri introduced his bill on 8 April 1918**, "to protect
  citizens of the United States against lynching in default of protection by the States." It would
  have charged lynch mobs with capital murder, tried the cases in federal court, fined the county
  where a lynching happened, jailed and fined up to **$5,000** any law officer who failed to prevent
  one or surrendered a prisoner to a mob, and barred mob participants from juries.
- **The House passed it on 26 January 1922**, with several hundred Black spectators in the gallery.
  **The Senate never voted.** Southern Democrats obstructed debate and threatened a filibuster;
  Senate Republicans dropped it.
- The NAACP published ***Thirty Years of Lynching in the United States, 1889-1919*** to push
  Congress and sent **James Weldon Johnson** to Washington as its lobbyist. Johnson: "I tramped the
  corridors of the Capitol and the two office buildings so constantly that toward the end, I could,
  I think, have been able to find my way about blindfolded."
  (https://history.house.gov/Exhibitions-and-Publications/BAIC/Historical-Essays/Temporary-Farewell/A-Generation-Lost/)
- **Not used, because not verified:** the House vote count of **230 to 119** (search summary only),
  and the NAACP total of **3,224 people lynched 1889-1918** (loc.gov PDF returns 403).

**The Springfield, Illinois riot of August 1908**, which produced the NAACP, is also `crime-justice`
material: mob violence over nearly three days, two men lynched (**Scott Burton and William
Donegan**), dozens of homes destroyed, nearly 2,000 Black residents driven out for good, state
militia called, **about 150 arrests and few convictions**.
(NPS, https://www.nps.gov/articles/illinois-1908-springfield-race-riot-archaeological-site.htm)

## Era 1: Before 1500 (T-265a, 2026-09-27)

**What can and cannot be known.** No nation north of Mexico kept written law before 1500. Every
account of how the nations handled wrongdoing comes from oral tradition written down later, or from
European and Native observers after about 1600. The book should say so, and should not date any
of these customs to a year before 1500.

### Cherokee clan law (the "law of blood")
- Primary source: "Indian Clans," *Cherokee Phoenix*, vol. 1 no. 49, 18 February 1829, p. 2 col. 5
  (Western Carolina University digital transcription,
  https://www.wcu.edu/library/DigitalCollections/CherokeePhoenix/Vol1/no49/indian-clans-page-2-column-5a.html).
  The paper was the Cherokee Nation's own newspaper. It lists the rules "previous to" the change:
  - "The Cherokee as a nation, had nothing to do with murder."
  - "Murder was punished upon the principle of retaliation."
  - "It belonged to the clan of the murdered to revenge his death."
  - "If the murderer fled, his brother or nearest relative was liable to suffer in his stead."
  - "If a man killed his brother, he was amenable to no law or clan."
  - "To kill, under any circumstance whatever, was considered murder, and punished accordingly."
    (So an accident counted as murder.)
  - The Cherokee had seven clans (Wolf, Deer, Paint and others). A child took the mother's clan.
  - The Cherokee "about twenty years ago in a solemn council, abolished it. From that time, murder
    has been considered a governmental crime." (Twenty years before 1829 = about 1809.)
  - The 1829 editor calls the old rules "savage and barbarous." That is the editor's judgment
    (the book must not adopt it, and may attribute it if used).
- Encyclopedia.com, "Native American Law: Blood Revenge" (American Eras; cites Charles Hudson, John
  Phillip Reid, Daniel K. Richter): the killing was "a private matter between the clans of the
  victim and the assailant"; the killer's relatives had "a sacred obligation to produce a life in
  exchange"; "The lex talionis also did not make a distinction between an accidental death or a
  premeditated homicide"; communities sometimes accepted "compensation payments in wampum, furs, or
  other goods instead of retaliating against the killer."
  (https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/news-wires-white-papers-and-books/native-american-law-blood-revenge)
- Date of the Cherokee abolition: Cherokee Nation's own social-media post says 1810; other summaries
  give a council at Broom's Town on 11 September 1808 and an act of 1810 (unconfirmed: search summary
  only). This is era 6 (T-265b). For era 1 use "about twenty years before 1829" from the *Phoenix*.
- Chota as a Cherokee "town of refuge" where no one could be harmed (unconfirmed: search summary
  only, Wikipedia-derived). Not used.

### Haudenosaunee: condolence and the Great Law
- NYSED, "The Great Law of Peace" teaching unit (New York State Education Department, Indigenous
  Education; https://www.nysed.gov/sites/default/files/programs/indigenous-education/unit-the-great-law-of-peace.pdf):
  the five nations (Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, Seneca) tell that before the Peacemaker they
  were caught in feuds. Hiawatha, grieving his murdered daughters, made strings "forming in his mind
  words of compassion and consolation, rather than vengeance," then shell wampum strings for "the
  words of condolence" that became "central to the Great Law of Peace."
- The Great Law in English: Arthur C. Parker, *The Constitution of the Five Nations*, New York State
  Museum Bulletin 184 (Albany, 1916), from manuscripts collected at Six Nations of the Grand River
  (Ontario) in 1910, one by Seth Newhouse (Mohawk) and one by the Six Nations chiefs (LOC record
  https://www.loc.gov/item/16027201/). Text read at Portland State's transcription,
  https://web.pdx.edu/~caskeym/iroquois_web/html/greatlaw.html:
  - Art. 20: if a Lord (chief) "should commit murder," the other Lords depose him. The War Chief
    says: "I now depose you and expel you and you shall depart at once from the territory of the
    Five Nations Confederacy and nevermore return again." The title passes to a sister family.
  - Art. 74: an adopted outsider who does wrong is first reprimanded by a war chief, and "if a
    similar offense is again committed the offending party or parties shall be expelled from the
    territory of the Five United Nations."
  - Caution for the writer: these are written versions from about 1900 of an older oral law. The
    book should say the law was told aloud for generations and written in English about 1900.
- "Twenty strings of wampum were given for murder as the penalty for homicide" (unconfirmed: search
  summary only). Not used.

### Southeastern nations: forgiveness at the Green Corn
- Eric E. Bowne, "Green Corn Ceremony," *Encyclopedia of Alabama*
  (https://encyclopediaofalabama.org/article/green-corn-ceremony/): the busk (from the Creek word
  for "to fast") was held in midsummer when the early corn ripened. People rekindled the sacred fire
  in the town square, and "the societies at large forgave all crimes except murder." Written
  accounts come from trader James Adair (mid-1700s), naturalist William Bartram and agent Benjamin
  Hawkins. Detailed descriptions date from the late 1800s and early 1900s.

## Era 2: The 1500s (T-265a, 2026-09-27)

**Thin, honestly.** European law reached this land only at a few Spanish outposts: St. Augustine
(1565) and Oñate's New Mexico colony (1598). English Roanoke (1585-1587) left no court record of
its own that this search found. No sheriff, jail or court of the later kind existed yet.

### Who judged, under Spanish law
- Geoffrey Pivateau, "Alcalde," *Handbook of Texas* (TSHA, https://www.tshaonline.org/handbook/entries/alcalde):
  the alcalde's "primary duties was to act as a judge. Though his judicial authority was limited to
  lesser civil and criminal issues, in practice he handled most judicial matters occurring within
  the limits of his jurisdiction." Appeals went to the *justicia mayor* "or to the governor himself."
  So in a Spanish colony the same officials governed, commanded soldiers and judged.
  (Encyclopedia.com and Britannica "alcalde" agree in search summaries.)

### The Acoma trial, February 1599 (justice angle; `native-nations` leads the event)
All from `research/research-native-nations.md`, section "Acoma Pueblo, December 1598 - February
1599" (verified 2026-09-07), which cites: Rebecca Carlson 2008 thesis (citing Simmons, *The Last
Conquistador*, 1991, and the trial record in Hammond and Rey, *Don Juan de Oñate*, 1953); EBSCO
"Battle of Acoma"; NPS "Acoma Pueblo"; NPS "Traditional Groups along El Camino Real"; AP (June 27,
2020); NPR (July 13, 2020); All Pueblo Council of Governors (Oct. 6, 2023); Sherry Robinson (2023)
quoting Kessell.
- December 1598: Acoma people killed Juan de Zaldívar and eleven (or twelve) of his men after the
  soldiers demanded food. The Acoma account says the soldiers also assaulted (raped) an Acoma woman.
- Oñate opened a judicial inquiry on 28 December 1598 and asked the Franciscan friars whether war
  would be just. They said yes. In January 1599 Vicente de Zaldívar led about 70 soldiers, who
  stormed and burned the pueblo. Acoma dead: about 600 (NM State Historian), 800 (All Pueblo
  Council), about 1,500 (NPS). Some 500 captives (EBSCO), 507 tried (NLM).
- The captives were tried before Oñate himself, the man who had ordered the attack. The trial began
  9 February 1599 and lasted three days (Carlson, citing Simmons); the sentence is dated 12 February
  1599 in other sources. Location: Santo Domingo Pueblo (Simmons) or San Juan (Wikipedia): not settled.
- Oñate appointed a defender, who asked for mercy. Captain Alonso Gómez Montesinos is named as the
  defender in some accounts (Wikipedia, uncited: treat as unconfirmed).
- Acoma witnesses said many had been working in their fields during the first fight, and that
  "some wanted to surrender but some refused, and so they fought" (trial record via Carlson).
- Sentence (no death sentences): men over 25, one foot cut off and twenty years of "personal
  servitude"; males 12-25 and females over 12, twenty years of servitude; children under 12 taken
  from their families and given to the friars (NPS: some 70 girls sent to convents in Mexico, "in
  all probability, none ever returned home"; All Pueblo Council: 60 children); two Hopi men had the
  right hand cut off and were sent home to spread word.
- Servitude, in plain words: the prisoners were handed to Spanish colonists to work for them, with
  no pay and no right to leave, for twenty years.
- The number whose feet were cut: 24, reported by AP, NPR and the Acoma oral tradition (NYT 1998).
  Historians Marc Simmons (who later changed his view) and John L. Kessell doubt the cutting was
  carried out. Kessell: "the historical record makes no mention of a one-footed Acoma slave."
  **Settled:** the order, the ages, the twenty-year terms, the taking of the children. **Not
  settled:** how many men were actually cut, and whether it was the whole foot.
- Method, stated honestly: the record gives the order to cut off a foot. It does not describe how.
  (Clinical-word rule: say what the sentence was, and that the record does not say how, or whether,
  it was done to each man.)
- Oñate's own trial (1612-1614) is era 3.

## Era 3: The 1600s (T-265a, 2026-09-27)

### Jamestown under martial law, 1610-1619
- Brent Tarter, "Lawes Divine, Morall and Martiall," *Encyclopedia Virginia*
  (https://encyclopediavirginia.org/entries/lawes-divine-morall-and-martiall/): Sir Thomas Gates
  issued the first orders on 24 May 1610. Thomas West, baron De La Warr, confirmed and added to them
  on 12 June 1610. Sir Thomas Dale issued military rules on 22 June 1611. William Strachey published
  the collection in 1612. Death for "blasphemy, uttering treasonous words or words critical of the
  company, murder, sodomy, robbery," and trading with Indians without permission. Whipping for
  "gambling, failing to attend church, fornication, adultery, unnecessarily killing livestock, or
  stealing agricultural implements or other people's crops." Government under the Lawes ceased when
  Sir George Yeardley arrived, 18 April 1619.
- Tarter, "Sir Thomas Dale (d. 1619)," *Encyclopedia Virginia*: Dale condemned "a man who stole food
  to be tied to a tree and left to starve to death as a warning to others."
- George Percy, "A Trewe Relacyon" (written 1625, printed 1922), transcription in *Colonial
  Williamsburg Journal*, Winter 2007
  (https://research.colonialwilliamsburg.org/Foundation/journal/Winter07/A%20Trewe%20Relation.pdf),
  on the building of the fort at Henricus (1611): men who "did Runne away unto the Indyans," when
  caught, Dale "cawsed to be executed. Some he apointed to be hanged some burned some to be broken
  upon wheles others to be Staked and some to be shott to deathe all theis extreme and crewell
  tortures he used and inflicted upon them To terrefy the reste." Men who robbed the storehouse
  he had "bownd faste unto Trees and so sterved them to deathe." No count and no names.
  Percy wrote in part to defend his own record. Attribute to him. "Staked" is not explained in
  the text: the prose must say the account does not explain it.

### Salem, 1692
- Britannica, "Salem witch trials" (fetched with curl 2026-09-27): trials June 1692 to May 1693.
  "19 people had been hanged and 5 others had died in custody. Additionally, a man was pressed
  beneath heavy stones until he died." First accusers: Betty Parris (9), Abigail Williams (11),
  Ann Putnam Jr. (about 12). First accused: Tituba (enslaved in minister Samuel Parris's household),
  Sarah Good, Sarah Osborn. Magistrates John Hathorne and Jonathan Corwin held the first
  examinations on 1 March 1692. Tituba denied it, then, "after being repeatedly badgered,"
  confessed. Putnam family members and in-laws were accusers "in dozens of cases." After his own
  wife was accused, Governor William Phips set up a new court that would not allow spectral
  evidence. "By May 1693 everyone in custody ... had been pardoned by Phips."
- Jess Blumberg, "A Brief History of the Salem Witch Trials," *Smithsonian* (updated 24 Oct 2022,
  https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/a-brief-history-of-the-salem-witch-trials-175162489/):
  "more than 200 people" accused. "At least five" died in jail. Court of Oyer and Terminer
  established 27 May 1692. Spectral evidence = "testimony about dreams and visions." Increase
  Mather: "It were better that ten suspected witches should escape than one innocent person be
  condemned." Court dissolved 29 October 1692. The Superior Court of Judicature that replaced it
  "condemned just 3 out of 56 defendants." Fast day 14 January 1697. Trials declared unlawful 1702.
  £600 to heirs 1711. Formal apology 1957. Giles Corey was 71 (Smithsonian).
  Correction note: the fetch summary gave "20 were executed by hanging." The count is 19 hanged plus
  Corey pressed (Britannica). Use 19 + 1.
- (unconfirmed: search summary only) William Stoughton, lieutenant governor, presided over seven
  judges. The 1711 act named 22 people.
- Giles Corey: Heather Rockwood, "Giles Corey, pressed to death," Massachusetts Historical Society
  *Beehive* blog, 17 Sept 2021 (https://www.masshist.org/beehiveblog/2021/09/giles-corey-pressed-to-death/):
  pressed 19 September 1692 (Samuel Sewall's diary: "pressed to death for standing mute"). He was
  made to "strip naked and lay down, face up, on the ground. A wooden board was then placed on top
  of him, and on top of the board, one by one, Sheriff George Corwin placed large rocks." It went on
  two days. He died on the third. He had refused to plead guilty or not guilty. The MHS post gives
  the reason as keeping his estate for his children (attribute it). "More weight" as his last words:
  NOT used. The UVA archive index lists his death as 16 September. MHS and Sewall give 19 September.
- Rebecca Nurse, case file, *Salem Witchcraft Papers* No. 94, University of Virginia Salem Witch
  Trials Documentary Archive (https://salem.lib.virginia.edu/n94.html):
  - 23 March 1692: warrant on the complaint of Edward Putnam and Jonathan Putnam, for afflicting Ann
    Putnam Sr., Ann Putnam Jr. and Abigail Williams, signed by magistrates John Hathorne and
    Jonathan Corwin. Marshal George Herrick arrested her 24 March.
  - 24 March examination: "I can say before my Eternal father I am innocent, & God will clear my
    innocency." "I am as clear as the child unborn." When she moved her hands, the accusers were
    recorded as falling into fits. She said she had been sick and not able to leave the house
    "these 8. or 9. dayes."
  - Four indictments: afflicting Ann Putnam Jr., Mary Walcott, Elizabeth Hubbard, Abigail Williams.
  - Petition of 39 neighbors (signers include Israel and Elizabeth Porter and several Putnams): "we
    never had Any: cause or grounds to suspect her of Any such thing as she is nowe Acused of."
  - 28 June 1692, her petition to the court: women had searched her body for a "Supernaturall
    Marke." The most experienced of them saw nothing but what "might Arise from A naturall Cause."
    She asked for other women, two of them midwives, to examine her before her trial. She signed
    with her mark (she could not write her name).
  - Juror Thomas Fisk's declaration, 4 July 1692 (printed by Robert Calef, 1700): the jury first
    found her not guilty. "The honoured Court was pleased to object against it," pointing to her
    words about Goodwife Hobbs and her daughter ("they used to come among us"). The jury went out
    again. In court she "made no reply," and Fisk took that as "a principal Evidence against her."
  - Her appeal: she meant only that they were fellow prisoners. "I being something hard of hearing,
    and full of grief, none informing me how the Court took up my words."
  - UVA summary (https://salem.lib.virginia.edu/minis/nurse1.html): tried June 1692. "Governor Phips
    pardoned her, but was later persuaded to reverse his decision by several men from Salem."

### Massachusetts Bay court records, 1631-1635: the watch, whipping, stocks, ears, branding
Source for every item here: Nathaniel B. Shurtleff, ed., *Records of the Governor and Company of the
Massachusetts Bay in New England*, vol. 1 (Boston, 1853), full text at
https://archive.org/details/recordsofgoverno01mass (read from the djvu text, 2026-09-27). Spelling as printed.
- **The first night watches.** Court of Assistants, Boston, 12 April 1631: "there shalbe a watch of 4
  kept evy night att Dorchester, & another of 4 att Waterton, the watches to begin att sunsett."
  Anyone who fired a gun after the watch was set forfeited 40 shillings or, if unable to pay, "to be
  whipped." Same court: Richard Norman fined for "negligence in watching" and Daniel Abbott fined
  "for refuseing to watch." Court of 5 July 1631: "there shalbe a watch of sixe & an officer kept
  evy night att Boston, 2 whereof are to be of Boston, 2 of Charlton, and 2 of Rocksbury." So the
  watchmen were ordinary settlers taking turns, and a man could be fined for refusing.
- **Philip Ratcliffe (spelled "Phillip Ratliffe" in the index, p. 88).** Court at Boston, 14 June
  1631: "It is ordered, that Phillip Ratliffe shalbe whipped, haue his eares cutt of, fyned 40£, &
  banished out of ye lymitts of this jurisdiccon, for vttering mallitious & scandulous speeches
  against the govmt & the church of Salem, &c., as appeareth by a particul[ar] thereof, proved vpon
  oath." (The printed OCR shows "40*"; Winthrop's journal, per standard secondary accounts, gives
  forty pounds. The record does not say how the ears were cut or by whom.) Same court: Philip
  Swaddon to be whipped "for running away from his maister, Robt Seely, intending to goe to Virginia."
- **Stocks.** Earlier 1630-31 court (same volume, before the March 1631 courts): "John Goulworth
  shalbe whipped, and afterwards sett in the stocks, for fellony committed by him, whereof hee is
  convicted by his owne confession"; Henry Lyn whipped "for the like offence"; John Boggust and John
  Pickryn "to sitt in the stocks 4 howers togeather, att Salem, for being accessary."
  Also: Bartholomew Hill "adiudged to be whipt for stealeing a loafe of breade from John Hoskins";
  John Baker "whipped for shooteing att fowle on the Sabboth day."
- **Bilboes** (iron shackles for the ankles; definition STILL TO SOURCE if used): William James for
  "incontinency" set "in the bilboes at Boston" and "in the stocks at Salem"; Edward Seale, for
  drunkenness, "set in the bilboes till the end of the Court, & then to bee severely whiped" (1630s).
- **Branding.** Court at Boston, 4 September 1632: "Richard Hopkins shalbe severely whipt, &
  branded with a hott iron on one of his cheekes, for selling peeces & powder & shott to the
  Indeans." Court at Newtown, 1 September 1635: Robert Scarlett, "a knowen theife," to be "severely
  whipt & branded in the forehead with a T," then sent back to his master Benjamin Felton, who must
  send him out of the colony. (Branding, in plain words, from the record itself: a hot iron pressed
  into the skin burned a mark or a letter that stayed for life. T stood for thief.)
- Also in this volume (1631, Chickatabut): the Massachusett sachem Chickatabut was fined a beaver
  skin for shooting a swine of Sir Richard Saltonstall. Native people were brought under the
  colony's court from its first year.

### A Native murder trial with a mixed jury: Plymouth, June 1675
Source: Nathaniel B. Shurtleff, ed., *Records of the Colony of New Plymouth*, vol. 5, Court Orders
1668-1678 (Boston, 1856), pp. 159, 167-168, full text https://archive.org/details/recordsofcolonyo05newp.
- 1 March 1674/5: the court required "an Indian called Tobias" to answer questions about "the
  suddaine and violent death of an Indian called John Sassamon." Tobias, Tuspaquin "the black
  sachem" of Namassakett, and Tuspaquin's son William pledged their lands, worth £100, that Tobias
  would appear at the June court.
- June 1675: "three natiues were araigned, vizt, Tobias, & Wampapaquan, and Mattashunannamo," accused
  of having, on 29 January 1674/5 (1675 in today's calendar), at Assowamsett Pond, murdered John
  Sassamon, "an other Indian, by laying violent hands on him and striking him, or twisting his necke,
  vntill hee was dead," and of casting "his dead body through a hole of the ice into the said pond."
  They denied it.
- The jury: twelve English colonists (William Sabine, William Crocker, Edward Sturgis, William
  Brookes, Nathaniel Winslow, John Wadsworth, Andrew Ringe, Robert Vixon, John Done, Jonathan Banges,
  Jonathan Shaw, Benjamin Higgins). The court added "some of the most indifferentest, gravest, and
  sage Indians" to "consult and advice" with the jury: Hope, Maskippague, Wannoo, George, Wampye and
  Acanootus, who "fully concurred." The six Native men advised. They were not sworn jurors.
- Verdict: "Wee, of the jury, one and all, both English and Indians, doe joyntly and with one
  consent agree" that Tobias, "his son Wampapaquan," and Mattashunannamo were guilty.
- Sentence: to be "hanged by the head vntill theire bodies are dead." Tobias and Mattashunannamo
  were hanged 8 June 1675. Wampapaquan was reprieved for a month, "But afterwards shott to death
  within the said month."
- What followed: King Philip's War began in June 1675 at Swansea, after Plymouth executed the three
  men (`research/research-war.md` §3.4, citing NPS, "The Unexpected Start to Metacom's War").
  `war` and `native-nations` lead the war. DEFECT NOTED (not ours to fix): `outlines/war.md` says
  Plymouth "hanged three Wampanoag men." The record says two were hanged and the third was shot a
  month later. Listed in the checkpoint TO PARK.
- Not in this record, and not used: what Sassamon had told Plymouth's governor before his death, and
  which nation each of the three men belonged to (later histories call them Wampanoag).

### Killing an enslaved person during "correction" made no crime: Virginia, October 1669
- William Waller Hening, ed., *The Statutes at Large ... of Virginia*, vol. 2 (1823), p. 270, full
  text https://archive.org/details/statutesatlargeb02virg: "An act about the casuall killing of
  slaves," assembly at James City, session prorogued to 20 October 1669, Sir William Berkeley
  governor. Text: "if any slave resist his master (or other by his masters order correcting him) and
  by the extremity of the correction should chance to die, that his death shall not be accompted
  felony, but the master (or that other person appointed by the master to punish him) be acquit from
  molestation, since it cannot be presumed that prepensed malice (which alone makes murther felony)
  should induce any man to destroy his owne estate."
  Plain words: "correction" meant a beating. If a master, or someone he ordered, beat an enslaved
  person to death, it was not treated as murder, because the lawmakers said no one would mean to
  destroy his own property. `slavery-freedom` leads slave law and tells the 1705 code. This chapter
  uses the act for who could kill without being punished.

### Oñate convicted, 1614 (justice angle; sources in `research/research-native-nations.md` §5)
Called to account in Mexico City, arraigned on 30 charges (1612 or 1613: sources differ), convicted
1614 on 12, including using excessive force against the Acoma and unjustly hanging two Indians.
Banished from New Mexico for life, exiled from Mexico City for four years (Carlson, TSHA, Oklahoma
Historical Society; PBS POV says five), fined 6,000 ducats. Whether he was later pardoned is not
settled. No Acoma person was asked to testify at his trial in any source read (native-nations bank:
"Nobody at Acoma was asked, and nothing was returned").
