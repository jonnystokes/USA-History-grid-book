## 1. Before 1500

### No money, working trade
- There was no coin, paper money, mint, bank, or price list anywhere in this land before 1500. Goods moved through barter, gift exchange, and obligation between people and between nations, across long distances — Gulf coast shells, Lake Superior copper, and western obsidian traveled hundreds of miles along rivers and trails. (Sources: Smithsonian/NMAI trade-network materials as compiled in `research/research-native-nations.md` and `research/research-economy.md`; the trade-network detail is `economy`'s and `native-nations`' lead — one clause here.)

### Wampum — what it actually was (do NOT call it "Indian money")
- Wampum is beads made from shell — white beads from whelk shells, purple-black beads from the quahog clam — strung or woven into strings and belts. (Sources: Coin and Currency Collections, University of Notre Dame, "Wampum: Introduction"; LOC Inside Adams blog "Shell Money," July 2024.)
- Its own uses were records, diplomacy, ceremony, and gifts — not buying and selling. Belts woven from the beads recorded agreements, treaties, and events; among the Haudenosaunee, wampum belts encoded oral agreements and histories, and belts were presented and read in councils. (Sources: Clements Library exhibit "American Encounters," Case 6 "Sacred Objects, Instruments of Negotiation, and Commodities"; LOC "Shell Money"; Onondaga Nation materials via `research/research-native-nations.md`.)
- Wampum's use AS currency was a colonial-era development in dealings between colonists and Native traders (see era 3). Before Europeans arrived, it was not a currency. (Sources: JSTOR Daily "Wampum Was Massachusetts' First Legal Currency"; Notre Dame Coin and Currency Collections intro.)
- **`native-nations` leads wampum's meaning** (Two Row Wampum, Covenant Chain — already in their outline). This chapter says plainly what wampum was, then tells only the currency episode.

## 2. The 1500s

### Spanish silver — somebody else's money passing by
- Spain found huge silver deposits at Potosí (in present-day Bolivia) in 1545 and at Zacatecas in Mexico; Spanish mints in the Americas struck the silver 8-real coin — the peso, called the "piece of eight" in English. Historians of money describe it as the first global currency: it circulated from South America through Europe and across the Pacific to Asia. (Sources: University of London Press, *New World Objects of Knowledge*, "Piece of Eight" chapter by Alejandra Irigoin and Bridget Millmore; CoinsWeekly "How the 8 Reales Became a World Currency"; Britannica/Wikipedia "Spanish dollar" summarizing the same literature.)
- In the land that became the United States there was still no mint and no money economy in the 1500s; Spanish coin belonged to Spain's empire and almost none of it circulated here yet. St. Augustine (founded 1565) was a paid Spanish garrison town — the payroll came from Spanish colonial funds, the coin of a foreign system. (Honest-thin framing; garrison funding detail general from St. Augustine histories — keep the outline line minimal.)
- Later American connection, for era 5+: the U.S. dollar was modeled on the Spanish milled dollar, and Spanish coins stayed legal tender in the United States until the Coinage Act of February 21, 1857 ended legal-tender status for foreign coin. (Sources: Coinage Act of 1857 — Wikipedia with statute citations; NMAH object records on the Spanish dollar.)
- "Two bits" for a quarter comes from the piece of eight: the coin was divided into eight "bits" or reales, so two bits = one quarter of a dollar. (Sources: NMAH/numismatic literature via CoinsWeekly and the Notre Dame collections; widely documented usage history.)

## 3. The 1600s

### Coin shortage and commodity money
- English colonies were chronically short of coin: England sent little, and what coin arrived flowed back out to pay for imports. Colonists paid each other in goods rated by law — "country pay." (Sources: Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland, Economic Commentary 2015-06, "Paper Money and Inflation in Colonial America"; TIME "History of Money in America" summarizing the same.)
- **Wampum as colonial legal tender, Massachusetts:** on November 15, 1637 the Massachusetts General Court made wampum receivable at 6 beads a penny for sums under 12 pence; on October 18, 1650 rates were set at 8 white or 4 black beads a penny and wampum made legal tender up to 40 shillings (not for taxes); on May 22, 1661 the law was repealed. (Source: University of Notre Dame Coin and Currency Collections, "Wampum: Introduction" — the standard numismatic chronology.)
- **Tobacco as money:** Virginia rated tobacco as currency from the 1620s and made it legal tender in 1642; Virginia, Maryland, and North Carolina used tobacco as money through much of the colonial period. (Sources: Encyclopedia.com "Tobacco as Money"; Cleveland Fed EC 2015-06.)
- **Beaver pelts and corn** also passed as money in the northern colonies. (Sources: Encyclopedia.com "Tobacco as Money" and general colonial-money literature; TIME history of colonial money.)

### The pine-tree shilling and the Boston mint, 1652
- The Massachusetts General Court set up a mint in Boston in 1652 — May 27, 1652 appointment — with silversmith **John Hull** as mintmaster, partnered with Robert Sanderson. It struck silver threepence, sixpence, and shillings. England was not asked; minting coin was a royal prerogative, but in 1652 England had no king (the Commonwealth period after Charles I's execution). (Sources: CoinWeek "The Boston Mint and the First Colonial Coins"; Smithsonian NMAH object record "1 Pine Tree Shilling, United States, 1652"; New England Historical Society "In 1652 John Hull Built a Fortune – One Shilling at a Time.")
- **The frozen date:** pine-tree-design shillings were actually struck from about 1667 to 1682, but nearly all Massachusetts silver coins bear the date 1652. Numismatic historians explain the frozen date as cover: after Charles II took the throne in 1660, coins dated 1652 could be passed off as leftovers from the kingless years rather than fresh defiance. The mint closed in 1682, and the crown revoked the Massachusetts charter in 1684 with the illegal mint among the complaints. (Sources: CoinWeek; Wikipedia "Pine tree shilling" citing Crosby and other numismatic literature; ANS digital library "Pine Tree Coinage of Massachusetts." The cover-story explanation is the standard numismatic account — attribute to historians, not stated as the colony's recorded motive.)
- **John Hull** (1624–1683): came to Boston from England as a boy in 1635, trained as a silversmith, took a cut of every coin struck (his contract paid him in shillings per batch), became one of New England's richest men and colony treasurer. The tale that he gave his daughter Hannah her weight in pine-tree shillings as a dowry is a legend, retold and embroidered by Nathaniel Hawthorne in *Grandfather's Chair* — tell it only labeled as legend, if at all. (Sources: New England Historical Society article; CoinWeek. No film about Hull exists.)

### 1690 — the first government paper money in the Western world
- Massachusetts printed paper "bills of credit" to pay soldiers back from the failed 1690 expedition against French Quebec (King William's War) — the colony's treasury had too little coin and the troops, by contemporary account, were near mutiny. The legislature authorized the issue dated February 3, 1690/91 (old-style calendar; the vote chain began in December 1690), for 40,000 pounds. Standard histories call these bills the first paper money issued by any government in the Western world; China had used paper money centuries earlier. (Sources: New England Historical Society "Massachusetts Issues the First Paper Money in the Western World"; American Numismatic Society "A History of American Currency." Dating note: popular summaries give "December 1690" and "February 3, 1690/91" — the old-style/new-style calendar makes both defensible; the outline says "in 1690" and stays safe.)
- Each bill promised the treasurer would accept it "in all Public payments" and that it could later be redeemed for coin. The bills circulated; by 1714 Massachusetts had issued 240,000 pounds, and by 1718 the other New England colonies had followed. (Source: New England Historical Society, same article.)

## 4. 1700 to 1750

### Colonial paper spreads — and misbehaves in places
- Every colony eventually printed paper bills of credit. Where colonies printed restrained amounts (the middle colonies — Pennsylvania, New York, New Jersey), the money held its value; where they printed heavily (New England, the Carolinas), prices in paper rose and the bills lost value against silver. (Source: Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland, Economic Commentary 2015-06, "Paper Money and Inflation in Colonial America" — its central finding.)
- **Tobacco notes:** Virginia set up public tobacco warehouses with government inspectors (system built out in the 1710s–1730s; the standard dates cited are 1713 for early warehouses and 1727/1730 for the inspection-and-notes system — sources differ on which year to headline; state the 1720s–1730s). The inspector's receipt — a "tobacco note" — certified stored, inspected tobacco and passed from hand to hand as money. (Sources: Encyclopedia.com "Tobacco as Money" (1727); Mises Institute "The Tobacco Standard in Colonial America" (1713 warehouses, 1730 inspection law); the *Journal of Risk and Financial Management* / Business Perspectives paper "Were tobacco warehouse receipts an economic form of money…" for the receipts-as-money analysis.)
- **Benjamin Franklin and paper money:** Franklin published *A Modest Enquiry into the Nature and Necessity of a Paper-Currency* in 1729, at age 23, arguing Pennsylvania needed more paper money; the pamphlet helped win his print shop the contract to print Pennsylvania's bills. His firm printed money for Pennsylvania, New Jersey, and Delaware. (Sources: Library of Congress exhibition "Benjamin Franklin: In His Own Words — Printer and Writer"; Library Company of Philadelphia materials.)
- **Franklin's anti-counterfeiting leaf prints:** Franklin printed detailed impressions of real leaves on the bills — "nature printing," almost certainly developed from prints he made with his friend Joseph Breintnall; the plates were probably cast in type metal from plaster molds of actual leaves. A leaf's vein pattern was effectively impossible for a counterfeiter to re-engrave by hand. A 2023 University of Notre Dame physics study of 600 Franklin-printed notes confirmed distinctive security features: the leaf prints, mica flakes in the paper, and colored-fiber paper recipes. Franklin-style leaf cuts stayed on colonial money into the Revolution. Colonial bills carried the warning "To Counterfeit is Death" — counterfeiting was a capital crime. (Sources: The Conversation, "We analyzed paper money printed by Ben Franklin…" (the Notre Dame study, published in PNAS 2023); Popular Science write-up of the same study; Library Company of Philadelphia on Breintnall; LOC Franklin exhibition.)
- Movie for Franklin story: **Benjamin Franklin (2022)** — Ken Burns' PBS documentary; exists, verified; already used by `technology`, `science`, `work-workers` story blocks for Franklin. (Source: PBS.)

### PATCH 2026-09-27 (T-252): paper money lent against land and against enslaved people, South Carolina
- South Carolina's provincial government began issuing ("emitting") paper money in **1703**. At first 1.5 South Carolina pounds equaled one pound sterling. By the late 1720s the rate had fallen to about **7 South Carolina pounds to 1 pound sterling**. (Source: Nic Butler, Charleston County Public Library, "Cash and Credit in South Carolina before the U.S. Dollar," April 12, 2024, opened.)
- South Carolina opened a land bank in **1712**: paper bills lent in limited sums on mortgages of land, with interest due each year and the principal repaid over eight or ten years. As borrowers repaid, officials burned part of the returned bills (unconfirmed: search summary only; the summary appears to come from the CCPL article or the NBER paper on North Carolina paper money, w23783).
- There were no banks in colonial South Carolina. People borrowed through private mortgages and bonds. A mortgage could be on land or on movable property, called chattel, and enslaved people were recorded as chattel. Butler: "A slave owner could mortgage their human chattel to a slave broker to secure credit used to purchase more slaves, then use their collective labor to satisfy the debt." Hundreds of colonial land mortgages survive in the Charleston County Register of Deeds, and thousands of early chattel mortgages are in the South Carolina Department of Archives and History. (Source: CCPL article, opened.) This is the colonial root of the 1830s planters' banks in era 6. Owner note: `slavery-freedom` leads people as property. This chapter states only the money mechanism: a person pledged as security for a loan.

## 5. 1750 to 1800

### London bans colonial paper
- **Currency Act of 1751:** Parliament barred the New England colonies (Massachusetts Bay, Rhode Island, Connecticut, New Hampshire) from issuing new bills of credit as legal tender — the answer to New England's depreciated paper. **Currency Act of April 19, 1764:** Parliament extended the ban on legal-tender paper to all thirteen colonies. The acts squeezed colonies already short of coin, and colonists listed them among their grievances with Britain. (Sources: Colonial Williamsburg "ouramericanrevolution.org" Currency Act of 1764 entry; American History Central "Currency Act"; Wikipedia "Currency Act" with statute citations.)

### The Continental dollar — paper that failed
- The Continental Congress had no power to tax, so it printed: Continental currency from June 1775, eventually $241,552,780 in total issues. The British counterfeited it on purpose to speed its collapse. (Sources: Harvard Library CURIOSity "Continental Currency"; New Jersey State Library "Not Worth a Continental"; Notre Dame Coin and Currency Collections.)
- Depreciation series (verified in the `economy` pass, sources kept): about **4 paper dollars to 1 silver by end of 1777**; about **8 to 1 by end of 1778**; about **100 to 1 by 1781** (Philadelphia; worse in some states — 210:1 North Carolina), when the paper stopped circulating. The collapse wiped out roughly **$226 million face value** (about $40 million in silver terms) — carried by whoever held the bills, mostly ordinary people. (Sources: Harvard CURIOSity "Continental Currency"; NY Fed Liberty Street Economics "Crisis Chronicles: Not Worth a Continental.")
- **"Not worth a Continental":** the phrase arose during and just after the Revolution for anything worthless, from the currency's collapse; it is documented in idiom histories and period usage, and by the late 1800s had crossed to Britain. (Sources: New Jersey State Library digital collection "Not Worth a Continental"; idiom-history references. The phrase's existence and origin are well documented; no single first use is recorded — do not invent one.)

### Building the dollar on purpose
- **Decimal money — Jefferson's part:** Thomas Jefferson's "Notes on the Establishment of a Money Unit, and of a Coinage for the United States" (written 1784) argued for the Spanish dollar as the unit, divided decimally — tenths and hundredths — instead of English pounds/shillings/pence. On **July 6, 1785** Congress resolved that "the money unit of the United States of America shall be one dollar" and that coins should "increase in decimal ratio." The United States was the first nation to adopt a fully decimal coinage. (Sources: Monticello "Currency and Coinage" (Jefferson Encyclopedia); Library of Congress, Jefferson Papers, "Notes on the Establishment of a Money Unit"; Founders Online.)
- **First Bank of the United States, 1791:** Hamilton's December 1790 *Report on a National Bank* proposed it; Congress chartered it **February 25, 1791** for twenty years, based in Philadelphia — the new government's fiscal agent, a holder of federal deposits, and an issuer of trusted banknotes. Jefferson and Madison opposed it as unconstitutional; Washington signed it after taking both sides' written opinions. Its charter lapsed in 1811 by one vote in each house. (Sources: Federal Reserve History essay "The First Bank of the United States"; National Archives Founders Online for the reports and opinions.)
- **Coinage Act of April 2, 1792:** established the U.S. Mint at Philadelphia (the first federal building erected under the Constitution), made the **dollar** the money unit, defined coins in gold (eagles), silver (dollars down to half-dimes), and copper (cents and half-cents), and fixed the decimal system of dollars, dimes, cents, and mills in law. (Sources: U.S. Mint "Philadelphia Mint Celebrates Its 230th Anniversary"; Statutes and Stories "Coinage Act of 1792" with statute text; the act itself.)
- Hamilton's January 1791 *Report on the Establishment of a Mint* did the design work behind the act (bimetallic dollar defined in both silver and gold at 15:1). (Sources: Founders Online, Report on the Establishment of a Mint; Federal Reserve History context essays.)

## 6. 1800 to 1850

### The Bank War — both sides plainly
- The **Second Bank of the United States** was chartered in 1816 (twenty years, Philadelphia) after the money chaos of the War of 1812. **Nicholas Biddle**, its president from 1823, ran it as a proto-central bank: it held federal deposits, moved money across the country, and disciplined state banks by presenting their notes for redemption. (Sources: Federal Reserve History essay "The Second Bank of the United States"; Britannica "Bank War.")
- **Andrew Jackson's case against it:** a private corporation with public power and foreign shareholders, unaccountable to voters, favoring the connected rich. Congress passed recharter in July 1832 (four years early, Henry Clay's election-year move); **Jackson vetoed it July 10, 1832**, calling the Bank "unauthorized by the Constitution, subversive of the rights of the States, and dangerous to the liberties of the people," and won reelection that fall over Clay. (Sources: Britannica "Bank War"; Richmond Fed *Econ Focus* "The Bank War," 2023Q2; veto message text at the Avalon Project/Miller Center.)
- **Biddle's side:** the Bank kept a uniform, sound currency; its defenders (and later many historians) held that killing it removed the one brake on reckless state-bank printing. Biddle's counterattack — contracting credit in 1833–34 to force a recharter — caused a sharp squeeze and lost him support. (Sources: Richmond Fed *Econ Focus* 2023Q2; Britannica "Nicholas Biddle.")
- **Removal of deposits, 1833:** Jackson ordered federal deposits moved to selected state banks (opponents called them "pet banks"); two Treasury secretaries balked and were replaced before Roger Taney did it; the **Senate censured Jackson March 28, 1834** (censure expunged 1837). The Bank's federal charter expired in **1836**; it continued as a Pennsylvania state bank and failed for good in 1841. Biddle died in 1844 facing lawsuits. (Sources: Britannica "Bank War"; Richmond Fed *Econ Focus*; U.S. Senate historical materials on the censure.)
- **PATCH 2026-09-27 (T-252): why Jackson distrusted paper money.** In 1795, at 28, Jackson sold 68,000 acres of land to David Allison and took Allison's promissory notes (written promises to pay) as payment. Jackson used the notes to buy supplies for a trading post. In 1797 Allison went bankrupt. Jackson was left responsible for the debts behind Allison's now-worthless paper, and it took him about 15 years to work his way out. By then, in the magazine's words, he had settled ideas about "debt, paper money, and speculation: He hated them all." Allison died in debtors' prison in Philadelphia (search summary of the same article, unconfirmed: search summary only). (Source: *American Heritage*, "Andy Jackson and the Perils of the Surplus," vol. 52 issue 3, May 2001, opened; Wikipedia "Andrew Jackson and land speculation" citing Remini 1977 p. 135 for the failed Allison partnership leaving "years of substantial debts.") Land note: the article does not say where the 68,000 acres lay or which nation's land it had been. Tennessee-region land in the 1790s was land claimed by the Cherokee and Chickasaw, but no source opened ties this tract to a nation. Do not name one.

### Money without a center — state banknotes
- From 1836 to 1863 the United States had no national currency and no federal bank. Money was gold and silver coin plus paper notes printed by state-chartered banks — each bank's notes its own design, good only as far as people trusted that bank. Notes lost value with distance from the issuing bank ("discounts"), and merchants checked printed guides before accepting them. (Sources: Federal Reserve History context essays on the free banking era; Richmond Fed *Econ Focus* "When Banking Was 'Free'," 2018Q1; Gerald Dwyer, "Wildcat Banking, Banking Panics, and Free Banking in the United States," Atlanta Fed *Economic Review* 1996.)
- **"Wildcat banks":** banks set up under loose state "free banking" laws, some in remote spots (the story went, out where the wildcats lived) to make their notes hard to redeem. Modern economic historians (Dwyer; Rolnick and Weber) find the era's reputation somewhat exaggerated — most free banks were not frauds, and noteholder losses, while real, were smaller than legend says. State the legend and the correction honestly. (Sources: Dwyer 1996 Atlanta Fed; Richmond Fed 2018Q1; Cato summary "The Fable of the Cats.")
- **Counterfeit detectors and bank note reporters:** weekly/monthly pamphlets (published roughly 1826–1866; Dillistin's standard bibliography covers those dates) listed which banks' notes traded at what discount and which fakes were circulating; shopkeepers kept them by the till. (Sources: ANS digital library, Dillistin, *Bank Note Reporters and Counterfeit Detectors, 1826–1866*; Wikipedia "Bank note reporter" citing same.)
- **Panic of 1837 — this chapter's slice is the runs:** on **May 10, 1837** New York City banks suspended specie payments — stopped paying out gold and silver for their notes and deposits — and banks across the country followed. Depositors and noteholders who lined up got paper promises or nothing. The downturn that followed (to about 1843, roughly 4 in 10 of about 850 banks failing wholly or partly) is `economy`'s telling. (Sources: `research/research-economy.md` (verified in that pass, Lumen/US-history surveys); Federal Reserve History "Panic of 1837" materials.)

### PATCH 2026-09-27 (T-252): bank money backed by mortgages on enslaved people (the money mechanism)
- **How the notes were made.** Under the Consolidated Association of the Planters of Louisiana (C.A.P.L.), enslavers mortgaged "slaves and cultivated land" to the bank and could borrow "up to half of the assessed value" in the bank's own notes. The bank's paper money was therefore lent out against appraised human beings. The state of Louisiana backed $2.5 million of C.A.P.L. bonds at 5 percent, and Baring Brothers of London advanced the money in sterling bills and sold the bonds in Europe. The Union Bank of Louisiana (1832) issued $7 million in state bonds, marketed by Baring Brothers and Prime, Ward and King of New York. The Citizens' Bank of Louisiana (chartered 1833) had $12 million in bonds that Hope and Company of Amsterdam agreed to market. A typical bond was $1,000, "roughly the price of a field hand," so a European investor could own a small share of the debt secured by thousands of enslaved people without owning any one person. (Source: Edward E. Baptist, "Toxic Debt, Liar Loans, and Securitized Human Beings: The Panic of 1837 and the Fate of Slavery," *Common-place* vol. 10 no. 3, April 2010, opened.)
- **A named loan:** in 1824 the merchant Vincent Nolte lent $48,000 to the Louisiana enslaver Alonzo Walsh, who pledged "from 90 to a 100 head of first rate slaves," worth up to $80,000 at New Orleans auction prices (Baptist, opened). The enslaved people's own names are not given in the article.
- **The crash:** cotton fell from 18 cents a pound in 1834 to under 10 cents by late 1836. By March 1837 ten major New Orleans cotton firms had failed. Planters owed New Orleans banks alone $33 million and could not expect to net more than $10 million from the 1837 crop. When planters defaulted, the people they had mortgaged were sold to raise cash (Baptist). Mississippi and Florida repudiated (refused to pay) the state-backed bonds, so bondholders in Europe and the North lost and the enslavers kept their property (Baptist). JPMorgan Chase's January 2005 report: its predecessor banks Citizens Bank and Canal Bank accepted about 13,000 enslaved people as collateral and came to own about 1,250 of them after defaults (NBC News/AP and NPR, January 2005, as sourced in `research/research-economy.md`).
- Owner note: `economy` tells these banks as the economy's capital and the panic. `slavery-freedom` leads people as property. This chapter's slice is one sentence of mechanism: the notes in people's pockets in Louisiana were bank money lent against mortgaged people.

## 7. 1850 to 1900

### Setup: the paper jungle at its peak
- By 1860, more than 10,000 different kinds of banknotes circulated in the United States (state-bank notes of every design, plus altered and counterfeit versions). By the 1860s, an estimated one-third — some estimates run toward half — of the paper money in circulation was counterfeit. (Sources: U.S. Marshals Service historical reading room "Catching Counterfeiters" (one-third estimate; 10,000+ notes figure appears in Fed and numismatic literature); U.S. Secret Service history pages (one-third); DHS 160th-birthday release ("nearly half"). State the range — estimates, not counts.)

### Greenbacks — the Civil War creates national paper money
- **Legal Tender Act, February 25, 1862:** to pay for the war, Congress authorized $150 million in United States Notes — paper money not redeemable in gold or silver, made legal tender by law; later acts raised the authorized total to $450 million. Printed with green ink on the back: "greenbacks." (Sources: U.S. Capitol Visitor Center artifact pages "HR 240, Legal Tender Act" and "Greenbacks issued March 10, 1862"; Federal Reserve History "National Banking Acts" essay for the totals.)
- Treasury Secretary **Salmon P. Chase** managed the new paper — and put his own portrait on the first $1 United States Note (1862), a fact later joked about when he sat on the Supreme Court hearing legal-tender cases. (Sources: Smithsonian NMAH currency collection records; Treasury/BEP historical materials. Keep as light verified detail.)
- **National Banking Acts of February 25, 1863 and June 3, 1864:** created federally chartered national banks issuing uniform national bank notes backed by government bonds. An 1865 law taxed state banknotes 10 percent, which ended them: state notes in circulation fell from $143 million in 1865 to $4 million by 1867. One kind of paper money, at last, everywhere. (Sources: Federal Reserve History essay "National Banking Acts of 1863 and 1864"; Flaherty, "A Brief History of Central Banking," rug.nl mirror of the same standard account.)
- **"In God We Trust":** a Pennsylvania minister, Rev. M. R. Watkinson, wrote to Chase in November 1861 urging recognition of God on the coinage; Chase directed the Mint to prepare a motto, and Congress's Coinage Act of April 22, 1864 authorized the two-cent coin, the first U.S. coin bearing IN GOD WE TRUST (1864). It reached paper money in 1957, after a 1955 law, and became the national motto in 1956. (Sources: Treasury history page "History of 'In God We Trust'"; U.S. Mint history pages. The Watkinson letter is preserved in Treasury records — documented, not legend.)
- **Secret Service, July 5, 1865:** founded as a Treasury division to suppress counterfeiting (protecting presidents was added after McKinley's assassination in 1901). First chief: William P. Wood. (Sources: secretservice.gov history timeline; DHS press release July 5, 2025; LOC/Congress.gov CRS R48138.)

### The gold-versus-silver war, explained simply
- Background for the writer: with the dollar tied to gold and the supply of gold-backed money growing slowly, prices FELL for most of 1865–1896. Falling prices meant a farmer's crop brought fewer dollars each year while his mortgage payment stayed fixed. "Free silver" — coining silver freely at 16 ounces of silver to 1 of gold — would have expanded the money supply and raised prices, easing debts. Bankers and bondholders wanted gold's stability; debtors and silver-mining states wanted silver's inflation. (Sources: Federal Reserve History and Britannica gold-standard/free-silver entries; EBSCO Research Starters "Crime of 1873.")
- **Coinage Act of February 12, 1873** ("the Crime of '73" to its enemies): dropped the standard silver dollar from the coinage list, putting the country on a de facto gold standard just as western silver production surged. (Sources: U.S. Mint "Mint History: the 'Crime of 1873'"; EBSCO "Crime of 1873"; American History Central.)
- **Bland-Allison Act, 1878** (passed over President Hayes's veto): Treasury required to buy $2–4 million of silver monthly and coin silver dollars. **Sherman Silver Purchase Act, 1890:** raised required purchases to 4.5 million ounces a month, paid in notes redeemable in gold — a drain on gold reserves; **repealed 1893** at President Cleveland's insistence during the panic. (Sources: Encyclopedia.com "Bland-Allison Act"; Wikipedia "Bland–Allison Act"/"Sherman Silver Purchase Act" with statute citations; Britannica.)
- **William Jennings Bryan, July 9, 1896:** at the Democratic convention in Chicago, the 36-year-old Nebraska congressman closed the platform debate for silver: "You shall not press down upon the brow of labor this crown of thorns; you shall not crucify mankind upon a cross of gold." The speech won him the nomination on the fifth ballot. He lost to William McKinley that fall (and again in 1900). (Sources: Britannica "Cross of Gold speech"; History Matters (GMU) speech text and Bryan's own account; Miller Center essay.)
- **PATCH 2026-09-27 (T-252): the 1896 result.** McKinley won 271 electoral votes to Bryan's 176, and 7,112,138 popular votes (51.0 percent) to Bryan's 6,510,807 (46.7 percent). Bryan's votes were concentrated "in the South, rural Midwest, and Rocky Mountain states." McKinley was strongest "in cities and in the Northeast, Upper Midwest, and Pacific Coast." The Republican platform endorsed the gold standard. (Sources: Wikipedia "1896 United States presidential election," opened, citing official returns; Miller Center, "William McKinley: Campaigns and Elections," opened, for the platforms and the roughly 600,000-vote margin.) ### Counterfeiting story — Emanuel Ninger, "Jim the Penman"
- Emanuel Ninger (1845–1924), a German-born sign painter living in New Jersey, hand-drew United States banknotes — mostly $50s and $100s — with pen, pencil, camel's-hair brush, and colored inks, tracing over a genuine note against glass, one note taking weeks. From 1883 to 1896 he passed them in Manhattan. On **March 28, 1896** he laid a bill on a saloon bar; the bar was wet, the ink smudged, and the bartender's man followed him and had him arrested. He pleaded guilty in May 1896 and was sentenced to six years. Experts and newspapers marveled that each fake had cost him more labor than honest sign-painting would have paid. (Sources: Smithsonian *Sidedoor* podcast S11E01 "Jim the Penman" (with transcript); *American Heritage*, "The Money Maker," Aug/Sept 1984; ANS "Kings of Counterfeiting." ## 8. 1900 to 1950

### Gold wins on paper — Gold Standard Act, March 14, 1900
- Made gold the sole standard behind the dollar ($20.67 an ounce), ending bimetallism in law. (Sources: Federal Reserve History gold-standard materials; Britannica "Gold Standard Act.")

### The Panic of 1907 — and one banker acting as the central bank
- October 1907: a failed attempt to corner United Copper stock wrecked the speculators' banks and spread fear to institutions linked to them. On **October 22, 1907** a run forced the **Knickerbocker Trust Company** — one of New York's largest trusts — to suspend; runs then hit the Trust Company of America and others. Depositors stood in lines around the block; some hired line-standers. (Sources: Federal Reserve History essay "The Panic of 1907"; LOC Inside Adams "United Copper Panic of 1907"; Morgan Library online exhibition guide, Stop 23.)
- **J.P. Morgan, 70, ran the rescue from his private library** at 36th and Madison: teams of examiners decided which trusts were sound, and money was pooled to hold the line — including $25 million the U.S. Treasury deposited with Morgan's syndicate and money-pool pledges Morgan extracted from bank and trust presidents. On the night of **November 2–3, 1907**, Morgan had the trust-company presidents gathered in the library and locked the doors — the key in his pocket, by the standard accounts — until they subscribed $25 million to save the weakest trusts; the deal was signed near 5 a.m. This is documented history, not legend: the Morgan Library's own exhibition tells it. (Sources: Morgan Library & Museum online guide "Stop 23. The Panic of 1907"; Federal Reserve History "The Panic of 1907"; Gotham Center for NYC History essay. Fine details — who held the key, exact hour — vary by memoir; the locked-door meeting itself is solidly documented.)
- Charles T. Barney, Knickerbocker's ousted president, shot himself November 14, 1907. (Source: Gotham Center essay; contemporary press.)
- The lesson drawn at the time: the country's money system depended on one aging private citizen. Congress created the National Monetary Commission (Aldrich-Vreeland Act, 1908) to design something better. (Sources: Federal Reserve History "Panic of 1907" and "Before the Fed" essays.)

### Jekyll Island and the Federal Reserve — documented fact, not conspiracy
- In **November 1910**, six men — Senator Nelson Aldrich; Treasury official A. Piatt Andrew; bankers Henry Davison, Frank Vanderlip, and Paul Warburg; and Aldrich's secretary Arthur Shelton — met secretly at the Jekyll Island Club off Georgia, traveling under cover as a duck-hunting party, and drafted a plan for a central banking system. Participants did not publicly admit the meeting until the 1930s. The Aldrich Plan failed in Congress, but its machinery shaped the act that passed. This is the Federal Reserve's own published history. (Source: Federal Reserve History essay "The Meeting at Jekyll Island.")
- **Federal Reserve Act, signed December 23, 1913** (Wilson): created the Federal Reserve System — twelve regional Reserve Banks under a Washington board. Explained simply for the outline: a bank for banks — it holds banks' reserves, moves money between them, and can lend to a solvent bank facing a run, so a scare need not become a collapse. The Reserve Banks opened November 16, 1914. (Sources: Federal Reserve History "Federal Reserve Act Signed into Law"; federalreserve.gov history pages.)

### The Depression runs, the holiday, and deposit insurance
- About **9,000 of the country's roughly 25,000 banks failed between 1930 and 1933** (carried from the economy pass; FDR Library "Great Depression Facts"). By March 1933 about nine million savings accounts had been wiped out (ushistory.org survey figure; use with the FDR Library number, both labeled estimates).
- **Bank of United States, December 11, 1930, New York City** — the largest U.S. bank failure to that date; some 400,000 depositors, many of them immigrant garment workers and small savers in the Bronx and Brooklyn. (Sources: Federal Reserve History "The Banking Panics of 1930-31"; Encyclopedia.com "Banking Crisis of 1933" context.)
- **February 14, 1933:** Michigan's governor declared an eight-day statewide bank holiday, freezing 900,000 depositors' money; runs spread state to state. (Source: Encyclopedia.com "Banking Crisis of 1933"; ushistory.org "A Bank Holiday.")
- **March 6, 1933:** two days after taking office, Roosevelt proclaimed a national **bank holiday** — every bank in the country closed; the Emergency Banking Act passed March 9; banks reopened March 13–15 after federal review. In the **first fireside chat, Sunday night March 12, 1933**, Roosevelt explained banking in plain words to some 60 million radio listeners and said it was safer to keep money in a reopened bank than under the mattress. When the banks opened, deposits exceeded withdrawals — the hoarded cash came back. (Sources: LOC "This Month in Business History — 1933 Bank Holiday"; Federal Reserve History "Emergency Banking Act of 1933"; FDR Library fireside-chat materials; LOC Inside Adams "First Fireside Chat.")
- **Leaving gold at home:** Executive Order 6102, **April 5, 1933**, required Americans to turn in gold coin, bullion, and gold certificates (small keepsakes and collections exempt) at $20.67 an ounce; the **Gold Reserve Act, January 30, 1934**, moved all monetary gold to the Treasury and reset gold at $35 an ounce. Americans could no longer redeem dollars for gold; foreign governments still could. (Sources: Federal Reserve History "Gold Reserve Act"; National Archives EO 6102 text; Wikipedia "Executive Order 6102" with statute citations.)
- **FDIC:** created by the Banking Act of June 16, 1933; insurance effective **January 1, 1934**, covering deposits to **$2,500** (raised to $5,000 that July). Explained simply: the government now promises that if an insured bank fails, small depositors get their money back — which removes the reason to run. (Sources: FDIC "A Brief History of Deposit Insurance"; FDIC 90th-anniversary timeline; LOC business-history guide.)
- **Ordinary story — Lydia Lobsiger:** when the Fond du Lac State Bank of East Peoria, Illinois failed in spring 1934 — the first insured bank to fail — Lobsiger received the first federal deposit insurance check, $1,250, dated July 3, 1934. Newspapers across the country carried it: for the first time, a bank had died and a small depositor had not lost her savings. (Sources: FDIC, *The First Fifty Years* and FDIC 90 Years timeline; FDIC "Brief History of Deposit Insurance." Name spelling per FDIC publications; Find-a-Grave gives Lydia Barth Schneider Lobsiger, 1870–1956.)

## 9. 1950 to 2000

### The credit card
- **Diners Club, 1950:** Frank McNamara, with Ralph Schneider (and early partners including Alfred Bloomingdale and publicist Matty Simmons), launched the first multi-merchant charge card — cardboard, accepted at first by a couple dozen New York restaurants; first used at Major's Cabin Grill, February 1950. It was a charge card: the bill was paid in full monthly. (Sources: Diners Club corporate history pages; Smithsonian NMAH object record "Diners' Club Credit Card, 1957"; Wikipedia "Diners Club International.")
- **The forgotten-wallet story is company legend, and its own PR man said so:** the oft-told tale — McNamara forgets his wallet at dinner in 1949, is embarrassed, invents the card — was, according to Matty Simmons (Diners Club's first publicity man, who was there), invented for publicity because it beat the truth, which was that McNamara simply had the idea and pursued it. Tell the story only labeled as the company's legend. (Sources: Simmons' own account, retold in payment-history journalism — a16z "The Fresno Free-for-All," payment-history newsletters; label as journalism.)
- **BankAmericard and the Fresno Drop, September 1958:** Bank of America mailed about **60,000 unsolicited, ready-to-use credit cards** to Fresno, California residents — no applications. It was the first true general-purpose bank credit card (revolving balance allowed). Delinquency hit about 22 percent against the roughly 4 percent projected, and fraud followed; the bank lost millions before the card found its footing, spread through California and then nationwide by licensing, and was spun off and renamed **Visa in 1976**. (Sources: KVPR "Central Valley Roots: The Fresno Drop"; a16z essay; 99% Invisible "The Fresno Drop"; Joe Nocera's 1994 Washington Post history — all journalism, labeled; Wikipedia "Visa Inc." for the 1976 renaming.)
- Congress banned mailing unsolicited credit cards in 1970 (Public Law 91-508 amendments to Truth in Lending). (Source: FTC/Truth in Lending histories via the same journalism; marketplace's angle — parked there.)

### Nixon closes the gold window — August 15, 1971
- Under the postwar Bretton Woods system, foreign governments could still redeem dollars for gold at $35 an ounce; by 1971 dollars abroad far exceeded U.S. gold, and a run on the gold stock was underway. After a Camp David weekend with advisers (including Fed chairman Arthur Burns, Treasury Secretary John Connally, and Paul Volcker, then a Treasury undersecretary), **Nixon announced on Sunday night August 15, 1971** that the United States would stop converting dollars to gold — announced as temporary, never reversed. Since then the dollar has been fiat money: backed by no metal, worth what it buys, resting on law and trust in the government that issues it. (Sources: Federal Reserve History essay "Nixon Ends Convertibility of US Dollars to Gold"; IMF history blog, August 2021.)

### The Great Inflation, and Volcker breaking it
- U.S. inflation climbed through the late 1960s and 1970s — the period economists call the Great Inflation (roughly 1965–1982). Consumer prices rose about 11 percent in 1974 and **13.5 percent in 1980**. President Ford's 1974 "Whip Inflation Now" campaign handed out WIN buttons; inflation did not whip. (Sources: Federal Reserve History "The Great Inflation"; BLS CPI data; Ford Library WIN materials.)
- What it felt like, in numbers: prices that had crept up 1–2 percent a year in the early 1960s were rising more than 1 percent a MONTH at the 1979–80 peak; food inflation topped 20 percent in late 1973; the average 30-year mortgage rate reached **18.63 percent in October 1981** (Freddie Mac survey — the highest ever recorded). A dollar saved in 1965 bought about a third as much by 1982 (BLS CPI arithmetic). (Sources: BLS; Freddie Mac rate series via Marketplace/Bankrate summaries; USDA/BLS food-price histories.)
- **Paul Volcker**, appointed Fed chairman by President Carter in August 1979, announced on **Saturday night, October 6, 1979** a new policy: the Fed would control the supply of money and let interest rates go where they must. The federal funds rate passed 20 percent in 1981; the 1981–82 recession pushed unemployment to **10.8 percent** (highest since the 1930s); farmers on tractors and indebted builders mailing sawed-off two-by-fours protested at the Fed. Inflation fell from 13.5 percent (1980) to **3.2 percent (1983)** and stayed low for a generation. Volcker, 6-foot-7, smoking cheap cigars, became the face of the cure and its cost. (Sources: Federal Reserve History essays "Anti-Inflation Measures" (the October 6, 1979 announcement) and "The Great Inflation"; San Francisco Fed Economic Letter "October 6, 1979"; St. Louis Fed *Review* 2021 "Paul Volcker and the 1979-82 War on Inflation." The two-by-fours and tractorcade protests are documented in Fed histories and contemporary press.)

### PATCH 2026-09-27 (T-252): savers against inflation, Regulation Q, and the first money market fund (fills the `1970s-inflation-family` slot)

- **Regulation Q:** the Banking Act of 1933 required the Federal Reserve Board to set maximum interest rates banks could pay on savings and time deposits. Regulation Q was first issued in **September 1933**. The 1980 Depository Institutions Deregulation and Monetary Control Act phased the caps out by **1986**. Dodd-Frank repealed the ban on interest on checking accounts in **2011**. When market rates climbed in the 1970s, savers moved money to unregulated investments, and "money market mutual funds grew quickly in the 1970s, attracting investors by paying a market rate of interest." (Source: Federal Reserve History, "Interest Rate Controls (Regulation Q)," opened.)
- **The ceiling in numbers:** in **July 1973** the Federal Reserve raised the commercial-bank ceiling on passbook savings from **4.5 percent to 5 percent**. Thrift ceilings sat 0.25 to 0.5 points higher. Most banks and savings and loans paid exactly the ceiling rate from 1973 to 1978. (Source: Richmond Fed *Economic Review*, Nov/Dec 1978, "Regulation Q and the Behavior of Savings and Small Time Deposits," opened as PDF.) Set against consumer prices rising about 11 percent in 1974 (bank, era 9 above), a passbook account at 5 percent lost about 6 percent of its buying power that year. (Arithmetic from the two sourced figures.)
- **Bruce R. Bent** (born May 25, 1937, Great Neck, New York; Marine Corps veteran; economics degree, St. John's University, 1961) and **Henry B. R. Brown** (February 13, 1926 to August 11, 2008) met while working at the TIAA-CREF pension firm. They set up the **Reserve Fund** in 1970. Minimum investment **$1,000**, shares $100 each. With no sales force they phoned advisers and handed out brochures. After a *New York Times* article on **January 7, 1973** (Robert D. Hershey Jr.), the fund held **$100 million** by the end of 1973. (Sources: Wikipedia "Henry B. R. Brown" and "Bruce R. ### PATCH 2026-09-27 (T-252): the savings and loan crisis, 1980s to 1995
- **What happened:** savings and loans (S&Ls, also called thrifts) took in savings deposits and made long-term, fixed-rate home loans. When inflation and interest rates rose in the late 1970s and early 1980s, Regulation Q ceilings kept them from paying savers market rates, and the old fixed-rate mortgages they held lost value. By 1983 closing the insolvent thrifts would have cost about **$25 billion**, but their insurance fund, the FSLIC, had reserves of only **$6 billion**. The **Garn-St Germain Act of 1982** let thrifts make new and riskier loans beyond home mortgages. (Source: Kenneth J. Robinson, "Savings and Loan Crisis," Federal Reserve History, opened.)
- **The cleanup:** the **Financial Institutions Reform, Recovery, and Enforcement Act of 1989 (FIRREA)** abolished the Federal Home Loan Bank Board and the FSLIC, moved thrift deposit insurance to the FDIC, and created the **Resolution Trust Corporation (RTC)**. The RTC closed **747 S&Ls with assets of over $407 billion** before it shut down on **December 31, 1995**. The cost to taxpayers was estimated at **as much as $124 billion**. (Federal Reserve History, opened.) A wider FDIC count: **1,043** of the country's 3,234 thrifts closed or were otherwise resolved from 1986 to 1995 (Curry and Shibut, *FDIC Banking Review*, 2000) (unconfirmed: search summary only). Record both: 747 closed by the RTC (Fed History) and 1,043 resolved in all (FDIC).
- **Depositors:** insured depositors were paid in full because the government stood behind the insurance fund. The loss fell on taxpayers. (Mechanism from Fed History: taxpayers bore up to $124 billion.)
- **Banks too:** FDIC-insured bank failures ran 120 in 1985, 145 in 1986, 203 in 1987, 280 in 1988, 207 in 1989, 169 in 1990, 127 in 1991 and 122 in 1992 (FDIC, *A Brief History of Deposit Insurance*, 1998, fund-failures table, opened). In 1984 federal regulators rescued Continental Illinois National Bank ($45 billion in assets) rather than let it fail, one of two uses of the FDIC's "essentiality" power (FDIC, same, opened). This is the origin of the phrase "too big to fail," a phrase attributed to Congressman Stewart McKinney in 1984 hearings (unconfirmed: search summary only; leave it out unless confirmed).

### PATCH 2026-09-27 (T-252): stablecoins and the GENIUS Act, 2025
- On **July 18, 2025** President Trump signed the **GENIUS Act** (Guiding and Establishing National Innovation for U.S. Stablecoins Act, S. 1582, Public Law 119-27). A payment stablecoin is "a digital asset issued for payment or settlement and redeemable at a predetermined fixed amount (e.g., $1)." Issuers must hold at least one dollar of approved reserves (currency, insured bank deposits, Treasury bills, repurchase agreements, government money market funds) for every dollar of stablecoins. Banks, credit-union subsidiaries and approved nonbanks may issue them. The act states that payment stablecoins are **not federally insured** and are not securities or commodities. (Source: Congressional Research Service Insight IN12553, "Stablecoin Legislation: An Overview of S. 1582, GENIUS Act of 2025," via EveryCRSReport, opened.) The law takes effect 18 months after enactment or 120 days after final rules, whichever comes first (unconfirmed: search summary only).
- Link for the writer: a stablecoin is a private promise to pay a dollar, backed by reserves. A 19th-century banknote was a private promise backed by coin in the vault. Neither carries federal insurance.

### The penny
- A penny cost **3.69 cents** to make and distribute in fiscal 2024 (up from 3.07 cents the year before) — the Mint lost money on every cent, about $85 million that year on pennies.
