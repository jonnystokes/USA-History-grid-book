# Research Bank: Chapter 32, Art (`art`)

Created 2026-09-28 by T-267a (eras 1-5). Eras 6-10 are T-267b's. Every fact carries an inline source. A fact seen only in a search-result summary is tagged "(unconfirmed: search summary only)".

## Era 01: Before 1500

### Pottery: Ancestral Pueblo black-on-white, and the Mimbres bowls
- NPS Teachers, "Pottery of the Ancestral Pueblo" (https://home.nps.gov/teachers/classrooms/pottery-of-the-ancestral-pueblo.htm): "Ancestral Pueblo pottery is called Black-on-White. The white is from the color of the clay." Made "by a method called coiling and scraping. A clay 'snake' is coiled around in a spiral, forming the base ... Then the coils are scraped together." Black paint "made from boiled plants (like beeweed or tansy mustard) or from crushed rock with iron in it (such as hematite)." "Paint brushes were made from the fibers of the yucca plant." Mesa Verde date range on the page: about 600 to 1300 A.D. The page does not say who (men or women) made the pots.
- Mesa Verde Black-on-white: earliest assemblages dominated by the type date to about A.D. 1180, the last to the late 1200s when people left the Mesa Verde region (Southwest Ceramic Typology, Office of Archaeological Studies NM, https://ceramics.nmarchaeology.org/typology/type?p=193) (unconfirmed: search summary only).
- Mimbres: Donna Yates, "Mimbres Pottery," Trafficking Culture encyclopedia, 2012 (https://traffickingculture.org/encyclopedia/case-studies/mimbres-pottery/): "Classic Mimbres" phase about 1000 to 1150 AD, Mimbres valley, southwestern New Mexico. The painted bowls "are usually found in human burials and appear to be used to cover the face or head of the deceased," with "a distinctive 'kill hole', an intentional puncture at the base." Other sources give 1000 to 1130 (Univ. of Houston Pressbooks, "Some Thoughts about Mimbres Pottery and Mortuary Customs"; unconfirmed: search summary only). Record both end dates.

### Mississippian engraved shell, copper, and stone figures
- Spiro Mounds, eastern Oklahoma: Dennis A. Peterson, "Spiro Mounds," Encyclopedia of Oklahoma History and Culture, Oklahoma Historical Society (https://www.okhistory.org/publications/enc/entry.php?entry=SP012): "Between A.D. 850 and 1450 twelve mounds, ceremonial areas, and a support city were eventually created."
- Etowah copper plates (Etowah Mounds, near Cartersville, Georgia): hammered sheet copper worked in raised relief (repousse), about 1250 to 1325 CE, Birdman figures of the Southeastern Ceremonial Complex. The two best-known "Rogan plates" were found by John P. Rogan in 1885 in a stone-box grave in Mound C, digging for the Smithsonian's Bureau of Ethnology, and are held by the Smithsonian National Museum of Natural History, Department of Anthropology, catalogue A91117 and A91113 (Wikipedia "Etowah plates," citing Townsend and Sharp 2004 and others, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etowah_plates. Smithsonian object records https://www.si.edu/object/nmnhanthropology_8319020 seen in search only).
- Flint-clay figures from the Cahokia area: the Birger figurine, found in 1979 at the BBB Motor site near Cahokia, shows a kneeling woman with a pack on her back, using a hoe on the back of a serpent with a cat's head. Carved from flint clay from near St. Louis, Missouri, in the Stirling phase, 1050 to 1200 CE. The Keller figurine from the same site shows a kneeling woman in a wrap skirt, 14 cm tall (Wikipedia "Mississippian stone statuary," citing Thomas E. Emerson, *Cahokia and the Archaeology of Power*, 1997, pp. 195-237, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mississippian_stone_statuary). Search summary dates it 1100-1200 (unconfirmed: search summary only).

### Hopewell stone pipes, Ohio valley
- Bill Pickard, "Some Thoughts on Tremper Pipes," Ohio History Connection archaeology blog, March 25, 2008 (https://www.ohiohistory.org/tremper-pipes/): Hopewell culture "between about 100 B.C. and A.D. 400 or 500." Tremper Mound (Scioto County, Ohio) held "136 smoking pipes included in a great cache of over 500 objects," dug in 1915 by William C. Mills of the Ohio Archaeological and Historical Society. The pipes show birds of prey, waterfowl, songbirds, frogs and toads, meat-eating animals and river otters. Most were thought to be Ohio pipestone. Analysis found 16 percent of those sampled were catlinite from the pipestone quarries of southwest Minnesota.
- Mound City (near Chillicothe, Ohio): more than 200 pipes found by Ephraim Squier and Edwin Davis in 1846, broken and placed together in a deposit under the floor of a ceremonial building (Ohio History Connection, "A History of the World in 100 Objects features Hopewell effigy pipe," https://www.ohiohistory.org/a-history-of-the-world-in-100-objects-features-hopewell-effigy-pipe/ : "one of more than 200 such pipes discovered by Ephraim Squier and Edwin Davis in 1846"; "broken and placed together in a ceremonial deposit beneath the floor of a structure used for ceremonies." Page opened 2026-09-28). NPS Hopewell Culture NHP Administrative History, ch. 6 (https://npshistory.com/publications/hocu/adhi/chap6.htm): Squier and Davis dug the Mound City Group in 1846. "In 1864, Davis sold the antiquities for $10,000 to William Blackmore, founder of the Blackmore Museum in Salisbury, England." "In 1931, the Mound City Group or 'Davis' collection changed hands again, sold to the British Museum." Fewer than two dozen of the effigy pipes were on public display there.

### Northwest Coast carving
- Burke Museum, "Historical Coast Salish Art" (https://www.burkemuseum.org/collections-and-research/heritage/artscultures/coast-salish-art/historical-coast-salish-art): "Works of art in the form of carved bone, stone, horn and basketry have been found and dated back to 5,000 years ago." "The oldest Coast Salish basketry was discovered at an archaeological site of a former fishing camp along the Olympic Peninsula's Hoko River and is approximately 2,500 years old." The oldest surviving hat, twined cedar bark, from the mouth of Wapato Creek, dates to 1450-1620 CE. The Sucia Island figure is carved elk antler with shell inlay (date as printed on the page: "1200-200 CE". Do not use that date without checking it. It may be a typo).
- Wood rots. Most carved cedar work (house posts, bentwood boxes, masks) from before 1500 has not survived. An estimated 85 percent of objects on the ancient Northwest Coast were made of wood or plant fibre (Archaeological Conservancy review of *Waterlogged*; unconfirmed: search summary only). So the outline claims only the antler, bone, stone and basketry record for before 1500.

### Rock art
- NPS, "New Mexico: Petroglyph National Monument" (https://www.nps.gov/articles/petroglyph.htm): "some 24,000 carvings of animals, people, brands, crosses, and other images" on the volcanic cliffs west of Albuquerque. "The earliest petroglyphs are considerably older, dating from 2000 BC" and most were made "between 1300 and the 1680s," by ancestral Pueblo people, "chipped off by chisel and other stones." "The petroglyphs also have contemporary religious and ceremonial meaning for some tribes." Another count: "estimated 25,000" (New Mexico Bureau of Geology tour page; unconfirmed: search summary only). State both: about 24,000 (NPS) to 25,000.
- A petroglyph is cut or pecked into rock. A pictograph is painted on it (standard usage; define in prose).

### How the objects left the nations: named diggers, sellers and buyers
- Spiro: Peterson, Encyclopedia of Oklahoma History (as above): "From 1933 until 1935 Pocola employees dug haphazardly into the burial mound. During two years the commercial diggers destroyed about one-third of the mound and sold thousands of artifacts, made of stone, copper, shell, basketry, and fabric, to collectors throughout the world." The destruction led "the Oklahoma Legislature to pass a licensing requirement ... and in November 1935 the Pocola Mining Company was finally shut down." Then "From June 1936 until October 1941 OU archaeologists oversaw Works Progress Administration (WPA) workers" at the site, and "From the Craig Mound more than six hundred complete or partial burials were recovered." The Pocola Mining Company was a group of men who leased the Craig Mound in 1933. The encyclopedia entry does not name them. Search summaries say the Spiro objects are now spread among more than 65 museums, and that about 90 percent of known Mississippian engraved shell came from this one mound (Spiro exhibition site https://www.spiromounds.com/collection/archaeology-looting; unconfirmed: search summary only).
- Mound City pipes: Davis sold them to Blackmore in England, 1864, $10,000. The British Museum bought them in 1931 (NPS admin history, above).
- Etowah: Rogan, for the Smithsonian, 1885 (above).
- Mimbres: Yates (Trafficking Culture, above): "Not a single Mimbres cultural site escaped being looted." In the 1960s "looters began to use heavy machinery and earth movers to reach painted bowls more quickly. Because of this many Mimbres sites have been completely obliterated." The bowls were grave goods, so the looters were digging up graves (bowls "usually found in human burials"). J. Walter Fewkes of the Smithsonian published the bowls in the 1910s.
- The law after: NAGPRA, November 16, 1990, requires museums and agencies that take federal money to inventory Native human remains and sacred objects and return them (NPS NAGPRA, https://www.nps.gov/subjects/nagpra/, as banked in `research-native-nations.md` and `research-science.md`). The Smithsonian returns under the National Museum of the American Indian Act of November 28, 1989 (`research-science.md`, ProPublica). The Archaeological Resources Protection Act, 1979, protects sites on public land (Yates). NAGPRA details and the 2024 rule belong to later eras, and `native-nations` leads.

### Story: Nathan Jackson (Tlingit carver), a living artist in the Northwest Coast tradition
- NEA, "Nathan Jackson," 1995 National Heritage Fellow (https://www.arts.gov/honors/heritage/nathan-jackson): born August 29, 1938, in southeastern Alaska. Sockeye Clan, Raven side, Chilkoot Tlingit. Carver of totem poles, clan crest panels, masks, canoes and carved doors, also a metalsmith and dancer. Learned the tribe's traditions from his clan uncle and grandfather, Jack David. After Army service in Germany (ended 1959) he began carving miniature totem poles while recovering from pneumonia. Studied at the Institute of American Indian Arts in Santa Fe, then under the carver Duane Pasco to learn adze work. Taught apprentices through the Alaska State Council on the Arts master and apprentice program. His words: "Tradition is something that most people say is identity ... the traditional way of life is something I identify with."
- Birthplace Tenakee Springs, Alaska (search summary of Wikipedia and Rasmuson Foundation; unconfirmed: search summary only).
- Maria Dudzak, "From fisherman to carver, Nathan Jackson shares his story," KRBD Ketchikan, Dec 14, 2017 (https://www.krbd.org/2017/12/14/fisherman-carver-jackson-shares-story/): a 54-day hospital stay with pneumonia, whittling small totems. He said: "I told the nurses, 'Those totem poles there, they have a price on them.'" Studied about three years in Santa Fe after joining the Chilkat Dancers of Haines in the early 1960s. KRBD puts the pneumonia in his mid-30s. The NEA puts it just after 1959 (he would have been about 21). The sources disagree. Do not state his age at the time.
- Jersey Arts, Sept 5, 2024 (https://www.jerseyarts.com/stories/renowned-native-american-pole-carver-complete-totem-pole-princeton): at 86, Jackson carved an eight-foot pole showing the Tlingit story of Raven and Fog Woman. He began it at the Edwin DeWill Carving Center in Saxman, Alaska, and finished it at the Arts Council of Princeton, New Jersey, in autumn 2024 (October, per search summary), where it stands in the lobby. Lives in Ketchikan.
- Movie: a film titled "Nathan Jackson" is listed by the American Indian Film Gallery, University of Arizona (https://aifg.arizona.edu/film/nathan-jackson). Not opened. Year and content unknown, so it is left off the story block.
- Not told in any other outline (grep, 2026-09-28).

## Era 02: The 1500s

### Jacques Le Moyne de Morgues, French Florida, 1564-1565 (the earliest, and lost)
- Dr. Judy Bense, "The early French and Jaques le Moyne," WUWF Unearthing Florida, March 28, 2024 (https://www.wuwf.org/unearthing-florida/2024-03-28/the-early-french-and-jaques-le-moyne): "the French brought along an official artist, Jaques le Moyne, to record their secret colony in Spanish Florida." "Le Moyne made many sketches of Fort Caroline, the settlers, the landscape, and the Natives they encountered." "when the Spanish attacked the fort in 1565, Le Moyne escaped, but he lost all his drawings." "Back in France Le Moyne redrew his illustrations from memory. After he died in 1588, his wife sold the illustrations to an engraver, Theodre de Bry," "who published them three years later, in 1591." "De Bry made many editorial changes to Le Moyne's drawings to make his books more marketable. He altered the people and landscapes, and made different versions for different audiences."
- Museum of Florida History, "The French in Florida" (https://museumoffloridahistory.com/explore/exhibits/permanent-exhibits/la-florida/forever-changed/meeting-of-the-cultures/the-french-in-florida/): the painting "Laudonnière and Athore, 1564" by Le Moyne, from the New York Public Library's collections, shows the French commander René de Laudonnière with the Timucua leader Athore. De Bry's *Grand Voyages* (1591) engravings were "after watercolors by Jacques Le Moyne de Morgues."
- Only one original Le Moyne picture of an American subject is known today, the Athore painting (Wikipedia "Jacques Le Moyne" and Jacksonville Public Library; unconfirmed: search summary only). Athore was a Timucua leader (Museum of Florida History). The 1565 Spanish attack itself is `exploration`/`war` material, not repeated here.
- Consequence for the outline: the seed's claim that White's watercolors are "the earliest detailed European pictures of people and places in what is now the United States" is too strong. Le Moyne drew Florida in 1564, 21 years before White, but his drawings from Florida were lost in 1565 and what survives was redrawn from memory. White's are the earliest surviving pictures made on the spot, and the first English ones. Smithsonian: "No Englishman had ever painted America before" (below).

### John White's watercolors, 1585
- NCpedia, "White, John" (https://www.ncpedia.org/biography/white-john): active 1577 to 1593 (birth and death years unknown), probably born in Cornwall, may have died in Ireland about 1593. Sailed on Sir Richard Grenville's 1585 voyage as artist and observer beside the mathematician Thomas Harriot, recording villages, people, plants and animals. His method: "carefully finished water-colour drawings," outlines in black lead filled with watercolor and body color, touches of gold and silver. Subjects: Indian villages, fishing, ceremonial dances, clothing, birds and fish. De Bry published engravings of the Indian drawings in *America*, Part I (1590), with Harriot's text. White was named governor of the 1587 colony and returned to Roanoke in August 1590 to find the colonists gone and "Croatoan" carved on a tree (NCpedia).
- Virtual Jamestown, "John White Drawings/Theodor De Bry engravings" (https://www.virtualjamestown.org/images/white_debry_html/introduction.html), annotations from Paul Hulton and David Beers Quinn, *The American Drawings of John White 1577-1590* (UNC Press): White made "a series of over seventy watercolor drawings of indigenous people, plants, and animals" during about thirteen months at Roanoke Island from 1585. "In his engravings, De Bry took certain liberties with White's images."
- Abigail Tucker, "Sketching the Earliest Views of the New World," *Smithsonian* magazine, December 2008 (https://www.smithsonianmag.com/travel/sketching-the-earliest-views-of-the-new-world-92306407/): the originals are "Owned by the British Museum" and kept in storage away from light. "In the mid-19th century they sustained heavy water damage in a Sotheby's auction house fire. Chemical changes in the silver pigments have turned them black." "No Englishman had ever painted America before." They are "the only surviving visual record of the land and peoples encountered by England's first settlers in America."
- Count: "over seventy" (Hulton and Quinn via Virtual Jamestown). "More than seventy" (Wikipedia). "75 watercolours now survive in the British Museum" (search summary; unconfirmed: search summary only). Bought in 1866 (search summary; unconfirmed: search summary only). The outline says "more than seventy."
- What de Bry changed: Benjamin Breen, "Painting the New World," Public Domain Review, April 24, 2012 (https://publicdomainreview.org/essay/painting-the-new-world/): in White's watercolor of a Secotan woman and her daughter, the girl holds "a tiny female doll wearing Elizabethan dress." "De Bry's depiction shows the Indian girl holding not only 'an English doll in Elizabethan clothing,' but 'an armillary sphere,'" a model of the globe and heavens (quoting Joyce Chaplin, p. 36). Bense (WUWF, above) on de Bry's general practice with Le Moyne: "He altered the people and landscapes." Search summaries say de Bry gave the people European faces and poses and added backgrounds (unconfirmed: search summary only). The outline states only the doll and sphere, which the page states.
- Who the pictures were for: White and Harriot were on Raleigh's expedition, paid for by Raleigh and investors. Harriot's *Briefe and True Report* (1588) was written to answer men who had spoken against the colony (see `research-news-communication.md`, `research-science.md`, which lead on Harriot). The seed line "made for English readers and investors deciding whether to pay for a colony" is supported only for the published 1590 book. The outline now says the engravings were printed in Harriot's book for European readers.
- White led an attack on the town of Dasamonquepeuc on August 8, 1587 (`outlines/disasters.md` line 60, which carries it). Not repeated here. `exploration` leads on Roanoke.

## Era 03: The 1600s

### Portraits: the Freake painter
- Wikipedia, "Freake Painter" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freake_Painter), citing the Worcester Art Museum and *The Magazine Antiques*, "Freake Out!": the painter is anonymous, active in the 1670s, also called the Freake Limner and the Freake-Gibbs Painter. "About ten portraits, all painted between 1670 and 1674 and showing residents of Boston, have been attributed to the Freake Painter." "It has been suggested that the artist might be identified as Samuel Clement (1635-78)" (a suggestion, not settled). "The Freakes were one of the wealthiest families in Boston." The picture was on a US postage stamp in 1998.
- Worcester Art Museum object record, "Elizabeth Clarke Freake (Mrs. John Freake) and Baby Mary," Freake-Gibbs Painter, 1671-1674, oil on canvas (https://worcester.emuseum.com/objects/15071/elizabeth-clarke-freake-mrs-john-freake-and-baby-mary; page blocked to curl and WebFetch, facts from the search summary; unconfirmed: search summary only): Elizabeth Freake (1642-1712/13) was married to the merchant and attorney John Freake (1631-1675). Baby Mary (born 1674) was added about 1674, when the painter also repainted the clothes. The museum's current title is "Elizabeth Clarke Freake (Mrs. John Freake) and Baby Mary."
- "Limner": a painter of portraits. Use only with a definition.

### Gravestone carvers: winged skulls in slate, and one named carver
- Woburn Historical Society, "Gravestone Iconology" (https://www.woburnhistoricalsociety.com/blank-10): Puritans "did not advocate using religious symbols, such as crosses and cherubs, but used motifs such as winged skulls (death's heads), crossed bones, hourglasses, coffins and skeletons." Latin phrases "Memento Mori" (remember you will die) and "Fugit Hora" (time flies). "One of the finest stone carvers in early New England was Joseph Lamson (1658-1722) of Charlestown, who was known for his expert carving and detailed stones of winged skulls, finials and borders of ornate fruits, vines and flowers." His skulls have "eyebrows that have hooked ends." The First Burial Ground in Woburn has several of his stones.
- Friends of Ancient Cemetery, Yarmouth MA, "Carvers" (https://www.friendsofancientcemetery.org/carvers): Joseph Lamson I (1658-1722) was "the progenitor of this multi-generational carving dynasty": his sons Nathaniel (1692/3-1755) and Caleb (1697-1760), and grandson Joseph Lamson II (1728-1789). "The Lamsons were one of the earliest and most prolific New England gravestone providers."
- Lamson born at Ipswich, Massachusetts, in 1658. His shop began about 1677 in Malden and Charlestown. He used slate (search summary of WikiTree and Cemetery Symbolism blog; unconfirmed: search summary only). The outline gives only the Woburn and Yarmouth facts.
- Soul effigies (winged faces) replaced skulls on many stones in the 1700s (New England Historical Society, "Winged Skulls and Poetic Epitaphs," https://newenglandhistoricalsociety.com/winged-skulls-and-poetic-epitaphs-the-art-and-soul-of-new-englands-gravestone-carvers/ : carvers made "skulls, winged angels, soul effigies ... urns and willows"). The seed put soul effigies in the 1600s. That timing is not sourced here. The outline keeps the 1600s to winged skulls.

### The Loara Standish sampler (a named girl's needlework)
- Pilgrim Hall Museum, "Treasures of the Collection" (https://www.pilgrimhall.org/treasures-of-the-collection/): "Sampler, Linen with Silk Embroidery Made in Duxbury, MA, c. 1653 PHM 108, Gift of Lucius Alden 1844." "Loara Standish, daughter of Captain Myles Standish, is thought to have made this sampler in her teens. It is the earliest known American-made sampler and the earliest known sampler with a verse." "Samplers of the 16th to mid 18th centuries served as permanent records of stitches and designs, intended for reference." "instruction in needlework passed from mother to daughter." Motifs: rose, carnation, oak leaf, an intertwined "S." Verse: "Loara Standish is my name Lorde guide my hart that I may doe thy will also My hands with such Convenient skill as may Conduce to virtue void of Shame and I will give The glory to thy name." It "is faded from many generations of display."
- Textile Research Centre, Leiden, "Loara Standish Sampler" (https://trc-leiden.nl/trc-needles/individual-textiles-and-textile-types/samplers/loara-standish-sampler): "the oldest known extant sampler in the USA, and was worked probably in the 1640's." "Loara Standish (born after 1627 and died before 1655) lived in the Plymouth Colony," one of seven children of Myles and Barbara Standish.
- Dates disagree: about 1653 (Pilgrim Hall) or probably the 1640s (TRC Leiden). State both.
- Duxbury and Plymouth were Wampanoag land (Patuxet was the Wampanoag town at Plymouth: Pilgrim Hall site menu lists "Patuxet," "Wampanoag World: Patuxet to Plymouth"). `native-nations` and `migration` lead on the land.
- Movie: none found.

### The first books printed and written here
- Library of Congress, "First Among Many: The Bay Psalm Book and Early Moments in American Printing," online exhibition (https://www.loc.gov/exhibits/bay-psalm-book-and-american-printing/online-exhibition.html): "A committee of thirty learned elders, including John Cotton, Richard Mather, and John Eliot, set about the task of creating a new translation of the 150 Hebrew psalms into English and casting the text into verse for singing." The Rev. Jose Glover sailed in 1638 with a press, and "Glover died aboard ship, and it was left to his widow Elizabeth to establish the press." She moved it to Cambridge, Massachusetts, under Stephen Daye (1594?-1668), "a locksmith by trade." "It is estimated that 1,700 copies of the book were printed, which sold for twenty pence. Today, only eleven copies survive." Imprint: "Cambridge, Massachusetts: Stephen Daye, 1640." "It was, nevertheless, the first full venture of printing in British North America."
- "First book printed in British North America" is the LOC catalogue's description (search summary of https://www.loc.gov/item/71002405/ ; page 403). The exhibition text supports "the first full venture of printing in British North America." Earlier small items (a broadside oath, an almanac, 1639) were printed on the same press (standard account; not opened here). The outline says "the first book printed in the English colonies," which the LOC record supports.
- Anne Bradstreet: New-York Historical Society, "Rare Books Revealed: Anne Bradstreet, American Muse" (https://www.nyhistory.org/blogs/rare-books-revealed-anne-bradstreet-american-muse): "Anne Bradstreet (1612-1672) was born in England to Thomas and Dorothy Dudley. She married Simon Bradstreet around 1628 and the couple, along with Bradstreet's parents, emigrated to the colonies in 1630." "The Bradstreets had eight children." *The Tenth Muse* printed "at London for Stephen Bowtell ... 1650." "On the title page of The Tenth Muse the author is called 'a Gentlewoman of those parts,' but Bradstreet's name appears in full on leaf A7." "It has usually been supposed that Bradstreet didn't know that her brother-in-law, Reverend John Woodbridge, was taking the manuscript of The Tenth Muse to London for publication, but later scholarship by Bradstreet biographer Charlotte Gordon disproves this assumption." Gordon's reading of the poems and "Bradstreet and Woodbridge's correspondence" shows Bradstreet "was not only aware of Woodbridge's plans ... but was involved in the plan from the start."
- Charlotte Gordon, "Humble Assertions: The True Story of Anne Bradstreet's Publication of The Tenth Muse," *Commonplace* (https://commonplace.online/article/humble-assertions-the-true-story-of-anne-bradstreets-publication-of-the-tenth-muse/): in her later poem "The Author to Her Book," Bradstreet calls Woodbridge a "friend more loving than true" and implies the book was published behind her back. Woodbridge's preface says the same. Gordon's evidence: "David's Lamentation," a poem on events of 1649, is in the book, so someone had to send it after Woodbridge sailed in 1648. The title page image caption calls it "the first book of New World poetry, published in England."
- "The Author to Her Book" was written in 1666 when a second edition was planned (Wikipedia via search; unconfirmed: search summary only). The second edition, *Several Poems*, came out in Boston in 1678, after her death (standard; not opened here). Not in outline.
- "First published poet" claims: "the first book of New World poetry" (Commonplace caption). The outline says the first book of poems by someone living in the English colonies.

### Mission churches in New Mexico, built by Pueblo workers
- NPS, "Acoma Pueblo: Ancient City in the Sky" (https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/acoma-pueblo-ancient-city-in-the-sky.htm): San Esteban del Rey is "the oldest surviving European church in New Mexico." In December 1598 Acoma people killed Captain Juan de Zaldívar and fourteen of his men. After the Spanish took the pueblo, "Oñate himself demanded sentencing survivors to indentured servitude and bodily mutilation." (`native-nations` leads on this, 1599.) Fray Juan Ramírez "likely ... began the building of San Esteban del Rey Mission Church upon his arrival in 1629, but the exact dates of its construction are unknown." "native workers had to carry all its materials up the mountain. These materials included the church's 40-foot long roof beams, originally hewn in the San Mateo Mountains, 30 miles away."
- Wikipedia, "San Estévan del Rey Mission Church" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Est%C3%A9van_del_Rey_Mission_Church): built 1629-1641. "In 1629 Father Juan Ramírez began construction of the mission, using enslaved Acoma and craftsmen." Beams came "some 40 miles (64 km) from Mount Taylor."
- Sources disagree: San Mateo Mountains, 30 miles (NPS) or Mount Taylor, about 40 miles (Wikipedia), or 50 miles (Atlas Obscura, search summary only). Mount Taylor is in the San Mateo range (not checked here). The outline gives "30 to 40 miles" and names the NPS figure.
- Whether the Acoma workers were enslaved, drafted or paid: NPS says only that they "had to carry." Wikipedia says "enslaved." The labour system in New Mexico was the encomienda (tribute and forced labour owed to Spanish holders) and the repartimiento (rotating drafts of Native workers, nominally paid), and New Mexico governors also used illegal slave labour (Britannica, as banked in `research-work-workers.md` lines 17-18). ## Era 04: 1700 to 1750

### Henrietta Johnston, Charleston, from 1708
- New-York Historical Society, Women and the American Story, "Professional Portraitist" (https://wams.nyhistory.org/primary-source/professional-portraitist/): "the first professional woman portraitist in the English colonies." Born in northern France about 1674. "In 1687 her family fled to London to escape religious persecution" (her family were French Protestants, Huguenots). "In 1694 she married a son of a baronet and moved to Ireland. While in Ireland, Henrietta learned to make pastel portraits." Her first husband died in 1704, leaving her with two young children. Her second husband, Gideon Johnston, an Anglican minister, moved the family to Charleston in 1708 as the bishop of London's representative. "The Johnston family continued to have money troubles in their new home, so Henrietta started to work as a professional portraitist for the city's elite." "She was the first recorded professional woman artist in the colonies, and the first colonial artist to use pastels." "Getting the supplies she needed was difficult, so Henrietta made a trip back to London in 1711. On her return journey, she survived an attack by pirates." "Her precious pastels could only be purchased in England." "When Gideon died in 1716, Henrietta became the sole provider for her family." "She died in Charleston on March 9, 1729." "Today about three dozen of her portraits survive." Glossary: "pastel A crayon made of a paste of powdered pigments and resin." Example on the page: Pierre Bacot, about 1708-1710.
- Size about 9 x 12 inches. Held by the Metropolitan Museum, the Gibbes Museum of Art, MESDA and the Greenville County Museum of Art (Wikipedia via search; unconfirmed: search summary only). Not in outline.
- The seed's "first woman working as a professional painter in the colonies" claim: NYHS says "first recorded professional woman artist in the colonies." The outline uses "the first woman recorded as working for pay as an artist in the English colonies."

### John Smibert, Boston, from 1729
- Dates: Wikipedia (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Smibert): born 24 March 1688, Edinburgh, died 2 April 1751, Boston. Britannica (https://www.britannica.com/biography/John-Smibert): born April 2, 1688, died March 2, 1751. The two sources swap the day and month (likely Old Style and New Style dating; not checked). Sitters included the merchant Peter Faneuil and Judge Samuel Sewall. Married Mary Williams in 1730. Designed Faneuil Hall in 1742 (Encyclopedia.com, Britannica).

## Era 05: 1750 to 1800

### Phillis Wheatley's *Poems*, 1773 (primary text)
- The book itself, as transcribed in the University of Virginia anthology edition, "Poems on Various Subjects, Religious and Moral" (https://anthologydev.lib.virginia.edu/work/Wheatley/wheatley-poems ; opened with curl 2026-09-28):
  - Title page: "POEMS ON VARIOUS SUBJECTS, RELIGIOUS AND MORAL. BY PHILLIS WHEATLEY, NEGRO SERVANT to Mr. JOHN WHEATLEY, of BOSTON, in NEW ENGLAND. LONDON: Printed for A. BELL, Bookseller, Aldgate; and sold by Messrs. COX and BERRY, King-Street, BOSTON. M DCC LXXIII." Dedicated to the Countess of Huntingdon.
  - John Wheatley's letter to the publisher: "PHILLIS was brought from Africa to America, in the Year 1761, between Seven and Eight Years of Age. Without any Assistance from School Education, and by only what she was taught in the Family, she, in sixteen Months Time from her Arrival, attained the English Language ... as to read any, the most difficult Parts of the Sacred Writings." Signed: "This Relation is given by her Master who bought her, and with whom she now lives. JOHN WHEATLEY."
  - The attestation, headed "To the PUBLICK": the publisher states "Numbers would be ready to suspect they were not really the Writings of PHILLIS," so "he has procured the following Attestation, from the most respectable Characters in Boston, that none might have the least Ground for disputing their Original." Text: "WE whose Names are under-written, do assure the World, that the POEMS specified in the following Page, were (as we verily believe) written by PHILLIS, a young Negro Girl, who was but a few Years since, brought an uncultivated Barbarian from Africa, and has ever since been, and now is, under the Disadvantage of serving as a Slave in a Family in this Town. She has been examined by some of the best Judges, and is thought qualified to write them."
  - The 18 signers, in order: Governor Thomas Hutchinson; Lieutenant Governor Andrew Oliver; Thomas Hubbard; John Erving; James Pitts; Harrison Gray; James Bowdoin; John Hancock; Joseph Green; Richard Carey; the Rev. Charles Chauncy (printed "Cheuney"); the Rev. Mather Byles; the Rev. Ed. Pemberton; the Rev. Andrew Elliot; the Rev. Samuel Cooper; the Rev. Samuel Mather; the Rev. John Moorhead; "Mr. John Wheatley, her Master." Count: 18. "N. B. The original Attestation, signed by the above Gentlemen, may be seen by applying to Archibald Bell, Bookseller."
  - Wheatley's poem "To S. M. a young African Painter, on seeing his Works" is in the book.
  - Boston Evening Post, Sept. 1773, quoted in the edition: "Boston, September 20, 1773. In Captains [unclear] from London, came Passengers ... also Phyllis, the extraordinary poetical genius, Negro Servant to Mr. John Wheatley, of this Town."
- Date of the examination: "On 8 October 1772, Phillis Wheatley, then about 18 years of age, was interviewed by 18 gentlemen" (Wikipedia, "Poems on Various Subjects, Religious and Moral"; unconfirmed: search summary only). The printed attestation carries no date. The outline says 1772.
- Wording rule from the seed: say plainly what the attestation was. It was a statement signed by 18 white men of Boston, including the governor and her enslaver, because the publisher expected readers to doubt that an enslaved African girl wrote the poems. It calls her "an uncultivated Barbarian from Africa." It is not an endorsement.
- Publication date September 1, 1773, London. Freed by the Wheatleys by November 1773, shortly after the book appeared. Married John Peters in 1778. Died in poverty in 1784, about age 31. "The first book of poetry published by an African American." (Museum of the American Revolution, National Women's History Museum, Poetry Foundation, as banked in `research-slavery-freedom.md` line 99. `slavery-freedom` leads on her enslavement and freedom.)
- Her elegy for George Whitefield, printed in Boston by Ezekiel Russell in 1770 when she was about seventeen (`research-religion.md` line 1053, LOC).

### Prince Demah, enslaved portrait painter in Boston
- Amelia Peck (Metropolitan Museum of Art) and Paula M. Bagger (Hingham Historical Society), "Prince Demah Barnes: Portraitist and slave in colonial Boston," *The Magazine Antiques*, Feb 13, 2015 (https://www.themagazineantiques.com/article/prince-demah-barnes-1/):
  - Prince and his mother Daphney "were baptized in Boston's Trinity Church in May 1745."
  - "In December 1769 Christian [Barnes] wrote to her friend Elizabeth Murray Smith, a fellow merchant, that Henry had recently purchased the son of their slave Daphney and confided their 'design of improving his genius in painting.'" Prince was "a young man in his late twenties." Henry Barnes was a Marlborough, Massachusetts, distiller and merchant. Christian wrote that "Copling [Copley] will not be willing to give him any instruction."
  - "In October 1770 Henry Barnes took Prince with him on a trip to London, and in February 1771 he reported that Prince was receiving instruction from a 'Mr. Pine who has taken him purely for his genius'" (likely Robert Edge Pine).
  - Henry Barnes feared Prince might "attempt his freedom" and wrote: "I do not let [Prince] converse with any of his own colour here."
  - "Between January and November 1773, an advertisement appeared at least nine times in the Boston News-Letter: 'Negro Artist. ... a Negro man whose extraordinary Genius has been assisted by one of the best Masters in London; he takes Faces at the lowest Rates.'"
  - "In February 1773 Prince painted the portrait of William Duguid, the young Scottish textile merchant residing in Boston, signing it 'Prince Demah Barnes.'" (The stretcher is signed and dated 1773. The Met bought it.) He also painted, or copied from Copley, portraits of Henry and Christian Barnes, now owned by the Hingham Historical Society.
  - The loyalist Barnes family "left for England in late 1775"; Henry "was banished by an act of the General Court in 1778." Prince's mother Daphney was left at their Marlborough estate.
  - "In April 1777 Prince, now just Prince Demah, enlisted in Colonel Thomas Crafts Jr.'s artillery regiment of the Massachusetts militia. His name appears in regimental records through early 1778." "on March 11, 1778, he wrote his will. As 'Prince Demah, limner' and a 'free Negro,' he left all he had to his mother, 'Daphne Demah.' One week later, he died, his burial recorded at Trinity Church." Cause of death not recorded (the authors think illness, perhaps smallpox, which was endemic near the barracks).
  - How he became free is not stated in the article: he was living as "a free Negro" by 1777-78 after the Barneses left.
- The Met calls him "the only known enslaved artist working in colonial America whose paintings have survived" (Met object page https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/20517 ; unconfirmed: search summary only). Peck and Bagger: "Until the discovery of Prince's work, the earliest African-American portraitist in oils was thought to be Joshua Johnson."
- Price paid for Prince: not stated. Movie: none found. Not in any other outline (grep).

### Peale, and people given as payment for portraits
- Mount Vernon, "Charles Willson Peale" (https://www.mountvernon.org/library/digitalhistory/digital-encyclopedia/article/charles-willson-peale): born in Chester, Maryland, April 1741. "Initially trained as a saddle maker, he discovered an interest in art and studied in England for three years." Lived in Annapolis, joined the Sons of Liberty. Came to Mount Vernon on May 18, 1772, "stayed a fortnight to paint miniatures of Martha Washington and her children, as well as an oil of George Washington in his Virginia militia uniform." "His seven sittings during Washington's lifetime." Washington "was an annual subscriber to the museum and contributed two golden Chinese pheasants." Dates 1741-1827.
- Search summary (Mount Vernon "Life Portraits"): seven sittings between 1772 and 1795, more than any other painter; the 1772 painting is the earliest known portrait of Washington (unconfirmed: search summary only).
- Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia, "Peale's Philadelphia Museum" (https://philadelphiaencyclopedia.org/essays/peales-philadelphia-museum/): "Peale formally announced the opening of the Philadelphia Museum in 1784 with a display of forty-four portraits that depicted 'worthy personages' of America's Revolutionary era" in his home at Third and Lombard Streets, a "pay-for-admission exhibit." A ticket "printed in 1788, provided admission." In 1802 Pennsylvania lent him the former State House (Independence Hall). "By 1811 ... a Mammoth Room that displayed the reassembled, nearly complete skeleton of a mastodon excavated by Peale in New York state." The museum hosted "the Columbianum in 1795 (America's first art society exhibit)." The family sold the natural history collection in 1849 and the portraits at auction in 1854.
- Dates dispute: 1784 (portrait gallery announced, Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia) or 1786 (museum of natural curiosities advertised; search summary). State both. Admission 25 cents (search summary: "open to anyone with a sense of wonder and 25 cents," Discover Lewis and Clark; unconfirmed: search summary only). The mastodon was dug up in 1801 (era 06). `science` leads.
- Scarborough and Lucy, and Moses Williams: Davis Art, "Emancipated Artist: Moses Williams" (https://www.davisart.com/blogs/curators-corner/emancipated-artist-moses-williams/): "By 1776, a Maryland plantation owner had given the biracial couple [Scarborough and Lucy] to Peale as payment for portraits Peale had painted." "Peale freed Scarborough and Lucy in 1786." ### Gilbert Stuart's Washington
- Mount Vernon, "Gilbert Stuart" (https://www.mountvernon.org/library/digitalhistory/digital-encyclopedia/article/gilbert-stuart-1755-1828): born December 3, 1755, in Kingstown, Rhode Island. Sailed for London in 1775. Benjamin West, "the Pennsylvania-born artist ... who had settled in England in 1763" and was "Historical Painter to the King," "was known to be gracious to young American artists seeking to learn," and "not only hired him but gave him a studio and a place to stay." "In 1787, Stuart left London for Dublin to flee his creditors." "He arrived in New York in May 1793." In 1796 Martha Washington commissioned portraits of her husband and herself: this was the Athenaeum portrait. "Stuart never gave the painting to Washington, nor did he give it to Martha, who repeatedly requested it after the death of her husband in 1799." "he did make and sell over a hundred copies of this George Washington bust." "an engraving of it is the one seen in the dollar bill."
- Wikipedia, "Athenaeum Portrait" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athenaeum_Portrait): after Washington's death Stuart "used it to paint 130 copies, which he sold for $100 each." It "served as the model for the engraving that would be used (in mirror image) for the United States one-dollar bill." Bought in May 1831 for $1,500 by the trustees of the Boston Athenaeum. Owned jointly by the Museum of Fine Arts, Boston, and the National Portrait Gallery since 1980. Count dispute: "over a hundred" (Mount Vernon), 130 (Wikipedia), "more than 60 of these copies still exist" (search summary; unconfirmed).

### Thomas Cole, *The Oxbow*, and the Hudson River School
- Britannica, "Thomas Cole" (https://www.britannica.com/biography/Thomas-Cole): born February 1, 1801, Bolton-le-Moors, Lancashire, England. Died February 11, 1848, Catskill, New York. "Cole's family immigrated first to Philadelphia and then settled in Steubenville, Ohio. He was trained by an itinerant portrait painter named Stein and then spent two years at the Pennsylvania Academy of the Fine Arts. In 1825 some of Cole's landscapes in a New York shop window attracted the attention of Colonel John Trumbull and the painter Asher B. Durand. They bought his works." In 1826 "Cole made his home in the village of Catskill, New York, on the western bank of the Hudson River." He sketched outdoors in pencil and painted in his studio in winter. Abroad, mainly Italy, 1829-1832 and 1841-1842. *The Course of Empire*, five canvases, 1836. Immigration year 1818 (Britannica quick facts). Durand painted *Kindred Spirits* in Cole's memory.
- Britannica, "Hudson River school" (quoted in the same page's summary): "a group of American landscape painters who worked between 1825 and 1870." "The name refers to a similarity of intent rather than to a geographic location, though many members drew inspiration from the Catskill region." Cole, Thomas Doughty and Asher Durand were early leaders.
- *The Oxbow*: Wikipedia, "The Oxbow" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Oxbow): full title *View from Mount Holyoke, Northampton, Massachusetts, after a Thunderstorm*, 1836, oil on canvas, 130.8 x 193 cm, Metropolitan Museum of Art, accession 08.228. It shows the Connecticut River valley, not the Hudson. Cole painted it for the April 1836 National Academy of Design exhibition at the suggestion of his patron Luman Reed. Cole wrote Reed in March 1836: "Fancy pictures seldom sell & they generally take more time than views so I have determined to paint one of the latter." He joined two views from Mount Holyoke into one. Logging scars on the far hill appear to form the Hebrew letters for "Noah" (first noticed by the art historian Matthew Baigell). Olivia Sage gave it to the Met in 1908.
- **Date dispute:** Britannica's Cole article gives "The Ox-Bow (1846)". Wikipedia, citing the Met accession and the 1836 National Academy exhibition, gives 1836. Cole's March 1836 letter to Reed places the painting in 1836. The outline uses 1836 and notes nothing further. Britannica's 1846 appears to be an error.
- Whose land: the Thomas Cole National Historic Site states on its own page (https://thomascole.org/category/learn/thomas-cole-biography/page/2/): "The Thomas Cole National Historic Site is located on the ancestral homelands of the Muh-he-conneok: the People of the Waters That Are Never Still. Today, they are known as the Stockbridge-Munsee Band of Mohican Indians ... Today, as a result of European colonization, the Stockbridge-Munsee reservation is in northeast Wisconsin." The site's land acknowledgement marker (Historical Marker Database, https://www.hmdb.org/m.asp?m=303801, photographed June 19, 2026) adds: "Despite tremendous hardship in being forced from here, today their community resides in Wisconsin."
- How the Mohican were moved in Cole's lifetime: Milwaukee Public Museum, "Stockbridge-Munsee History" (https://www.mpm.edu/content/wirp/ICW-158): the Stockbridge founded New Stockbridge in upstate New York in 1785 at the Oneida's invitation. After an 1822 treaty with the Menominee and Ho-Chunk, "The Stockbridge in Indiana and New York began moving to Wisconsin, settling along the Fox River near present-day Kaukauna." "By 1831, 225 Stockbridge had migrated to Wisconsin along with 100 people from the Munsee Delaware." Federal officials made them move again, to the east shore of Lake Winnebago, 1832 to 1834 ($25,000 paid for improvements left behind). In 1856 one group got about 44,000 acres of the Menominee reservation in Shawano County. The Mohican had left the Hudson valley itself long before Cole's time: pushed out through the 1600s and 1700s, most moved to the mission town of Stockbridge, Massachusetts, and were then forced from there (search summary of the Cole site and hmdb; the MPM page confirms the later moves).
- The Oxbow's valley (Northampton, Mount Holyoke): not sourced in this pass for its nation. Do not name one.
- Indian Removal Act: signed May 28, 1830 (`research-native-nations.md` line 137, via NPS). SAAM's Catlin exhibition text: "Passage of the Indian Removal Act commenced the twelve-year migration of American Indians from lands east of the Mississippi River." `native-nations` and `migration` lead. The art chapter's slice: the land in these paintings had been Mohican land, and the Mohican were moved west in the same years the paintings sold.

### George Catlin and the people he painted
- Smithsonian American Art Museum, "George Catlin and His Indian Gallery" (https://americanart.si.edu/exhibitions/catlin): Catlin (1796-1872), "a lawyer turned painter." In 1830 he visited William Clark, superintendent of Indian affairs in St. Louis. "In 1832, Catlin made an epic journey that stretched over 2,000 miles along the upper Missouri River. St. Louis became Catlin's base of operations for the five trips he took between 1830 and 1836, eventually visiting fifty tribes." "Catlin's dream was to sell his Indian Gallery to the U.S. government ... After several failed attempts to persuade various officials, he toured with it in Europe in the 1840s, where he often featured Native Americans dancing, creating the earliest version of what would later become the Wild West show." "he was forced to sell the original Indian Gallery due to personal debts in 1852." In 1872 he worked in a studio in the Smithsonian Castle until his death. "A Philadelphia collector's widow donated the original Indian Gallery, more than 500 works, to the Smithsonian in 1879." (The object credit lines name her: "Gift of Mrs. Joseph Harrison, Jr.") Catlin's own words, quoted by SAAM: "If my life be spared, nothing shall stop me from visiting every nation of Indians on the Continent of North America."
- Named sitter 1, Mandan: SAAM, *Máh-to-tóh-pa, Four Bears, Second Chief, in Full Dress*, 1832, oil on canvas, 29 x 24 in., 1985.66.128 (https://americanart.si.edu/artwork/mah-toh-pa-four-bears-second-chief-full-dress-4064). "The artist painted this portrait at a Mandan village in 1832." Catlin called him "the first and most popular man in the nation" (Catlin, *Letters and Notes*, 1841). A companion portrait, *Four Bears, Second Chief in Mourning*, 1832, is also at SAAM (search result).
- Four Bears was himself a painter. Wikipedia, "Mato-tope" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mato-tope): born about 1784, died July 30, 1837. He painted buffalo robes showing his own battles. "Catlin secured a robe recounting Four Bears' deeds in 1832, now preserved in the United States National Museum." Prince Maximilian of Wied, travelling with the Swiss painter Karl Bodmer in 1833-34, took another Four Bears robe to Europe. It is in the Linden Museum, Stuttgart, Germany.
- Four Bears's death: the American Fur Company's steamboat *St. Peters* brought smallpox to Fort Clark, beside the Mandan villages, on June 18, 1837. About 2,000 Mandan lived there in spring 1837, and 138 were alive in October (State Historical Society of North Dakota, via `research-health.md` lines 323-325). Wikipedia "Mato-tope": 27, or by other accounts 100 to 150, survivors. Four Bears died July 30, 1837. The fort's trader Francis Chardon wrote: "One of our best friend of the Village (The Four Bears) died to day." Catlin later claimed Four Bears starved himself in grief. `health` leads on the epidemic.
- Named sitter 2, Seminole: Osceola. General Thomas Jesup ordered his seizure under a white flag of truce on October 21, 1837, near St. Augustine. He was held at Fort Marion, St. Augustine, then moved to Fort Moultrie, South Carolina, and died a prisoner there on January 30, 1838 (NPS "Osceola," via `research-war.md` PATCH T-259b). The Charleston Museum, "Captive Sitter: Osceola at Fort Moultrie" (https://www.charlestonmuseum.org/news-events/captive-sitter-osceola-at-fort-moultrie/, Chad Stewart, 2023): at Fort Moultrie the prisoner drew crowds, "this group included at least three portraitists." He died of "chronic malaria, tonsillitis, and an abscess." Wikipedia "Osceola" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osceola): the three were George Catlin, W. M. Laning and Robert John Curtis, and they "persuaded the Seminole leader to allow his portrait to be painted despite his being gravely ill." Smithsonian magazine ("A Seminole Warrior Cloaked in Defiance," https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/a-seminole-warrior-cloaked-in-defiance-60004300/): Catlin painted him from life in 1838 and later made a lithograph of it, while Osceola "suffered from malaria." After Osceola died, the army doctor Frederick Weedon "cut off Osceola's head and buried his decapitated body, displaying the Seminole leader's head in his drug store" (Wikipedia "Osceola"). The Charleston Museum states his wish to be buried in Florida "was not honored" and "his body was ignobly decapitated before burial at Fort Moultrie." `war` and `native-nations` lead on the capture. Art's slice: the portrait sittings of a dying prisoner, and what the doctor did with his head.
- What the paintings were used for (sourced): Catlin tried to sell the gallery to the United States government and failed; he toured it through Europe in the 1840s with Native performers dancing; he sold it to pay his debts in 1852; the buyer's widow gave it to the Smithsonian in 1879 (SAAM, above). Catlin's 1832 call for "a nation's park" is `land-environment`'s (`research-land-environment.md` line 76).
- Names of the Native performers who toured Europe with Catlin (Ojibwe and Iowa groups, 1840s): not opened in this pass. The outline does not name them.

### Dave the Potter (David Drake), Edgefield, South Carolina
- Smithsonian American Art Museum, "David 'Dave' Drake: Potter and Poet" (Rothschild and Umberger, Feb 1, 2023, https://americanart.si.edu/blog/david-drake-potter-poet): Drake (about 1800 to after 1870). "Drake himself was born into slavery around 1800 and labored in bondage in Edgefield area potteries for most of his life." "Dave" is the name signed on the jars. He was freed by the Emancipation Proclamation, and took the surname Drake after emancipation. Method: he turned the bottom of a jar on a wheel, then added large coils of clay by hand for the top. The Edgefield potteries grew "because white manufacturers exploited the unpaid labor of enslaved African men, women, and children." "In 1834, South Carolina outlawed literacy among its enslaved population." "Drake is the only Black potter in antebellum Edgefield known to have signed his own work." He may have learned to read while enslaved by Harvey Drake and working at Harvey Drake's partner Abner Landrum's newspaper, the *Edgefield Hive*. He signed other enslaved potters' names beside his own: Mark, Abram and Baddler. Inscriptions quoted: "I wonder where is all my relation / Friendship to all, and every nation" (August 16, 1857, Greenville County Museum of Art); "I made this Jar all of cross, If you dont repent, you will be, lost" (1862, National Museum of American History). SAAM's own jar is dated November 9, 1860, marked "LM" for Lewis Miles.
- The Charleston Museum, "The Remarkable Edgefield Pottery of David Drake" (Chad Stewart, https://www.charlestonmuseum.org/news-events/the-remarkable-edgefield-pottery-of-david-drake/): "held in bondage by at least four men over his lifetime." First enslaver Harvey Drake (pottery with Abner Landrum). "He was later transferred or sold within the Landrum family and was enslaved by Rev. John Landrum, and Franklin Landrum, before finally ending up with Lewis Miles who owned the 'Stony Bluff' factory." Jar dated July 31, 1840: "Dave belongs to Mr. Miles, wher the oven bakes and the pot biles." Two of the museum's jars hold "in excess of forty gallons each." He is believed to have died in the 1870s.
- Smithsonian magazine, "For the Enslaved Potter David Drake, His Literary Practice Was His Resistance" (https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smithsonian-institution/dave-the-potters-resistance-was-his-literary-practice-180981707/): "an 1834 state law made it unlawful for the enslaved to learn to read and write." A 2006 survey by Arthur F. Goldberg and James Witkowski "counted 169 vessels made by and attributed to Drake. More than a hundred, dated between 1849 and 1864, are boldly signed 'Dave.'" "Drake was only one of 76 known enslaved people who worked at the 12 potteries in Edgefield." "His poetry and insignia are known to appear on 50 vessels." The record gives "the sale away of his family and a leg amputation." His name is absent from the 1880 census.
- Britannica, "Dave the Potter" (https://www.britannica.com/biography/Dave-the-Potter): a pot from 1834 reads "put every bit all between / surely this jar will hold 14." The last pot attributed to him is dated 1864. The 1870 census lists "David Drake, occupation: turner." (Britannica's AI summary says "Starting in 1840, Dave signed his pots, with inscriptions appearing even earlier.")
- Wikipedia, "David Drake (potter)" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Drake_(potter)): first legal record June 13, 1818: "a boy about 17 years old country born" "mortgaged to Eldrid Simkins by Harvey Drake." After Harvey Drake died in 1832, his brother Reuben held him until Reuben left for Louisiana in 1835. The Rev. John Landrum died in 1846 and "all 18 of the people he enslaved were put up for sale." Lewis Miles bought him in 1849. Poems: three in 1857, eight in 1858, seven in 1859. How he lost his leg is not known: one account says an owner beat him for writing, another says a train accident. Neither is established.
- First inscription: a jar dated June 12, 1834, carries the single word "Concatination" (Sotheby's 2005 lot title confirms a Dave jar "dated June 12, 1834"; the word itself is from search summaries only, unconfirmed: search summary only).
- Land: Edgefield District. Its Native nation was not sourced in this pass. SAAM notes Catawba coiling methods resemble those in Drake's jars but says the link to the area's Native people "is unclear."
- Era split: the 1834 law, his first inscriptions (1834, 1840) and his early enslavers are era 06. His most-inscribed years (1857-1862), emancipation and the 1870 census are era 07.
- Movie: none found. Not told as a story in any other outline (grep of `outlines/`).

### Painters on the road, and folk art
- Heritage Museums and Gardens, "Folk Portraits, Fact and Fiction" (https://heritagemuseumsandgardens.org/folk-portraits-fact-and-fiction-part-i/): self-taught artists "traveled from town to town, charging somewhat less" than trained painters, mostly 1700 to 1850. On the seed's claim: "A common misconception about folk portraits is that artists would paint generic bodies on canvases, then ask their customers to choose a body on which to have their head painted ... In fact, incomplete folk art portraits that have been discovered have the faces finished but not the bodies, showing that the artists painted the heads of their sitters first." **Correction to the seed:** the body-in-advance practice is a myth. The outline now states the finding.
- Ammi Phillips: Wikipedia (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ammi_Phillips): born April 24, 1788, Colebrook, Connecticut, died July 11, 1865. Itinerant portrait painter from the mid-1810s to the early 1860s in Connecticut, Massachusetts and New York. First signed portraits 1811. *Girl in Red Dress with Cat and Dog* (Dutchess County, mid-1830s) was bought for the American Folk Art Museum in 1985 for one million dollars. For years his work went under the names "Kent Limner" and "Border Limner" until collectors Barbara and Larry Holdridge matched it to him.
- Edward Hicks: Wikipedia (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Hicks): born April 4, 1780, Attleboro (now Langhorne), Pennsylvania, died August 23, 1849, Newtown, Pennsylvania. Many show William Penn meeting Native people (the Lenape; see era 04 on the Penn deeds and the Walking Purchase). "In his lifetime Hicks was better known as a minister than as a painter."
- Moses Williams: Davis Art, "Emancipated Artist: Moses Williams" (Karl Cole, June 17, 2024, https://www.davisart.com/blogs/curators-corner/emancipated-artist-moses-williams/): freed from Peale's household "around 1802." ### Photography arrives, 1839
- Robert Cornelius's self-portrait, Philadelphia, October or November 1839, "the earliest surviving American portrait photograph" (Library of Congress, via `research-technology.md` line 106). News of Daguerre's process reached the United States in late August to September 1839. `technology` leads on the camera.
- Visualizing 19th-Century New York (Bard Graduate Center), Claire McRee, "Mathew Brady and the Daguerreotype Portrait" (https://visualizingnyc.org/essays/mathew-brady-and-the-daguerreotype-portrait/): "the first American commercial portrait studio opened there [New York] in 1840, and by 1853 the city had more daguerreotype studios than all of England." "by 1850 The Daguerreian Journal reported that the city had seventy-one daguerreotype studios." Brady opened his first studio in 1844 at 205 Broadway, across from P. T. Barnum's American Museum. "cheaper studios on Broadway and especially the Bowery offered working-class individuals the opportunity to have portraits made."
- Price: *American Daguerreotypes* (University of Virginia American Studies project), ch. 2 (https://americandaguerreotypes.com/ch2.html), citing Beaumont Newhall, *The Daguerreotype in America*, p. 63: "The price of a daguerreotype, at the height of its popularity in the early 1850's, ranged from 25 cents for a sixteenth plate (of 1 5/8 inches by 1 3/8 inches) to 50 cents for a low-quality 'picture factory' likeness to $2 for a medium-sized portrait at Matthew Brady's Broadway studio." Brady added cheaper portraits in 1853. The prices are the early 1850s (era 07 by date), used in era 06 as the result of the 1840s spread.
- A daguerreotype: a photograph made directly on a silver-coated copper plate. Each one is a single picture that cannot be copied.

### Photographs of the Civil War dead: Brady, Gardner, Gibson, O'Sullivan
- Wikipedia, "Alexander Gardner (photographer)" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Gardner_(photographer)): Gardner (born October 17, 1821, Paisley, Scotland, died December 10, 1882, Washington). Worked for Mathew Brady from 1856. Brady put him in charge of the Washington gallery in 1858. He photographed the Battle of Antietam in September 1862. "Gardner's work has often been misattributed to Brady." About November 1862 "Gardner ended his working relationship with Brady, probably in part because of Brady's practice of attributing his employees' work as 'Photographed by Brady.'" In May 1863 Gardner and his brother James opened their own Washington studio, "hiring many of Brady's former staff." He photographed Gettysburg (July 1863) and Petersburg. In 1866 he published *Gardner's Photographic Sketch Book of the Civil War*, two volumes, 50 prints each, and credited each photographer: Timothy H. O'Sullivan, James F. Gibson, John Reekie, William Pywell, James Gardner, John Wood, George N. Barnard, David Knox and others. The book did not sell well.
- The Antietam show: *The New York Times*, October 20, 1862, p. 5, "Brady's Photographs. Pictures of the Dead at Antietam" (transcribed by the American Battlefield Trust, https://www.battlefields.org/learn/primary-sources/pictures-dead-antietam): "Mr. Brady has done something to bring home to us the terrible reality and earnestness of war. If he has not brought bodies and laid them in our door-yards and along the streets, he has done something very like it. At the door of the gallery hangs a little placard, 'The Dead of Antietam.' Crowds of people are constantly going up the stairs." The article credits Brady. The photographs were taken by Gardner, with James F. Gibson (search summaries of Gilder Lehrman and Explore New York History; Gardner's role confirmed by the Wikipedia article above). The gallery was at Broadway and Tenth Street (search summary). American Battlefield Trust's estimated casualties for Antietam: 22,717 (Union 12,401, Confederate 10,316). `war` leads on the battle.
- The moved body at Gettysburg: Wikipedia, "Home of a Rebel Sharpshooter" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Home_of_a_Rebel_Sharpshooter): photographed July 6, 1863, at Devil's Den. A sales catalog months after the battle named O'Sullivan as the photographer, though Gardner later claimed it. Gardner's 1866 caption called the dead man a Confederate sharpshooter killed by a shell and said the photographer found his bones there in November. "In 1961, it was suggested in a magazine article that the photograph had been staged." In 1975 the historian William A. Frassanito showed it "was one of six taken of the same body, which had originally been located on the south edge of the Devil's Den before being moved next to a stone wall." The rifle "was likely a prop." **The argument:** in 1998 the Maryland artist James Groves argued, from a Union artillery officer's postwar recollection, that the body first lay in the stone niche and was moved from there to the other spot. Scott Fink (*Civil War Times*, 2018) says the officer's memory was probably shaped by the famous photograph. Both sides agree the photographers moved the body. They disagree about which direction. The dead man's name is not known.

### Photographs and paintings of the West, and whose land they showed
- Carleton Watkins: Wikipedia (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carleton_Watkins): born November 11, 1829, Oneonta, New York, died 1916, Napa, California. In July 1861 he took a mammoth-plate camera (glass plates 18 by 22 inches) to Yosemite and came back with 30 mammoth plates and 100 stereo negatives. They were shown at Goupil's gallery in New York in 1862. "His photographs of the valley significantly influenced the United States Congress' decision to preserve it." In 1864 the California State Geological Survey hired him.
- Yosemite Grant: signed by Lincoln June 30, 1864 (`research-land-environment.md` line 85, National Archives). Yosemite's people: the Ahwahneechee. In 1851 the Mariposa Battalion, about 200 mounted volunteers under Major James D. Savage, raised by California Governor John McDougal, invaded the valley. NPS: "By 1910, over 90% of the original Ahwahneechee inhabitants were dead or missing" (`research-land-environment.md` lines 109-112). `land-environment` leads.
- William Henry Jackson and Thomas Moran on the 1871 Hayden survey: Jackson took the first known photographs of the Yellowstone falls. The survey helped persuade Congress, and President Grant signed the Yellowstone act on March 1, 1872 (`research-exploration.md` line 382). Moran's painting *The Grand Canyon of the Yellowstone* (1872, oil, 84 x 104.8 in.) "was purchased by Congress in 1872 for $10,000 and displayed at the United States Capitol" and moved to the Interior Department in 1950 (Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Grand_Canyon_of_the_Yellowstone_(1872)). The painting shows four small figures in the foreground, one of them a Native man.
- Yellowstone's people: the Tukudika ("Sheep Eaters"), a band of Mountain Shoshone, "lived for thousands of years in the area," and "were forcibly removed to reservations" (NPS, via `research-land-environment.md` line 114).
- Timothy O'Sullivan photographed for the Wheeler survey of 1872-1879, whose orders included to "discover the numbers, habits, and disposition of Indians" (`research-exploration.md` line 384).

### Ledger art by Plains prisoners at Fort Marion, 1875-1878
- Why they were held, and by whom: Judith Lee Wilmot, "Warrior-Artists in a Tourist Town," *Cowboys and Indians*, August 12, 2017 (https://www.cowboysindians.com/2017/08/warrior-artists-in-a-tourist-town/): after the Red River War, "the military charged 72 Cheyenne, Arapaho, Kiowa, Comanche, and Caddo warriors with war crimes or with being 'ringleaders.' Sheridan ordered them sent 'to an eastern fort to be held without trial for an undefined time.'" (General Philip Sheridan.) The trip from Fort Sill, Indian Territory, took 26 days by wagon, train, steamship and oxcart. The poet Sidney Lanier saw them arrive "in leg-irons." Lt. Richard Henry Pratt removed the shackles. "Illness, homesickness, and depression combined with Florida's summer heat and the fort's lack of proper facilities increased mortality." Released April 1878. "More than a thousand examples of Fort Marion prisoner-artists' work can still be found in collections around the world." The group included one woman, Mochi, and one child, Black Horse's daughter, about nine.
- Making Medicine's 1877 letter for the young prisoners (same article): "We have lived in this old place two years. It is old and we are young. We want Washington to give us our wives and children, our fathers and mothers. We are willing to learn and want to work." In June 1875 the Kiowa Mah-Mante (Skywalker) asked, "only give us our women and children." President Grant agreed, then changed his mind.
- Wikipedia, "Ledger art" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ledger_art): "From 1875 to 1878, the 71 men and one woman were under the command of Richard Henry Pratt." He gave them "pencils, ink, crayons, watercolor paint, and paper." "Twenty-six of the Fort Marion prisoners engaged in drawing. They were younger Cheyenne, Arapaho, and Kiowa men." Named artists: Paul Caryl Zotom (Kiowa), Making Medicine (David Pendleton Oakerhater, Cheyenne), Tichkematse or Squint Eyes (Cheyenne), Wohaw (Kiowa), Howling Wolf (Cheyenne), Etahdleuh Doanmoe (Kiowa), White Bear (Arapaho), Koba (Kiowa), Bear's Heart (Cheyenne). Ledger art grew out of painting on buffalo hides. Plains artists moved to paper when the buffalo became scarce. Ledger books were used because they were the paper at hand.
- Massachusetts Historical Society, "Book of Sketches Made at Fort Marion, St. Augustine, Fla., 1877" (https://www.masshist.org/collection-guides/view/fao0007): sketches by Making Medicine and others, including Bear's Heart, Buffalo Meat, Etahdleuh Doanmoe and Koba. Biographies: Making Medicine (1844-1931, Cheyenne name O-kuh-ha-tuh), ordained an Episcopal deacon June 7, 1881. Bear's Heart (1851-1882, Nockkoist) died of tuberculosis. Buffalo Meat (1847-1917) died of tuberculosis. Etahdleuh Doanmoe (1856-1888, Kiowa) went to Hampton and Carlisle. Koba (1848-1880, Kiowa) died of tuberculosis. Howling Wolf (1849-1927, Ho-na-nist-to), sergeant of the prisoner guard company; after release his eyesight failed; he spoke against the Dawes Act of 1887; he died in a car accident.
- Howling Wolf and Sand Creek: Wikipedia, "Howling Wolf (Cheyenne)" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Howling_Wolf_(Cheyenne)): member of Black Kettle's band. He and his father Eagle Head (Minimic) were in the camp at Sand Creek on November 29, 1864, when Colonel John Chivington and the First Regiment of Colorado Volunteers attacked it. He was 15. About 137 Southern Cheyenne and Arapaho were killed, most of them women and children (Wikipedia's figure). His later drawings include Sand Creek (Wikipedia "Ledger art" caption: "The Sand Creek massacre as drawn by eyewitness Howling Wolf (1874-1875)"). `native-nations` and `war` lead on Sand Creek.
- Sale of the drawings: `research-education.md` line 670: Pratt "sold their crafts and drawings." A search summary gives about $2 a book and $2,000 to $3,000 in total (unconfirmed: search summary only). Not in the outline as a figure.
- Pratt then founded Carlisle (`education` leads).

### Painters after the war
- Winslow Homer: Britannica (https://www.britannica.com/biography/Winslow-Homer): born February 24, 1836, Boston. Died September 29, 1910, Prouts Neck, Maine. Apprenticed at a lithography firm, freelance illustrator for *Ballou's Pictorial* and *Harper's Weekly*. "During the American Civil War, he created drawings at the front for Harper's Weekly, focusing on everyday camp life." Watercolor from 1873. Cullercoats, England, 1881. Moved to Prouts Neck, Maine, in 1883, and the sea became his main subject (*The Life Line*, *Fog Warning*).
- Thomas Eakins: Wikipedia (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Eakins): born July 25, 1844, Philadelphia, died June 25, 1916. *The Gross Clinic*, 1875. Taught at the Pennsylvania Academy from 1876, salaried 1878, director 1882. "He was ultimately forced to resign in 1886, for removing the loincloth of a male model in a class where female students were present." Students included Henry Ossawa Tanner. Some students then formed the Art Students' League of Philadelphia (1886-1893), where he taught.
- Mary Cassatt: Britannica (https://www.britannica.com/biography/Mary-Cassatt): born May 22, 1844, Allegheny City (now Pittsburgh), died June 14, 1926, near Paris. Studied at the Pennsylvania Academy 1861-65. Went to Europe in 1866. Settled in Paris in 1874. "Degas was known to admire her drawing especially, and at his request she exhibited with the Impressionists in 1879 and joined them in shows in 1880, 1881, and 1886." Britannica caption: "She was the only American to exhibit with the French Impressionists in the 1870s and '80s."
- Robert S. Duncanson: Wikipedia (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_S._Duncanson): born about 1821, Fayette, New York, died December 21, 1872, Detroit. Son of a free Black tradesman. Self-taught. Worked in Cincinnati, where about 3,000 Black people lived when he arrived in 1840. "Considered the first African-American artist to be internationally known." Nicholas Longworth hired him to paint eight landscape murals, each about nine feet high, in the entry of his Cincinnati house, Belmont (dated about 1850 in one passage and 1851 in another of the same article; the house is now the Taft Museum of Art). *Uncle Tom and Little Eva*, 1853, Detroit Institute of Arts. "During the Civil War, Duncanson exiled himself to Canada and the United Kingdom" (exhibited in Montreal 1864, Dublin 1865).

### Henry Ossawa Tanner
- Wikipedia (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Ossawa_Tanner): born June 21, 1859, Pittsburgh. Died May 25, 1937, Paris. Father Benjamin Tucker Tanner, a bishop of the African Methodist Episcopal Church. Studied with Eakins at the Pennsylvania Academy. Moved to Paris in 1891, Académie Julian. *The Banjo Lesson*, 1893. *The Resurrection of Lazarus* (1896) "was purchased by the French government after winning the third-place medal at the 1897 Salon" (Musée d'Orsay). Legion of Honor 1923.
- Will South, "A Missing Question Mark: The Unknown Henry Ossawa Tanner," *Nineteenth-Century Art Worldwide*, Autumn 2009 (https://www.19thc-artworldwide.org/autumn09/a-missing-question-mark): Tanner at the Pennsylvania Academy "on and off through 1885" from 1879. From Tanner's autobiographical article "The Story of an Artist's Life" (*The World's Work*, 1909), on looking for a teacher as a boy: "No man or boy to whom this country is a land of 'equal chances' can realize what heartache this question caused me, and with what trepidation I made the rounds of the studios. The question was not, would the desired teacher have a boy who knew nothing and had little money, but would he have me, or would he keep me after he found out who I was." The printmaker Joseph Pennell, a fellow student, wrote in a memoir: "one night his easel was carried out into the middle of Broad Street and, though not painfully crucified, he was firmly tied to it and left there." (Pennell does not name the students who did it.) Atlanta patrons Bishop Joseph Crane Hartzell and Mrs. Hartzell paid for his study in Europe by buying every painting in a show of his work. In Paris in 1891-92 other American students mocked him (James Taylor Harwood's letters). He came back to the United States in 1893 with typhoid fever and painted *The Banjo Lesson* in Philadelphia.
- **The seed asked for "the quotation about why he did not stay."** The commonly repeated line "I am simply M. Tanner, an American artist. Nobody knows or cares what was the complexion of my forebears" is given by Wikiquote only "as quoted in" a 2012 magazine, with no primary source. A search summary says he "fled quickly back to Paris, convinced that he could not fight racial prejudice in this country and pursue his art at the same time": this is a writer's paraphrase, not his words. The outline prints his own 1909 words about teachers instead, and the Pennell account, and does not print the "M. Tanner" line.

### Edmonia Lewis
- Birth: Wikipedia (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmonia_Lewis): "c. July 4, 1844," Greenbush, Rensselaer County, New York. "On official documents she variously gave 1842, 1844, and 1854 as her birth year" (citing the *American National Biography*). Oberlin College Libraries (https://oberlincollegelibraries.tumblr.com/post/620541150901780480/wcw-edmonia-lewis): "Born July 4, 1844. Died September 17, 1907." Named Wildfire at birth, orphaned by age 9. Mother Catherine Mike Lewis, of Mississauga Ojibwe and African American descent. As a girl she and her aunts sold Ojibwe baskets, moccasins and embroidered blouses to tourists at Niagara Falls. Her brother Samuel paid for her schooling from gold-rush money.
- Oberlin, 1862: Oberlin College Libraries: "Attended Oberlin Academy Preparatory School and Oberlin College from 1859 until 1862 when she was accused of poisoning two of her white roommates. Although acquitted of the charge, Lewis was assaulted by unknown white vigilantes and not permitted to graduate." Wikipedia: the two were Maria Miles and Christina Ennes. In winter 1862 Lewis served them spiced wine before a sleigh ride, and they fell ill. Doctors said they had been poisoned, "reportedly with cantharides also known as 'Spanish Fly.'" Both recovered. "One night while Lewis was walking home alone, she was dragged into an open field by unknown assailants, badly beaten, and left for dead." Days later officials arrested her for the poisoning. She was too badly hurt to walk, and the trial was postponed. "Her attackers were never identified." John Mercer Langston, an Oberlin graduate and the first Black lawyer in Ohio, defended her. The charges were dismissed because no one had tested the young women's stomach contents, so there was no evidence of poison. About a year later she was accused of stealing art supplies. That case was dismissed too. She was not allowed to register for her last term. Oberlin awarded her a degree in 2022 (Times Union, May 31, 2022, https://www.timesunion.com/news/article/East-Greenbush-historian-secures-diploma-for-19th-17208566.php: "Lewis was expelled from Oberlin College on trumped-up accusations by two white female classmates"). Cantharides ("Spanish fly"): Wikipedia disambiguation "Spanish fly" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_fly): cantharidin is "a poisonous compound secreted by blister beetles, historically used in medication and as an aphrodisiac." Plain wording for the prose: a poison made from beetles.
- Boston and Rome: Wikipedia: in Boston she made a bust of Colonel Robert Gould Shaw; the Shaw family bought it, and she sold 100 plaster copies at $15 each. That money paid for Rome. Her 1865 passport reads: "M. Edmonia Lewis is a Black girl sent by subscription to Italy having displayed great talents as a sculptor." She moved to Rome in 1866 (Wikipedia) or 1865 (Oberlin College Libraries). State both. Her own words, *The New York Times*, December 29, 1878, p. 5, "Seeking equality abroad" (quoted in Wikipedia): "I was practically driven to Rome in order to obtain the opportunities for art culture, and to find a social atmosphere where I was not constantly reminded of my color. The land of liberty had no room for a colored sculptor."
- *The Death of Cleopatra*: Smithsonian American Art Museum (https://americanart.si.edu/artwork/death-cleopatra-33878): marble, carved 1876, 63 x 31 1/4 x 46 in., gift of the Historical Society of Forest Park, Illinois, 1994.17. Shown at the Centennial Exhibition in Philadelphia in 1876. A critic in 1878 called the realistic death "ghastly" and "absolutely repellant" (William J. Clark, *Great American Sculpture*). "Not long after its debut, however, Death of Cleopatra was presumed lost for almost a century, appearing at a Chicago saloon, marking a horse's grave at a suburban racetrack, and eventually reappearing at a salvage yard in the 1980s." Wikipedia: shown at the 1878 Chicago Interstate Exposition. A gambler, "Blind John" Condon, bought it from a saloon on Clark Street to mark the grave of a racehorse named Cleopatra in Forest Park. It was later moved to a construction yard in Cicero, Illinois. The scholars Marilyn Richardson and Dorothy Porter Wesley brought it to the Smithsonian curator George Gurney's attention.
- Died September 17, 1907, London (Oberlin College Libraries).

### The Shaw Memorial, Boston, 1897
- National Park Service, "The Robert Gould Shaw Memorial" (https://www.nps.gov/articles/the-robert-gould-shaw-memorial.htm): Joshua Bowen Smith, a Black Bostonian who had worked for the Shaw family, is credited with the idea, soon after the 54th Massachusetts attacked Fort Wagner in July 1863. Corporal James Gooding of the 54th wanted it in Massachusetts. Lydia Maria Child wrote that "the colored soldiers who fell ought to have a monument also." A committee of 21 hired Augustus Saint-Gaudens in August 1882. He worked on the relief from about 1883 to 1897, "14 years of his life," longer than on any other work. He did not use photographs of the soldiers and did not meet them. "Instead, Saint-Gaudens hired Black men he came across on the street near his studio," and made about 40 study heads. Shaw's sister Josephine Lowell asked for the names of all the men of the 54th killed at Fort Wagner and after to be put on the base. "Lowell's efforts were fruitless ... the original names included only the white officers. This later became rectified in the early 1980s." Dedicated May 31, 1897, on Boston Common. The procession was led by 65 veterans of the 54th. Sergeant William Carney carried the flag.
- Search summary (NPS and others): the Friends of the Public Garden added the soldiers' names in 1982 (unconfirmed: search summary only for the exact year; NPS says "early 1980s").

### PATCH 2026-09-28 (T-267a): Spiro, who dug, and what they did (era 01)
- "The Treasure of Spiro Mounds," *405 Magazine* (author-photographer's name truncated in the page text) (journalism, drawing on David La Vere, *Looting Spiro Mounds: An American King Tut's Tomb*, University of Oklahoma Press, 2007) (https://www.405magazine.com/the-treasure-of-spiro-mounds/): "in 1933 ... a group of six men, calling themselves the Pocola Mining Company, kicked in $50 each to raise the $300 Evans was asking for a 2-year lease to excavate the mound on the Craig property." George Evans, the grandfather of the Craig heirs, leased the land to pay off his mortgage and to treat his grandson's tuberculosis. Forrest Clements of the University of Oklahoma's archaeology department "tried to stop the dig" and tried to get Evans to cancel the lease. Under the new 1935 law Clements filed a complaint with the LeFlore County sheriff. When he left for California the diggers returned. "Witnesses tell of beads, pearls and arrowheads spilled across the site, feather capes and elaborate weavings trampled, ancient cedar poles burned as firewood and human bones piled at the edge of camp, where they soon crumbled to dust." "Accounts tell of the miners hauling out the decorated shells by the wheelbarrow and dumping them near the entrance, where they were crushed underfoot." La Vere, quoted: "In a fit of spite, just to jab their finger in Clements' eye, they packed the central chamber of the Great Temple Mound with kegs of black powder and touched off a mighty explosion." The blast created a cave-in and a large crack in the mound.
- Oklahoma "passing one of the nation's first laws to protect ancient sites in 1935" (same article).
- The outline adds the black-powder blast and the bones, attributed to La Vere's history.

### PATCH 2026-09-28 (T-267b): Catlin's gallery, the performers, the price and the sale (era 06)
- Smithsonian magazine, "George Catlin's Obsession" (https://www.smithsonianmag.com/arts-culture/george-catlins-obsession-72840046/): admission to his gallery at Clinton Hall, New York, opened September 23, 1837, was 50 cents. "In 1837, he tried to sell his gallery to the federal government, but Congress dawdled." He sailed for England in November 1839 with the gallery, "a buffalo-hide tepee and two live bears." In London, Brussels and at the Louvre in Paris "he was joined by several groups of Indians (21 Ojibwe and 14 Iowa) who were touring Europe with promoters," and "Catlin convinced the Ojibwe and the Iowa to reenact hunts, dances, even scalpings." In 1845 in Paris "the Ojibwe got smallpox. Two died; the rest went back to the plains." (The two are not named in this source.) "In 1852, his funds exhausted, the 56-year-old Catlin was thrown into a London debtor's prison." "Congress thought the price too steep, even when Catlin lowered it from $65,000 to $25,000." Joseph Harrison, "a wealthy Pennsylvania railroad tycoon," "paid Catlin's debts, acquired his gallery for $20,000 and shipped it from London to Philadelphia," where it sat "in Harrison's boiler factory." Harrison's widow gave "some 450 of Catlin's original paintings" and a third of a freight car of objects to the Smithsonian seven years after Catlin's death. By 1836 Catlin "had visited 48 tribes."
- **Count dispute:** 48 nations (Smithsonian magazine) or fifty (SAAM exhibition text). More than 500 works (SAAM) or some 450 paintings plus objects (Smithsonian magazine). The outline uses fifty from SAAM for the nations. State both where a writer needs a number.
- Catlin took objects from the people he painted: "As proof of his art's accuracy, Catlin sometimes acquired artifacts (such as this Sioux cradle) from his subjects." In 1836 he went to the red-stone pipe quarry in southwestern Minnesota "despite the vehement protests of Sioux elders" (same article). The Four Bears robe (era 06 section) is one of these objects.
- The Ojibwe group in the search results came "mostly from Walpole Island" and included Mississauga and Ottawa people (search summary of the National Gallery of Canada Review; unconfirmed: search summary only).

### PATCH 2026-09-28 (T-267b): Joseph Whiting Stock, a painter who could not walk (era 06, disability thread)
- Springfield Museums, "Thomas Henry and Wilbur Fisk Stock, Joseph Whiting Stock" (https://springfieldmuseums.org/blog/portfolio-item/thomas-henry-and-wilbur-fisk-stock-joseph-whiting-stock/): born January 30, 1815, Springfield, Massachusetts, third of twelve children. At eleven "an upturned oxcart fell on him, leaving him paralyzed from the waist down, and unable to move from his bed. He was bedridden until age nineteen, when a local doctor designed and built a wheelchair for him." A doctor suggested art. Lessons from seventeen. Journal: "I attempted my sister Eliza's portrait and made so good a likeness as to induce my friends to encourage me by their patronage." First trip: a month in Wilbraham, 1836. Two years in Warren, Rhode Island, and New Bedford, and several years in Orange County, New York. "During his travels he stayed with local families, sometimes exchanging paintings for room and board or art supplies." Died 1855, aged forty. "he completed over nine hundred paintings, most of which are recorded in his journal. Only one hundred or so are known today." The museum holds the journal.
- Wikipedia, "Joseph Whiting Stock" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Whiting_Stock): Dr. James Swan commissioned anatomical drawings in 1834 and "constructed a wheelchair that enabled Stock to paint large canvasses and be lifted on trains so as to travel for commissions." Teacher: Franklin White, a pupil of Chester Harding. The journal is published: Juliette Tomlinson, ed., *The Paintings and the Journal of Joseph Whiting Stock* (Wesleyan University Press, 1976).
- Terra Foundation for American Art (https://collection.terraamericanart.org/people/358/joseph-whiting-stock): his career "was cut short by tuberculosis." "He left an account of his life and career that details his patronage and the creation and prices of more than nine-hundred works he completed between 1832 and 1846."
