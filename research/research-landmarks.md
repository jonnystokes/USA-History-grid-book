## 01 Before 1500 — the landmarks were already here

- **Monks Mound, Cahokia (Illinois).** The largest earthen structure in the Americas: about 100 feet tall, base of about **14 acres** — a bigger footprint than the Great Pyramid of Giza's (about 13 acres). The comparison is base area, not height or volume: the Great Pyramid is far taller. Built in stages, mostly c. 1050–1200. Sources: Cahokia Mounds State Historic Site / [National Geographic](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/history/history-magazine/article/this-american-indian-metropolis-was-mysteriously-abandoned-archaeologists-want-to-know-why); base-vs-base figures cross-checked ([Monks Mound overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monks_Mound)). City-building's verified bank carries the same numbers (100 ft, ~14 acres). Cahokia as a city and nation = `native-nations` (lead) and `city-building`; the structure itself is ours.
- **Serpent Mound (Ohio).** Quarter-mile-long earthen snake effigy, the largest serpent effigy in the world. **Builder dispute — state honestly:** no artifacts inside the mound identify its builders. 1991 radiocarbon tests pointed to the Fort Ancient culture (c. 1070 AD); 2014 tests pointed to the Adena culture (c. 300 BC); later work supports both, so archaeologists now consider it possible the mound was built and rebuilt in more than one period. Sources: [Ohio History Connection — Who Built Serpent Mound?](https://www.ohiohistory.org/who-built-serpent-mound/), [Ohio History Connection site page](https://www.ohiohistory.org/visit/browse-historical-sites/serpent-mound/).
- **Poverty Point (Louisiana).** Mound-and-ridge complex built **c. 1700–1100 BC** by hunter-fisher-gatherers — five mounds, six concentric earthen ridges, a central plaza. The largest earthwork complex of its age north of Mexico. Named a **UNESCO World Heritage Site June 22, 2014**. Sources: [Louisiana state parks](https://www.lastateparks.com/historic-sites/poverty-point-world-heritage-site), [UNESCO/State Dept. announcement](https://2009-2017.state.gov/r/pa/prs/ps/2014/06/228265.htm). Clears the seed's [VERIFY].
- **Chaco Canyon great houses (New Mexico), c. 850–1150.** Pueblo Bonito, the largest, held roughly 600–650+ rooms (counts vary by whether upper stories are estimated) and rose four stories. Sources: NPS Chaco Culture NHP; [SAH Archipedia](https://sah-archipedia.org/buildings/NM-01-045-0056). Numbers consistent with city-building's verified bank ("over 650 rooms").
- **Mesa Verde Cliff Palace (Colorado).** The largest cliff dwelling in North America: about 150 rooms and 23 kivas, built c. 1190–1280. Source: NPS Mesa Verde (via city-building's verified bank, same numbers; cross-checked [Britannica Kids/NPS-derived summaries](https://kids.britannica.com/students/article/cliff-dwelling/480477)).
- **Framing fact:** these are American landmarks, built by Native nations, standing centuries before any European arrived. No individual builder's name survives from any of them — the honest Zoom 3 is the modern descendants and tribal preservation officers who care for the sites today (story slot kept as target).

### PATCH 2026-09-27 (T-249): the Hopewell earthworks, the Octagon golf course, and Chief Glenna Wallace (fills the era-1 story slot)
- **The Hopewell Ceremonial Earthworks.** "The American Indian people we now call the Hopewell culture" designed and built earthen mounds and earthworks in southern Ohio about 2,000 years ago, "during a short 400-year period" (NPS, [HCE World Heritage inscription 2023](https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/hce-world-heritage-inscription-2023.htm)). The Smithsonian's American Indian Magazine dates them from about A.D. 1 through the next four centuries (John E. Hancock, "Celestial Masterpieces," [American Indian Magazine, Winter 2025/Spring 2026](https://www.americanindianmagazine.org/story/hopewell-ceremonial-earthworks)). A WVXU report gives the Hopewell period as 200 BCE to 500 CE (Tana Weingartner, [Ideastream/WVXU, Aug. 1, 2024](https://www.ideastream.org/2024-08-01/country-club-earthwork-site-settlement-deal)). Record all three spans. "Hopewell" is an archaeologists' name, not the name of a nation (see `religion` outline line 28, same point).
- **The eight sites** (American Indian Magazine): the Octagon Earthworks and the Great Circle Earthwork in Newark, five sites near Chillicothe (Hopewell Mound Group, Seip Earthworks, Mound City, Hopeton Earthworks, High Bank Works), and Fort Ancient in southwest Ohio.
- **World Heritage listing:** on **September 19, 2023**, in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, the UNESCO World Heritage Committee voted unanimously to add the Hopewell Ceremonial Earthworks to the World Heritage List (American Indian Magazine; NPS). The delegation was tribal representatives, NPS staff and Ohio History Connection staff (NPS). "Chief of the Eastern Shawnee Tribe, Glenna Wallace, served as the keynote address during the inscription event" (NPS, its wording). Correction note: Wikipedia's Glenna Wallace article dates the listing September 19, **2024**. That is wrong. NPS, the Smithsonian magazine and the Ohio governor's office give 2023.
- **The Octagon Earthworks, Newark, Ohio:** a large circle joined to an eight-sided enclosure, together enclosing nearly **70 acres**. Moonrise lines up with the walls on a cycle of **18.6 years** (American Indian Magazine). Built about 2,000 years ago (Ohio Supreme Court summary below).
- **Who put a golf course on it, and when:** the Moundbuilders Country Club began leasing the site for golf in **1910**. The Ohio History Connection (then the Ohio Historical Society, a state-chartered nonprofit) has owned the land since **1933** and kept leasing it to the club (Court News Ohio, [State Can Acquire Octagon Earthworks From Country Club, Dec. 7, 2022](https://www.courtnewsohio.gov/cases/2022/SCO/1207/200191.asp)). The American Indian Magazine describes the lease as lasting "nearly a century."
- **The court case:** the Ohio History Connection moved to end the lease by eminent domain (a legal power to take property for public use, with payment). On **December 7, 2022** the Ohio Supreme Court ruled 5-1 that it could. Justice Michael P. Donnelly wrote the majority opinion, Chief Justice Maureen O'Connor and Justices R. Patrick DeWine, Melody Stewart and Jennifer Brunner joined, Justice Patrick F. Fischer concurred in judgment only, and Justice Sharon L. Kennedy dissented (Court News Ohio; opinion 2022-Ohio-4345, [PDF](https://www.supremecourt.ohio.gov/rod/docs/pdf/0/2022/2022-Ohio-4345.pdf)). In April 2024 the court declined to hear the club's further appeal (Ideastream, [Apr. 16, 2024](https://www.ideastream.org/2024-04-16/ohio-supreme-court-earthworks-moundbuilders-ohio-history-connection)). Appraisals disagreed: the Ohio History Connection's experts valued the club at about $2 million, the club's experts at about $22 million (search summary of WYSU/Ideastream coverage, unconfirmed: search summary only). The two sides settled in **August 2024**, terms not disclosed. The club operated through the end of 2024, and public access began **January 1, 2025** (Ideastream/WVXU, Aug. 1, 2024). By October 2025 "the country club was gone" (American Indian Magazine).

Served 18 years on the Eastern Shawnee Business Committee, then was elected chief in **2006**, the tribe's first woman chief ([Glenna Wallace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glenna_Wallace), citing 417 Magazine, Columbus Monthly and others; [Eastern Shawnee Tribe, About Glenna Wallace](https://www.estoo-nsn.gov/history/page/about-glenna-wallace)).

  - Her words when the settlement was announced (Ideastream/WVXU, Aug. 1, 2024): "At last. So many people have worked so long for this to become reality." She told people to "look to the heavens and say to our ancestors, 'We did not forget you.'"

## 03 The 1600s — the oldest survivors

- **Castillo de San Marcos, St. Augustine.** Begun **October 1672**, finished **1695** — the oldest masonry fort in the continental U.S. Built of **coquina**, a soft local stone made of compressed seashells (plain wording: "a stone made of packed seashells"). In the **1702 siege** (Governor James Moore of Carolina, about two months) and the **1740 siege** (General James Oglethorpe of Georgia, 27 days), cannonballs did not shatter the walls — the shell stone crushed where the ball hit and absorbed it, so balls sank in or bounced off. Both sieges failed; the town burned in 1702 but the fort and the people inside it survived. Now Castillo de San Marcos National Monument (NPS). Sources: [NPS Castillo de San Marcos](https://www.nps.gov/casa/), [Atlas Obscura on the coquina walls](https://www.atlasobscura.com/articles/coquina-fort-in-florida), materials study in [Journal of Dynamic Behavior of Materials](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40870-015-0035-1). **Era fix confirmed:** begun 1672 → belongs in the 1600s (the seed's v1 error had it in the 1500s; the correction stays).
  - Labor: built over 23 years by Spanish workers, hired and conscripted laborers, and Native workers; Spanish records document the labor force collectively. No named individual verified this session — story slot stays a target.

### PATCH 2026-09-27 (T-249): who built the Castillo, named and counted (fills the era-3 story slot)

- **Why it was built (cause).** On the night of May 28-29, 1668, about a hundred English pirates, who had seized a Spanish supply ship and used it to fool the harbor pilot, came ashore at midnight and sacked St. Augustine. "More than half a hundred Spaniards were killed" (A). The pirates had sounded the bar and said they meant to return and take the fort (A). Queen Mariana of Spain then named Don Manuel de Cendoya governor and sent him to collect 12,000 pesos from the Viceroy of New Spain to start a stone fort (A). The pirate captain is not named in A. (Other accounts name him as Robert Searles. Not confirmed from an opened source here, so do not name him.)
- **Dates (A and B agree):** first worker drew pay **August 8, 1671** (A). Ground broken about 4 p.m. Sunday **October 2, 1672**, recorded by the public scribe Juan Moreno (A, B). First foundation stone laid by Cendoya **Wednesday, November 9, 1672** (A, B). Main work finished by Governor Juan Cabrera (A's spelling) about 1687 (A: "7 years" after his arrival in fall 1680). Governor Laureano de Torres put on the last stones of the water defenses, **1695-1696**: B says "August 1695", A says "1696". The bank's "finished 1695" stands with this note.
- **Cost (A):** 75,000 pesos by 1680, 92,609 pesos by the main completion, close to 100,000 pesos (A: "approximately $150,000" in 1942 dollars) by 1696.
- **Native labor (forced, and counted).** "Indians from three Nations, the Guale (Georgia), Timucua (eastern Florida) and Apalache (western Florida), were called upon for labor. Some of them had to travel 80 leagues [about 200 miles] to reach the presidio. Many of them served unwillingly." (A). Men had to choose between bringing their families or leaving the women and children in the home villages to feed themselves. "In some cases, not even the chiefs were exempt from the draft." Men were held past their assigned terms, and one chief "was forced to labor on the works for more than 3 years without once returning to his own lands" (A). "Some of the Indians were used as servants by the Governors" (A). The Apalache had been "condemned years before to labor on the fortifications as the penalty for rebellion" (A). Pay: **1 real a day plus maize rations**. A converts 1 real to "12½¢", B to "about 20 cents". Record both. A half dozen Native men became carpenters at 8 reales a day (A). The draft system is called *repartimiento* in Spanish colonial law (not in A or B. Omit the word unless sourced). The count: the job needed 150 laborers for 15 artisans and "it was hard to get as many as 100" (A). At times up to 300 Native people were working for the Crown in St. Augustine, on the fort and in the maize fields (A). In the first winter an epidemic ("El Contagio") struck the Native laborers "and the laboring force dwindled to nothing" (A). A does not count the dead.
- **Enslaved Africans.** "Eighteen black slaves belonging to the crown joined the labor gang in 1687" (B). A mentions "a few of the Crown's Negro slaves" and 30 more promised from Havana who had not arrived in the first winter.
- **Whose land (land rule):** Menéndez landed on September 8, 1565 at **Seloy**, a Timucua town, and the first Spanish settlement was made in Seloy's village (see `research-transportation.md` line 27 and `research-home-family.md` line 31, both sourced). The Castillo stands about a mile south of the Seloy site (transportation bank).
- **Freedom seekers from Carolina (1687).** "Eleven enslaved Africans, eight men, two women and a nursing child, escape from the Carolinas and arrive in St. Augustine. Governor Quiroga grants their request for sanctuary, sees them baptized in the Catholic Church, and hires the men as paid labor at the Castillo de San Marcos." In 1693 King Charles II of Spain decreed freedom for enslaved people escaping British colonies who became Catholic. "Payroll records show that eleven Africans worked as paid labor" by 1695. (NPS, [African Americans in St. Augustine 1565-1821](https://www.nps.gov/casa/learn/historyculture/african-americans-in-st-augustine-1565-1821.htm), punctuation adapted.) Another passage on the same page says "Six of the men were put to work on the new Castillo de San Marcos, but two others were assigned to work with the blacksmith." No names are given. - **Convicts:** convicts from the local garrison or sent from Caribbean ports, for set terms. A's example: a Spaniard caught smuggling English goods, sentenced to 6 years' labor on the fort, the term doubled if he ran (A). The drawbridge was "the work of a convict" (A), unnamed.
- **Spanish skilled men, named (A):** engineer **Ignacio Daza** from Havana, 3 pesos a day, died within a year of arriving. **Lorenzo Lagones**, master of construction, and two master masons at 20 reales a day, 7 masons, 8 stonecutters, a dozen carpenters. **Alonso Díaz**, quarry overseer on Anastasia Island. **Juan Marqués** (A's spelling), Cabrera's master of construction, who found Lagones's errors (a cordon set 3 feet too low on the San Pablo bastion).
- **How the stone was cut (A):** quarries on Anastasia Island, 3 leagues from the town. Workers cut grooves in the coquina with picks and axes, broke blocks loose with bars and wedges, and dug down until the pits reached water. The largest blocks were 2 feet thick and 4 feet long, "that six strong men could hardly lift." Oxen hauled them to a wharf, and rafts ferried them across the bay. Oyster shells burned in kilns made the lime for mortar: 4,000 fanegas (about 7,000 bushels) stored by spring 1672. The walls taper from 14 feet thick at the base to about 9 feet at the top, about 25 feet above the foundation.
- **John Collins, "Juan Calens" (story, ordinary, verified; A and B agree).** An Englishman from the new Carolina colony. In 1670 a ship bound for Charleston put in by accident at the Spanish mission of Santa Catalina, near the Savannah River, and the Spanish captured William Carr and John Rivers. A rescue sloop came from Charleston, and **Joseph Bailey and John Collins** carried a message ashore. The Spanish held them too and sent all four to St. Augustine as prisoners (A, B). Governor Cendoya put the prisoners to work. Three were masons, and the Spanish payrolls list them as Bernardo Patricio (Bernard Patrick), **Juan Calens (John Collins)** and Guillermo Car (William Carr) (A, B). "He could burn more lime in a week than Spanish workmen could in twice the time" (A. B: "than others could in twice the time"). As a prisoner he was paid **8 reales a day instead of the 20 due a master workman** (A). He rose "from master of the kilns to quarry master" (A, B), then ran the dugout boats, the provisions and the convicts, and finally held the office of pilot from St. Augustine to Charleston. The Crown recognized his service after "19 years or more" in the town (A). At least one of the English prisoners married a Florida woman. A does not say which one (A). Collins's birth and death dates, and whether he ever went home, are not in A or B.

## 05 1750 to 1800 — a capital built partly by enslaved hands

- **The President's House (White House): cornerstone October 13, 1792.** Designed by Irish-born architect James Hoban. **The U.S. Capitol: cornerstone September 18, 1793**, laid by George Washington. Sources: [White House Historical Association — Building the White House](https://www.whitehousehistory.org/building-the-white-house), [History.com date entries](https://www.history.com/this-day-in-history/october-13/white-house-cornerstone-laid).
- **Enslaved labor built both — verified fact, say it plainly.** Enslaved people worked at every stage: quarrying Aquia Creek sandstone, cutting timber, making bricks, sawing, carpentry, hauling. The commissioners hired enslaved workers from their owners, who collected the pay. Sources: [WHHA — Did enslaved people build the White House?](https://www.whitehousehistory.org/questions/did-slaves-build-the-white-house), [WHHA — Enslaved labor and the U.S. Capitol](https://www.whitehousehistory.org/enslaved-labor-and-the-construction-of-the-u-s-capitol). Shared with `slavery-freedom` (the system; parked there).
  - **Named people (story, ordinary, verified): Tom, Peter, Ben, Harry, and Daniel** — five enslaved carpenters on the May 1795 President's House payroll. Four were held by architect James Hoban and one by his assistant Pierce Purcell (WHHA's own materials differ on which man Purcell held — state as "four by Hoban, one by Purcell"). The payroll records their days: that month Peter and Ben each worked 28 days; Daniel 25; Harry 9. Hoban signed for their wages. Sources: [WHHA payroll image](https://www.whitehousehistory.org/photos/photo-2-4), [WHHA — Building the President's House with Enslaved Labor](https://www.whitehousehistory.org/building-the-presidents-house-with-enslaved-labor).
- **Monticello.** Jefferson began building **1769**; left it unfinished 1784; redesigned and rebuilt **1796–1809**. Built and largely crafted by enslaved workers. **Mount Vernon** similarly built and expanded with enslaved labor. As landmark houses only — household life is `home-family`. Source: [Monticello house FAQ](https://www.monticello.org/encyclopedia/monticello-house-faq).
- **Thomas Jefferson as builder (story, famous, verified):** designed Monticello himself over 40 years and pushed the classical temple look (Virginia State Capitol, 1785 design) that the new capital's buildings followed. L'Enfant's city plan = `city-building` (their outline features him); the buildings are ours.
- Washington and the capital under construction: the President's House was unfinished when John Adams moved in (November 1800); the Capitol had only one wing. (WHHA, Architect of the Capitol.)

### PATCH 2026-09-27 (T-249): the Capitol payrolls, who was paid for enslaved labor, and how much
Main source, read in full: William C. Allen, Architectural Historian, Office of the Architect of the Capitol, *History of Slave Laborers in the Construction of the United States Capitol*, June 1, 2005, foreword by Richard Baker (Senate Historian) and Kenneth Kato (House history office) ([PDF copy](https://www.chenarch.com/images/arch-texts/2005-Allen-Slavery-Report-to-Congress.pdf)). Written for the congressional task force formed in May 2005.
- **Who hired them and who was paid:** the three commissioners of the District of Columbia rented enslaved men from their owners by the year. Records of payments for "Negro hire" charged to the Capitol run from **February 11, 1795 to May 17, 1801: 385 payments**, the most in 1798. "Negro hire" was the commissioners' term for the yearly rental of an enslaved person. **The pay went to the owner: $60 a year at first, when an unskilled white laborer earned $70.** Later the commissioners raised it to $70 a year (Allen: "raised to $70 in 1797"). Owners were docked for the men's absences.
- **Owners named in the payments (Allen):** they included two of the commissioners themselves, **Gustavus Scott** and **William Thornton** (Thornton also designed the Capitol), the White House architect **James Hoban**, the commissioners' treasurer **William Deakins**, the overseer of Capitol laborers **Middleton Belt**, the developer **Thomas Law**, and many Maryland slaveholders, several of them women (Ann Digges, and Teresa, Mary, Eleanor, Elizabeth and Jane Brent).
- **Enslaved men named in the entries (first names only):** Dick and Tom (Ann Digges, 1795), Nace, Gerrard, David, Charles, Harry, Gabe and Sil (the Brent women, April 2, 1795). "No more than a few slave names are recorded for the period 1795-1801, and virtually nothing is known of their lives" (Allen).
- **Hours:** enslaved sawyers were probably worked seven days a week. The owner got about $5 a month for the six-day week, and the men themselves got small "extra wages" for nights, Sundays and holidays (Allen). The commissioners told overseers to "keep the yearly hirelings at work from sunrise to sunset," six days a week, with housing in "huts" and rations of salt meat and whiskey (Sarah Fling, WHHA, [Enslaved Labor and the Construction of the U.S. Capitol](https://www.whitehousehistory.org/enslaved-labor-and-the-construction-of-the-u-s-capitol)).
- **The quarry:** on December 2, 1791 the commissioners paid $6,000 for a sandstone quarry on Aquia Creek, Stafford County, Virginia (Allen). WHHA: the Government Island quarry was worked by "forty 'stout armed' Negro men" and free workers.
- **Counts:** "No one will ever know how many slaves helped to build the" Capitol (Baker and Kato foreword). WHHA cites historian Bob Arnebeck: at least 200 known enslaved laborers on the Capitol and White House together, owners paid "between $55 and $65 a year."
- **Running away:** Jacob ran from the federal city work in 1793 (owner offered a $6 reward). Daniel Brown ran from Capitol work in November 1827, and his owner Christina Hamilton offered $50 in the *National Intelligencer* (WHHA).
- **A later worker, freed:** Captain **George Pointer**, born enslaved in 1773 in Frederick County, Maryland, bought his freedom at 18 with his earnings. He helped build the Potomac canal and captained a boat that carried Seneca sandstone and Potomac marble to the Capitol (Allen, from Pointer's 1829 petition).
- **What Congress did about it:** the task force's recommendations included naming the Capitol Visitor Center's great hall **Emancipation Hall**, which was done. In 2009 the House Administration Committee reported H. Con. Res. 135, directing the Architect of the Capitol to place a marker there acknowledging slave labor ([H. Rept. 111-153](https://www.congress.gov/committee-report/111th-congress/house-report/153/1)). Members of Congress unveiled the marker on **February 28, 2012**: a single block of Aquia Creek sandstone from the Capitol's 1824-1826 East Front, with a plaque reading "It was quarried by laborers, including enslaved African Americans, and commemorates their important role in building the Capitol" (Architect of the Capitol, [Slave Labor Commemorative Marker](https://www.aoc.gov/explore-capitol-campus/art/slave-labor-commemorative-marker)). This belongs to era 10 as a landmark fact.

## 06 1800 to 1850 — the country starts building monuments to itself (thin era)

- **John Hemmings (story, ordinary, verified).** Enslaved joiner at Monticello (1776–1833): trained under hired white joiners from age 17, made the fine woodwork of Monticello's remodeled interiors and much of its furniture, and led the joinery at Jefferson's Poplar Forest retreat. Freed by Jefferson's will in **1826** — one of only five people freed — and given the tools of the joiner's shop. Died 1833. Sources: [Monticello — John Hemmings](https://www.monticello.org/site/research-and-collections/john-hemmings), [Monticello joinery pages](https://www.monticello.org/slavery/the-plantation/work-on-the-monticello-plantation/joining-and-woodworking/). (His main documented work falls 1800s–1826, so the story sits in this era.)
- **Bunker Hill Monument, Charlestown, Massachusetts.** Cornerstone June 17, 1825 (Lafayette present; Daniel Webster spoke); money ran out repeatedly; a women's fair organized by Sarah Josepha Hale in 1840 raised $30,000 of the cost; the 221-foot granite obelisk was completed 1842 and dedicated June 17, 1843. Sources: [NPS — Building the Bunker Hill Monument](https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/building-the-bunker-hill-monument.htm), [Mass Moments](https://www.massmoments.org/moment-details/bostonians-lay-cornerstone-for-bunker-hill-monument.html).
- **Washington Monument begun: cornerstone July 4, 1848** (Washington National Monument Society; about 20,000 attended). Work stopped **1854** at about **150 feet** when money ran out — and politics finished the stop: in March 1854 members of the anti-Catholic **Know-Nothing** movement stole the block of marble donated by Pope Pius IX (the "Pope stone") and, by the usual account, threw it in the Potomac — it was never found; Know-Nothings then seized control of the monument society (1855–58). The stub stood for over two decades. Sources: [National Geographic](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/travel/article/washington-monument-construction-know-nothings-elevator), [NPS Washington Monument history](https://www.nps.gov/wamo/index.htm), [EBSCO research starter](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/construction-washington-monument-begins). Completion is the next era's story.
- **Smithsonian Institution Building ("the Castle"),** red sandstone, designed by James Renwick Jr.; cornerstone May 1, 1847, built 1847–1855. Source: [Smithsonian Institution Archives](https://siarchives.si.edu/blog/smithsonian-castle-construction-begins).

## 07 1850 to 1900 — iron, steel, and stone

- **Statue of Freedom / Philip Reid (story, ordinary, verified).** The bronze figure atop the new Capitol dome was cast 1860–62 at Clark Mills' foundry by a crew that included **Philip Reid**, an enslaved craftsman born c. 1820 in Charleston. When the Italian sculptor-foreman demanded more pay to reveal how to separate the plaster model's sections, Reid worked out how to do it with a pulley and tackle. He was paid $1.25 a day — but only Sundays were his; his owner collected the other six days. Reid was freed April 16, 1862 by the D.C. emancipation act — **a free man before the statue was set on the dome December 2, 1863**. Sources: [Architect of the Capitol](https://www.aoc.gov/explore-capitol-campus/blog/philip-reid-and-statue-freedom), [U.S. Senate historical office](https://www.senate.gov/artandhistory/history/common/generic/Civil_War_ReidPhilip_StatueofFreedom.htm), [WHHA](https://www.whitehousehistory.org/philip-reed). (He later signed his name "Reed.")
- **Brooklyn Bridge, opened May 24, 1883.** The Roebling story, verified:
  - **John A. Roebling** designed it; in June 1869, while surveying the tower site, a ferry crushed his foot against the dock pilings; he refused conventional treatment and died of tetanus weeks later, before construction began.
  - **Washington Roebling**, his son, took over as chief engineer; working in the compressed-air caissons he was wrecked by **caisson disease (decompression sickness — "the bends")**, collapsing in 1872; partially paralyzed, he directed the work for the next decade from his Brooklyn Heights room, watching through a telescope.
  - **Emily Warren Roebling** — told honestly: Washington kept the chief engineer's title and made the engineering decisions from his sickroom; Emily became his messenger, aide, and public face for 11 years — she studied stress analysis, cable construction, and bridge mathematics to carry accurate instructions to the engineers and answer their questions, and she defended Washington before the trustees when they tried to remove him. Some writers now call her the "effective chief engineer"; the honest statement is that the bridge could not have been finished the way it was without her, and the engineers dealt with her daily. She was the first to cross by carriage, carrying a live rooster as a sign of victory. Sources: [History.com](https://www.history.com/articles/brooklyn-bridge-emily-roebling), [Emily Warren Roebling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emily_Warren_Roebling), [MCNY blog](https://blog.mcny.org/2012/09/11/the-curse-of-the-roeblings-the-construction-of-the-brooklyn-bridge/).
  - **Movie (real, verified): *Brooklyn Bridge* (1981)** — Ken Burns' first documentary, narrated by David McCullough; nominated for the Academy Award for Best Documentary Feature. Sources: [PBS](https://www.pbs.org/kenburns/brooklyn-bridge), [IMDb awards page](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt0082106/awards/). About the bridge (and the Roeblings), not a biography.
  - About 20–30 workers died building it (accounts vary); caisson disease crippled many. (MCNY; numbers vary by source — say "at least 20".)
- **Washington Monument finished.** Congress took over 1876; the Army Corps of Engineers (Lt. Col. Thomas Lincoln Casey) restarted work 1879; **completed December 6, 1884** with a **100-ounce cast aluminum tip** — aluminum was then a showpiece metal at about $1 an ounce (roughly a day's wage for a laborer); the whole tip cost $225. Dedicated February 21, 1885. At 555 feet it was the tallest structure in the world until the Eiffel Tower (1889). **The color line:** about 150 feet up, the marble visibly changes shade — the post-1879 courses came from different quarry beds than the 1848–54 stone. The stub-and-restart story is written in the stone. Sources: [TMS/JOM history of the aluminum cap](https://www.tms.org/pubs/journals/jom/9511/binczewski-9511.html), [NPS — The Point of the Matter](https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/wamocap.htm), [National Geographic](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/travel/article/washington-monument-construction-know-nothings-elevator).
- **Statue of Liberty, dedicated October 28, 1886** (President Cleveland presiding). Verified chain: proposed by Édouard de Laboulaye (1865); sculpted by **Frédéric Auguste Bartholdi**; the completed **arm and torch were exhibited at the 1876 Centennial Exhibition in Philadelphia**, then stood in Madison Square Park, New York, 1877–1882 to raise pedestal money; the internal iron skeleton was engineered by **Gustave Eiffel** (joined 1879) — the copper skin is 3/32 inch thick, "about the thickness of two pennies placed together" (NPS's own comparison), hung on an iron frame; the head and shoulders were shown at the **1878 Paris Universal Exposition**; the finished statue crossed the Atlantic aboard the French ship Isère in **350 pieces packed in 214 crates**, arriving June 17, 1885 ([Smithsonian Magazine](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/statue-liberty-arrived-350-pieces-180955920/), [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/How-Was-the-Statue-of-Liberty-Transported)); France paid for the statue, America owed the pedestal — and pedestal money stalled until **Joseph Pulitzer's New York World campaign (1885)** raised over $100,000 from about **120,000 donors**, most giving under a dollar, many of them schoolchildren's coins, every donor's name printed in the paper. Statue 151 feet; with pedestal about 305 feet. Declared a National Monument 1924 (Coolidge). Sources: [statueofliberty.org history](https://www.statueofliberty.org/statue-of-liberty/overview-history/), [History.com](https://www.history.com/articles/statue-of-liberty), [National Archives Prologue blog](https://prologue.blogs.archives.gov/2024/10/14/body-of-iron-soul-of-fire-the-statue-of-liberty/), [NPS](https://www.nps.gov/stli/). **Emma Lazarus's "The New Colossus"** (1883 fundraising sonnet) was mounted on a pedestal plaque in **1903** (VERIFIED in Ch2) — the immigrant-welcome meaning is `immigration`'s story; ours is the building of it.
- **Biltmore House, Asheville, North Carolina, 1889–1895** — George W. Vanderbilt's 250-room house, about 179,000 square feet, still the largest privately owned house in the U.S. Opened to family Christmas Eve 1895. Source: [Biltmore's own history](https://www.biltmore.com/blog/10-fast-facts-about-biltmore/), [Biltmore Estate overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biltmore_Estate).

### PATCH 2026-09-27 (T-249): Native mounds destroyed by builders, the St. Louis case (land and destruction)
- **"The Mound City."** St. Louis was built on a group of Mississippian mounds. Counts differ by source and area: about **40** mounds in the city, built A.D. 600-1300 (Wikipedia, [Sugarloaf Mound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sugarloaf_Mound), citing the National Register nomination). A popular history counts 27 on the riverfront (Distilled History, no sources cited). Record all.
- **Big Mound**, the largest, about 30-34 feet high and 150-319 feet long by various accounts, "was finally destroyed completely in 1869 and its fill ... sold to the North Missouri Railroad to build track alignments." Workers removing it found **thirty-two burials in two chambers** (Weil, Landmarks Association). The final digging began in November 1868 for railroad fill (search summaries of the St. Louis Post-Dispatch "A Look Back" and Wikipedia, unconfirmed: search summary only). "Very little (if any) archaeological work was done during its demolition" (Distilled History). The records here do not name who dug it out or who sold the fill.
- **1904:** before the World's Fair in Forest Park, "two groups of mounds (totaling 16 in all) were destroyed along the River des Peres" (Weil, Landmarks Association). The fair's builders are not named.
- **Sugarloaf Mound**, on the south side of the city, is the only mound left in St. Louis. It was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1984 (Weil). Someone built a house on its top in **1928**. The **Osage Nation** bought that house and about a third of the mound in **2009**, demolished the house in **2017**, and with the arts group Counterpublic got the remaining two properties transferred to the Nation in **2025** (Osage Nation, ["Osage Nation Reacquires Sugarloaf Mound"](https://www.osagenation-nsn.gov/news-events/news/osage-nation-reacquires-sugarloaf-mound-sacred-osage-site-and-oldest-human-made), Sept. 22, 2025). **Dr. Andrea Hunter**, the Osage Nation Historic Preservation Officer: "It has been a long 17-year journey, and I am thrilled to finally see the entire mound come under Osage Nation control." ## 08 1900 to 1950 — the giant decades

- **Lincoln Memorial, dedicated May 30, 1922.** Architect Henry Bacon; the seated Lincoln by sculptor Daniel Chester French. Hard truth from day one: the dedication crowd was segregated, and the only Black speaker, Robert Russa Moton of Tuskegee, had his speech text vetted. On Easter Sunday, **April 9, 1939**, after the DAR barred her from Constitution Hall, **Marian Anderson** sang from its steps to about 75,000 people — the concert story belongs to `rights-movements`; the memorial-as-stage is ours. Sources: [History.com dedication entry](https://www.history.com/this-day-in-history/may-30/former-president-taft-dedicates-lincoln-memorial), [Smithsonian Music on Anderson](https://music.si.edu/story/marian-anderson-and-concert-lincoln-memorial), [National Archives blog](https://rediscovering-black-history.blogs.archives.gov/2014/05/20/marian-anderson-and-the-easter-sunday-concert-april-9-1939/).
- **Mount Rushmore, carved October 1927 – October 31, 1941,** by about 400 workers under sculptor **Gutzon Borglum** (died March 1941; his son Lincoln finished the season). Told honestly, both halves:
  - The mountain is **Tunkasila Sakpe, the Six Grandfathers**, in the Black Hills — land guaranteed to the Lakota by the **1868 Fort Laramie Treaty** and taken after the 1874 gold rush; in **United States v. Sioux Nation (1980)** the Supreme Court ruled the taking violated the treaty and awarded compensation; **the Lakota have refused the money** (now over $1 billion with interest), holding that the land was never for sale. Sources: [Smithsonian Magazine](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/sordid-history-mount-rushmore-180960446/), [U.S. v. Sioux Nation, 448 U.S. 371 (1980)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/448/371/), [National Archives — Fort Laramie Treaty](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/fort-laramie-treaty).
  - **Borglum before Rushmore** carved the Confederate memorial at **Stone Mountain, Georgia** (1915–1925), a project tied up with the revived Ku Klux Klan, which helped fund it; historians document that Borglum attended Klan meetings and was deeply involved in Klan politics, though no membership record is proven. Say both plainly. Sources: [Washington Post history desk](https://www.washingtonpost.com/history/2020/07/03/mount-rushmore-gutzon-borglum-klan-stone-mountain/), [Smithsonian Magazine](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/sordid-history-mount-rushmore-180960446/).
  - Carving facts: 90% of the "carving" was dynamite; about 450,000 tons of rock removed; **no worker died** on the project (NPS). Workers earned by the hour in Depression years and were glad of the jobs.
  - **Nick Clifford (story, ordinary, verified):** Donald "Nick" Clifford, hired 1938 at 17 — the youngest Rushmore worker — partly because the Borglums wanted him for the crew's baseball team (he pitched). Earned 55 cents an hour; ran the winch that carried men up the face and drilled dynamite holes. Worked to 1940; last surviving carver; died November 23, 2019, at 98. Sources: [NPR obituary](https://www.npr.org/2019/11/26/783091531/last-living-mount-rushmore-carver-dies-at-98), [Mount Rushmore Society](https://mountrushmoresociety.com/2019/11/honoring-nick-clifford/).
- **Empire State Building, opened May 1, 1931.** Built in about **410 days** from first steel to opening — up to 3,400 workers a day, the frame rising about 4.5 floors a week during the push. **Five workers died** — official count; the "hundreds" of legend is false. 102 stories, 1,250 feet (1,454 to today's antenna tip); world's tallest building until 1971 (World Trade Center). **Lewis Hine** photographed the "sky boys" for his book *Men at Work* (1931–32) — the photos are `work-workers`' shared thread. Among the riveting gangs were **Mohawk ironworkers from Kahnawake**, continuing a high-steel tradition dating from the 1880s — Native builders on the tallest thing in the world (Native continuity past 1900; the full story lands with Harold McComber in the next era). Sources: [History.com construction article](https://www.history.com/articles/empire-state-building-construction), [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Empire-State-Building).
- **Golden Gate Bridge, opened May 27–28, 1937** (200,000 walked it on Pedestrian Day, May 27). Chief engineer **Joseph Strauss**; **credit dispute stated honestly:** structural engineer **Charles Ellis** did the detailed design and thousands of calculations in 1929–31, was fired by Strauss before construction, and got no credit for decades — in 2007 the bridge district's own report finally gave Ellis principal design credit. Strauss's real distinction on the job: **safety.** He required hard hats and spent about $130,000 on the first big **safety net** in American bridge-building — it saved **19 men**, who called themselves the **"Halfway to Hell Club."** Eleven men died in all: one in October 1936, then **ten on February 17, 1937**, when a falling work platform tore through the net. Sources: [Half Way to Hell Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half_Way_to_Hell_Club), [Linda Hall Library on Ellis](https://www.lindahall.org/about/news/scientist-of-the-day/charles-alton-ellis/), [History News Network on the 2007 credit report](https://www.historynewsnetwork.org/article/design-credit-for-golden-gate-bridge-reassigned), Golden Gate Bridge District history pages.
  - **Al Zampa (story, ordinary, verified):** Alfred Zampa, ironworker from Crockett, California. On October 19, 1936 he slipped on wet steel and fell into the net — but near the shore the net sagged to the rocks, and he broke four bones in his back. Twelve weeks in the hospital, two years in a back brace — then back to ironwork, a founding member of the Halfway to Hell Club and later its unofficial spokesman. In **2003** the new Carquinez Strait span was named the **Alfred Zampa Memorial Bridge** — named for a working ironworker, not a politician. Sources: [Al Zampa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al_Zampa), [Alfred Zampa Memorial Bridge Foundation](https://www.alzampabridge.com/the-man), [California SCR-97](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billTextClient.xhtml?bill_id=199920000SCR97).
- **Hoover Dam, dedicated September 30, 1935** (FDR); generators running 1936. **The deaths, honestly:** the Bureau of Reclamation's official count is **96 industrial fatalities** (falls, rock slides, equipment, drowning); it excludes deaths recorded as "pneumonia," which many historians believe covered carbon-monoxide poisoning of tunnel crews (42 such deaths) — so the true toll is higher than 96 and the exact number is not known. **Debunk plainly: nobody is buried in the concrete.** The dam was poured as many small blocks in shallow lifts, with crews working over each pour — a body could not have been left in it. Sources: [Bureau of Reclamation — Fatalities at Hoover Dam](https://www.usbr.gov/lc/hooverdam/history/essays/fatal.html).

### PATCH 2026-09-27 (T-249): Mount Rushmore, the Black Hills taking with its actors, and Borglum
- **Carving dates and crew (NPS):** "October 4, 1927 - October 31, 1941." "Nearly 400 men and women" worked on it, from call boys to drillers to blacksmiths. "During the 14 years of construction not one fatality occurred." Workers went down the faces in bosun chairs (a seat hung on a cable). "90% of the mountain was carved using dynamite," then workers drilled close rows of holes ("honeycombing") and smoothed the granite with hand tools. One worker's wage is given as $8.00 a day (NPS, [Carving History](https://www.nps.gov/moru/learn/historyculture/carving-history.htm)).
- **Method:** Borglum's studio model was 1/12 the size of the finished faces. Workers used a "pointing machine" (a measuring arm with a plumb line) to carry each measurement from the model to the mountain at 12 times the scale (search summaries of SDPB and Travel South Dakota, unconfirmed: search summary only).
- **Cost:** $989,992.32 in all, about $836,000 of it federal (search summary, unconfirmed: search summary only).
- **Borglum at Stone Mountain (NPS):** the design was Lee, Stonewall Jackson and Jefferson Davis on horseback with a column of soldiers. Work began in 1923, and "the head of Lee was unveiled in 1924." "Trouble had been brewing between Borglum and the businessmen directing the project, and Borglum was abruptly dismissed." He then "destroyed his models in order to protect his design and this so angered the directors that a warrant was issued for his arrest and he was forced to flee Georgia." He died in Chicago after surgery on **March 6, 1941**, "several days before his 74th birthday," and his son **Lincoln Borglum** finished the work (NPS, [Gutzon Borglum](https://www.nps.gov/moru/learn/historyculture/gutzon-borglum.htm)). - **Who took the Black Hills, and how (U.S. v. Sioux Nation, 448 U.S. 371, decided June 30, 1980, opinion by Justice Harry Blackmun, Justice William Rehnquist dissenting, [Cornell LII text](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/448/371)):**
  - The 1868 Fort Laramie Treaty set the Great Sioux Reservation, including the Black Hills, "apart for the absolute and undisturbed use and occupation" of the Sioux, and said no cession was valid unless signed by at least three-fourths of the adult Sioux men.
  - **July 2, 1874:** Lt. Col. **George Armstrong Custer** led close to 1,000 soldiers and teamsters into the Black Hills, sent by Lt. Gen. Philip Sheridan. By mid-August they had confirmed gold.
  - **August 1876:** members of Congress passed an appropriations bill saying "hereafter there shall be no appropriation made for the subsistence" of the Sioux unless they gave up the Black Hills. That is, officials would stop the food rations the Sioux then lived on until they signed.
  - **September 1876:** a commission headed by **George Manypenny** brought a treaty already written. The commissioners told the Sioux the United States no longer owed them rations. The "agreement" was "signed by only 10% of the adult male Sioux population," not the three-fourths the treaty required.
  - **February 28, 1877:** members of Congress passed that agreement into law anyway (19 Stat. 254). This ended the 1868 treaty's promise and took the Black Hills.
  - The Indian Claims Commission set the land's 1877 value at **$17.1 million**, plus $450,000 for gold prospectors took before 1877. The Court of Claims added **5 percent a year interest from 1877**, and the Supreme Court affirmed. The total award in 1980 is commonly given as about $102 million to $106 million (ICT: "$102 million"). The Sioux nations have not taken it.
  - **Value today (perishable, 2026):** the fund is held by the US Interior Department's Bureau of Trust Funds Administration. Its current balance is sealed. The Oglala Sioux Tribe opposed a CNN records request to reveal it. Oglala Sioux president **Frank Star Comes Out**: "We will never sell out our holy lands, the Black Hills, to the United States for monetary compensation." ([ICT, "Black Hills still not for sale"](https://ictnews.org/news/black-hills-still-not-for-sale-oglala-sioux-tribe-rejects-foia-request-to-unseal-value/), 2025, date not on fetched text). Published estimates: "over $1 billion" (ICT, speculation), "$1.3 billion" (PBS NewsHour headline, 2011), "over $1.8 billion" (search summary, unconfirmed: search summary only). Prose should say "more than $1 billion, by published estimates. The exact figure is sealed."
  - The memorial now consults with **21 associated tribal nations** (NPS, [Associated Tribal Nations](https://www.nps.gov/moru/learn/historyculture/the-associated-tribal-nations-of-mount-rushmore-national-memorial.htm)).
  - Shared with `native-nations` (lead on the treaty, the war of 1876-77 and the claim). Their bank lines 155-156 and 477 hold the same core facts. The actors above are for the landmark cell.

### PATCH 2026-09-27 (T-249): the Quebec Bridge deaths and the Kahnawake women's rule (sources the Harold McComber story)
- On **August 29, 1907**, the south half of the Quebec Bridge, then being built across the St. Lawrence River, fell. **The Canadian Encyclopedia** gives **75** workers killed (James H. Marsh, ["Quebec Bridge Disaster"](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/quebec-bridge-disaster-feature)). The Kahnawake newspaper *The Eastern Door* gives **76** (Eve Cable, ["Remembering the Quebec Bridge disaster"](https://easterndoor.com/article/remembering-the-quebec-bridge-disaster), Aug. 31, 2023). Record both. **Thirty-three of the dead were Kahnawake men** (The Eastern Door). A Royal Commission blamed the design errors of the consulting engineer **Theodore Cooper**, who had lengthened the span to save money, and chief engineer **John Deans**, who kept the crews working after Cooper's warning (Canadian Encyclopedia). The Canadian Encyclopedia notes many of the dead were "local Caughnawaga [Kahnawake], famous for their high steel work."
- **What the community did:** afterward the women of Kahnawake "made sure that their families were not all working on the same job at any one time" ("If a lady had seven sons, they're not all going to the same job"). Crosses made from the bridge's steel stand in the village cemetery. In 2007 a monument with the image of each man killed and a 40-foot steel replica of the bridge went up (The Eastern Door).
