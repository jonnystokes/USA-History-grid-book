## 01 Before 1500 — the landmarks were already here

- **Monks Mound, Cahokia (Illinois).** The largest earthen structure in the Americas: about 100 feet tall, base of about **14 acres** — a bigger footprint than the Great Pyramid of Giza's (about 13 acres). The comparison is base area, not height or volume: the Great Pyramid is far taller. Built in stages, mostly c. 1050–1200. Sources: Cahokia Mounds State Historic Site / [National Geographic](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/history/history-magazine/article/this-american-indian-metropolis-was-mysteriously-abandoned-archaeologists-want-to-know-why); base-vs-base figures cross-checked ([Monks Mound overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monks_Mound)). City-building's verified bank carries the same numbers (100 ft, ~14 acres). Cahokia as a city and nation = `native-nations` (lead) and `city-building`; the structure itself is ours.
- **Serpent Mound (Ohio).** Quarter-mile-long earthen snake effigy, the largest serpent effigy in the world. **Builder dispute — state honestly:** no artifacts inside the mound identify its builders. 1991 radiocarbon tests pointed to the Fort Ancient culture (c. 1070 AD); 2014 tests pointed to the Adena culture (c. 300 BC); later work supports both, so archaeologists now consider it possible the mound was built and rebuilt in more than one period. Sources: [Ohio History Connection — Who Built Serpent Mound?](https://www.ohiohistory.org/who-built-serpent-mound/), [Ohio History Connection site page](https://www.ohiohistory.org/visit/browse-historical-sites/serpent-mound/).
- **Poverty Point (Louisiana).** Mound-and-ridge complex built **c. 1700–1100 BC** by hunter-fisher-gatherers — five mounds, six concentric earthen ridges, a central plaza. The largest earthwork complex of its age north of Mexico. Named a **UNESCO World Heritage Site June 22, 2014**. Sources: [Louisiana state parks](https://www.lastateparks.com/historic-sites/poverty-point-world-heritage-site), [UNESCO/State Dept. announcement](https://2009-2017.state.gov/r/pa/prs/ps/2014/06/228265.htm). Clears the seed's [VERIFY].
- **Chaco Canyon great houses (New Mexico), c. 850–1150.** Pueblo Bonito, the largest, held roughly 600–650+ rooms (counts vary by whether upper stories are estimated) and rose four stories. Sources: NPS Chaco Culture NHP; [SAH Archipedia](https://sah-archipedia.org/buildings/NM-01-045-0056). Numbers consistent with city-building's verified bank ("over 650 rooms").
- **Mesa Verde Cliff Palace (Colorado).** The largest cliff dwelling in North America: about 150 rooms and 23 kivas, built c. 1190–1280. Source: NPS Mesa Verde (via city-building's verified bank, same numbers; cross-checked [Britannica Kids/NPS-derived summaries](https://kids.britannica.com/students/article/cliff-dwelling/480477)).
- **Framing fact:** these are American landmarks, built by Native nations, standing centuries before any European arrived. No individual builder's name survives from any of them — the honest Zoom 3 is the modern descendants and tribal preservation officers who care for the sites today (story slot kept as target).

## 02 The 1500s — thin, honestly

- **St. Augustine, Florida, founded September 1565** by Pedro Menéndez de Avilés — the oldest continuously occupied European-founded city in the continental U.S. (VERIFIED in Ch2 and in city-building's bank.) Nothing the newcomers built in this century survives above ground: the early forts were wood and earth, burned and rebuilt repeatedly (Francis Drake burned the town in 1586). The era's "landmark" is the founding site itself; the stone fort comes in the 1600s.
- Spanish mission buildings of this century in Florida and the Southwest survive only as archaeology.

## 03 The 1600s — the oldest survivors

- **Castillo de San Marcos, St. Augustine.** Begun **October 1672**, finished **1695** — the oldest masonry fort in the continental U.S. Built of **coquina**, a soft local stone made of compressed seashells (plain wording: "a stone made of packed seashells"). In the **1702 siege** (Governor James Moore of Carolina, about two months) and the **1740 siege** (General James Oglethorpe of Georgia, 27 days), cannonballs did not shatter the walls — the shell stone crushed where the ball hit and absorbed it, so balls sank in or bounced off. Both sieges failed; the town burned in 1702 but the fort and the people inside it survived. Now Castillo de San Marcos National Monument (NPS). Sources: [NPS Castillo de San Marcos](https://www.nps.gov/casa/), [Atlas Obscura on the coquina walls](https://www.atlasobscura.com/articles/coquina-fort-in-florida), materials study in [Journal of Dynamic Behavior of Materials](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40870-015-0035-1). **Era fix confirmed:** begun 1672 → belongs in the 1600s (the seed's v1 error had it in the 1500s; the correction stays).
  - Labor: built over 23 years by Spanish workers, hired and conscripted laborers, and Native workers; Spanish records document the labor force collectively. No named individual verified this session — story slot stays a target.
- **San Miguel Chapel, Santa Fe.** Adobe church built c. **1610–1626** by Tlaxcalan workers from Mexico in the Barrio de Analco. Burned in the **1680 Pueblo Revolt**; rebuilt **1710** on the old foundations. **"Oldest church" claim, told honestly:** it is often called the oldest church building in the continental U.S.; its foundations and site are that old, but the standing structure is substantially the 1710 rebuild. Sources: [NPS](https://www.nps.gov/places/san-miguel-chapel-in-santa-fe.htm), [Historic Santa Fe Foundation](https://www.historicsantafe.org/san-miguel-chapel).
- **Old Ship Meetinghouse, Hingham, Massachusetts, 1681.** The only surviving 17th-century Puritan meetinghouse in the country, and the oldest church building in continuous use in the U.S. Its open timber roof frame reminded the congregation of a ship's hull upside down — hence the name. Sources: [Hingham Historical Society](https://hinghamhistorical.org/project/old-ship-meeting-house-1681/), [Old Ship Church history](https://oldshipchurch.org/history/).
- (Palace of the Governors, Santa Fe, c. 1610 — oldest continuously occupied public building — is told by `city-building`; one-line mention at most here.)

## 04 1700 to 1750 — the first public buildings meant to last

- **Old North Church (Christ Church), Boston, 1723** — the oldest standing church building in Boston; steeple completed 1740 (191 feet). Founding date is this era; its fame — sexton Robert Newman and Capt. John Pulling Jr. hanging **two lanterns on April 18, 1775** to signal that British troops were moving by water — belongs to the next era's events and is `war`/`news-communication` material; we note the building carried the signal. Sources: [Old North Church history](https://oldnorth.com/history-education/), NPS Boston NHP.
- **Boston Light, Little Brewster Island, 1716** — the first lighthouse in what became the U.S.; first lit September 16, 1716. Blown up by the British in 1776, rebuilt 1783 (that rebuild is the tower standing today — say so). Sources: [NPS Boston Light](https://www.nps.gov/places/boston-light.htm), [Massachusetts Moments](https://www.massmoments.org/moment-details/boston-lighthouse-lit-for-the-first-time.html). Clears the seed's [VERIFY].
  - **George Worthylake, first keeper (story, ordinary, verified):** appointed 1716; on November 3, 1718 he drowned with his wife Ann and daughter Ruth when their canoe capsized on the way back to the island; his enslaved man Shadwell and two others also drowned. Twelve-year-old Benjamin Franklin wrote and hawked a street ballad about it, "The Lighthouse Tragedy" — Franklin himself later called it "wretched stuff," and no copy survives. Days later, replacement keeper Robert Saunders also drowned. Sources: [Founders Archives (Franklin's autobiography note)](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Franklin/01-01-02-0006), [New England Historical Society](https://newenglandhistoricalsociety.com/cursed-sentinel-of-boston-harbor-the-1st-boston-light/), [George Worthylake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Worthylake). No film.
- **Independence Hall (Pennsylvania State House), Philadelphia.** Construction began **1732**, finished **1753** — 21 years; designed by Andrew Hamilton with master carpenter Edmund Woolley; tower built 1750–53, and the bell inscribed "Proclaim liberty throughout all the land" hung in 1753. What happened inside (1776, 1787) belongs to `government-politics`; the building is ours. Sources: [NPS Independence NHP](https://www.nps.gov/inde/), [ushistory.org](https://www.ushistory.org/tour/independence-hall.htm), [Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia](https://philadelphiaencyclopedia.org/essays/independence-hall/). Clears the seed's [VERIFY].
- **The Alamo (Mission San Antonio de Valero), San Antonio.** Mission founded **1718**; stone church cornerstone laid **May 8, 1744**; that church collapsed in the 1750s; the present church (the famous facade) was begun **1758** and never finished by the missionaries — the planned bell towers and dome were never built. The 1836 battle is `war`'s; the building is ours. Sources: [Texas State Historical Association](https://www.tshaonline.org/handbook/entries/san-antonio-de-valero-mission), [NPS Spanish missions itinerary](https://www.nps.gov/subjects/travelspanishmissions/mission-san-antonio-de-valero-the-alamo.htm).

## 05 1750 to 1800 — a capital built partly by enslaved hands

- **The President's House (White House): cornerstone October 13, 1792.** Designed by Irish-born architect James Hoban. **The U.S. Capitol: cornerstone September 18, 1793**, laid by George Washington. Sources: [White House Historical Association — Building the White House](https://www.whitehousehistory.org/building-the-white-house), [History.com date entries](https://www.history.com/this-day-in-history/october-13/white-house-cornerstone-laid).
- **Enslaved labor built both — verified fact, say it plainly.** Enslaved people worked at every stage: quarrying Aquia Creek sandstone, cutting timber, making bricks, sawing, carpentry, hauling. The commissioners hired enslaved workers from their owners, who collected the pay. Sources: [WHHA — Did enslaved people build the White House?](https://www.whitehousehistory.org/questions/did-slaves-build-the-white-house), [WHHA — Enslaved labor and the U.S. Capitol](https://www.whitehousehistory.org/enslaved-labor-and-the-construction-of-the-u-s-capitol). Shared with `slavery-freedom` (the system; parked there).
  - **Named people (story, ordinary, verified): Tom, Peter, Ben, Harry, and Daniel** — five enslaved carpenters on the May 1795 President's House payroll. Four were held by architect James Hoban and one by his assistant Pierce Purcell (WHHA's own materials differ on which man Purcell held — state as "four by Hoban, one by Purcell"). The payroll records their days: that month Peter and Ben each worked 28 days; Daniel 25; Harry 9. Hoban signed for their wages. Sources: [WHHA payroll image](https://www.whitehousehistory.org/photos/photo-2-4), [WHHA — Building the President's House with Enslaved Labor](https://www.whitehousehistory.org/building-the-presidents-house-with-enslaved-labor).
- **Monticello.** Jefferson began building **1769**; left it unfinished 1784; redesigned and rebuilt **1796–1809**. Built and largely crafted by enslaved workers. **Mount Vernon** similarly built and expanded with enslaved labor. As landmark houses only — household life is `home-family`. Source: [Monticello house FAQ](https://www.monticello.org/encyclopedia/monticello-house-faq).
- **Thomas Jefferson as builder (story, famous, verified):** designed Monticello himself over 40 years and pushed the classical temple look (Virginia State Capitol, 1785 design) that the new capital's buildings followed. L'Enfant's city plan = `city-building` (their outline features him); the buildings are ours.
- Washington and the capital under construction: the President's House was unfinished when John Adams moved in (November 1800); the Capitol had only one wing. (WHHA, Architect of the Capitol.)

## 06 1800 to 1850 — the country starts building monuments to itself (thin era)

- **John Hemmings (story, ordinary, verified).** Enslaved joiner at Monticello (1776–1833): trained under hired white joiners from age 17, made the fine woodwork of Monticello's remodeled interiors and much of its furniture, and led the joinery at Jefferson's Poplar Forest retreat. Freed by Jefferson's will in **1826** — one of only five people freed — and given the tools of the joiner's shop. Died 1833. Sources: [Monticello — John Hemmings](https://www.monticello.org/site/research-and-collections/john-hemmings), [Monticello joinery pages](https://www.monticello.org/slavery/the-plantation/work-on-the-monticello-plantation/joining-and-woodworking/). (His main documented work falls 1800s–1826, so the story sits in this era.)
- **Bunker Hill Monument, Charlestown, Massachusetts.** Cornerstone June 17, 1825 (Lafayette present; Daniel Webster spoke); money ran out repeatedly; a women's fair organized by Sarah Josepha Hale in 1840 raised $30,000 of the cost; the 221-foot granite obelisk was completed 1842 and dedicated June 17, 1843. Sources: [NPS — Building the Bunker Hill Monument](https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/building-the-bunker-hill-monument.htm), [Mass Moments](https://www.massmoments.org/moment-details/bostonians-lay-cornerstone-for-bunker-hill-monument.html).
- **Washington Monument begun: cornerstone July 4, 1848** (Washington National Monument Society; about 20,000 attended). Work stopped **1854** at about **150 feet** when money ran out — and politics finished the stop: in March 1854 members of the anti-Catholic **Know-Nothing** movement stole the block of marble donated by Pope Pius IX (the "Pope stone") and, by the usual account, threw it in the Potomac — it was never found; Know-Nothings then seized control of the monument society (1855–58). The stub stood for over two decades. Sources: [National Geographic](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/travel/article/washington-monument-construction-know-nothings-elevator), [NPS Washington Monument history](https://www.nps.gov/wamo/index.htm), [EBSCO research starter](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/construction-washington-monument-begins). Completion is the next era's story.
- **Smithsonian Institution Building ("the Castle"),** red sandstone, designed by James Renwick Jr.; cornerstone May 1, 1847, built 1847–1855. Source: [Smithsonian Institution Archives](https://siarchives.si.edu/blog/smithsonian-castle-construction-begins).

## 07 1850 to 1900 — iron, steel, and stone

- **Statue of Freedom / Philip Reid (story, ordinary, verified).** The bronze figure atop the new Capitol dome was cast 1860–62 at Clark Mills' foundry by a crew that included **Philip Reid**, an enslaved craftsman born c. 1820 in Charleston. When the Italian sculptor-foreman demanded more pay to reveal how to separate the plaster model's sections, Reid worked out how to do it with a pulley and tackle. He was paid $1.25 a day — but only Sundays were his; his owner collected the other six days. Reid was freed April 16, 1862 by the D.C. emancipation act — **a free man before the statue was set on the dome December 2, 1863**. Sources: [Architect of the Capitol](https://www.aoc.gov/explore-capitol-campus/blog/philip-reid-and-statue-freedom), [U.S. Senate historical office](https://www.senate.gov/artandhistory/history/common/generic/Civil_War_ReidPhilip_StatueofFreedom.htm), [WHHA](https://www.whitehousehistory.org/philip-reed). (He later signed his name "Reed.")
- **Brooklyn Bridge, opened May 24, 1883.** The Roebling story, verified:
  - **John A. Roebling** designed it; in June 1869, while surveying the tower site, a ferry crushed his foot against the dock pilings; he refused conventional treatment and died of tetanus weeks later, before construction began.
  - **Washington Roebling**, his son, took over as chief engineer; working in the compressed-air caissons he was wrecked by **caisson disease (decompression sickness — "the bends")**, collapsing in 1872; partially paralyzed, he directed the work for the next decade from his Brooklyn Heights room, watching through a telescope.
  - **Emily Warren Roebling** — told honestly: Washington kept the chief engineer's title and made the engineering decisions from his sickroom; Emily became his messenger, aide, and public face for 11 years — she studied stress analysis, cable construction, and bridge mathematics to carry accurate instructions to the engineers and answer their questions, and she defended Washington before the trustees when they tried to remove him. Some writers now call her the "effective chief engineer"; the honest statement is that the bridge could not have been finished the way it was without her, and the engineers dealt with her daily. She was the first to cross by carriage, carrying a live rooster as a sign of victory. Sources: [History.com](https://www.history.com/articles/brooklyn-bridge-emily-roebling), [Emily Warren Roebling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emily_Warren_Roebling), [MCNY blog](https://blog.mcny.org/2012/09/11/the-curse-of-the-roeblings-the-construction-of-the-brooklyn-bridge/).
  - **Movie (real, verified): *Brooklyn Bridge* (1981)** — Ken Burns' first documentary, narrated by David McCullough; nominated for the Academy Award for Best Documentary Feature. Sources: [PBS](https://www.pbs.org/kenburns/brooklyn-bridge), [IMDb awards page](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt0082106/awards/). About the bridge (and the Roeblings), not a biography.
  - About 20–30 workers died building it (accounts vary); caisson disease crippled many. (MCNY; numbers vary by source — say "at least 20".)
- **Washington Monument finished.** Congress took over 1876; the Army Corps of Engineers (Lt. Col. Thomas Lincoln Casey) restarted work 1879; **completed December 6, 1884** with a **100-ounce cast aluminum tip** — aluminum was then a showpiece metal at about $1 an ounce (roughly a day's wage for a laborer); the whole tip cost $225. Dedicated February 21, 1885. At 555 feet it was the tallest structure in the world until the Eiffel Tower (1889). **The color line:** about 150 feet up, the marble visibly changes shade — the post-1879 courses came from different quarry beds than the 1848–54 stone. The stub-and-restart story is written in the stone. Sources: [TMS/JOM history of the aluminum cap](https://www.tms.org/pubs/journals/jom/9511/binczewski-9511.html), [NPS — The Point of the Matter](https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/wamocap.htm), [National Geographic](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/travel/article/washington-monument-construction-know-nothings-elevator).
- **Statue of Liberty, dedicated October 28, 1886** (President Cleveland presiding). Verified chain: proposed by Édouard de Laboulaye (1865); sculpted by **Frédéric Auguste Bartholdi**; the completed **arm and torch were exhibited at the 1876 Centennial Exhibition in Philadelphia**, then stood in Madison Square Park, New York, 1877–1882 to raise pedestal money; the internal iron skeleton was engineered by **Gustave Eiffel** (joined 1879) — the copper skin is 3/32 inch thick, "about the thickness of two pennies placed together" (NPS's own comparison), hung on an iron frame; the head and shoulders were shown at the **1878 Paris Universal Exposition**; the finished statue crossed the Atlantic aboard the French ship Isère in **350 pieces packed in 214 crates**, arriving June 17, 1885 ([Smithsonian Magazine](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/statue-liberty-arrived-350-pieces-180955920/), [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/How-Was-the-Statue-of-Liberty-Transported)); France paid for the statue, America owed the pedestal — and pedestal money stalled until **Joseph Pulitzer's New York World campaign (1885)** raised over $100,000 from about **120,000 donors**, most giving under a dollar, many of them schoolchildren's coins, every donor's name printed in the paper. Statue 151 feet; with pedestal about 305 feet. Declared a National Monument 1924 (Coolidge). Sources: [statueofliberty.org history](https://www.statueofliberty.org/statue-of-liberty/overview-history/), [History.com](https://www.history.com/articles/statue-of-liberty), [National Archives Prologue blog](https://prologue.blogs.archives.gov/2024/10/14/body-of-iron-soul-of-fire-the-statue-of-liberty/), [NPS](https://www.nps.gov/stli/). **Emma Lazarus's "The New Colossus"** (1883 fundraising sonnet) was mounted on a pedestal plaque in **1903** (VERIFIED in Ch2) — the immigrant-welcome meaning is `immigration`'s story; ours is the building of it.
- **Biltmore House, Asheville, North Carolina, 1889–1895** — George W. Vanderbilt's 250-room house, about 179,000 square feet, still the largest privately owned house in the U.S. Opened to family Christmas Eve 1895. Source: [Biltmore's own history](https://www.biltmore.com/blog/10-fast-facts-about-biltmore/), [Biltmore Estate overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biltmore_Estate).
- One-line shared items: the first steel-frame tall buildings (Chicago, 1885 on) are `city-building`'s story as urban form; Yellowstone (1872, first national park) is `land-environment`'s as land policy — each gets a pointer sentence only.

## 08 1900 to 1950 — the giant decades

- **Lincoln Memorial, dedicated May 30, 1922.** Architect Henry Bacon; the seated Lincoln by sculptor Daniel Chester French. Hard truth from day one: the dedication crowd was segregated, and the only Black speaker, Robert Russa Moton of Tuskegee, had his speech text vetted. On Easter Sunday, **April 9, 1939**, after the DAR barred her from Constitution Hall, **Marian Anderson** sang from its steps to about 75,000 people — the concert story belongs to `rights-movements`; the memorial-as-stage is ours. Sources: [History.com dedication entry](https://www.history.com/this-day-in-history/may-30/former-president-taft-dedicates-lincoln-memorial), [Smithsonian Music on Anderson](https://music.si.edu/story/marian-anderson-and-concert-lincoln-memorial), [National Archives blog](https://rediscovering-black-history.blogs.archives.gov/2014/05/20/marian-anderson-and-the-easter-sunday-concert-april-9-1939/).
- **Mount Rushmore, carved October 1927 – October 31, 1941,** by about 400 workers under sculptor **Gutzon Borglum** (died March 1941; his son Lincoln finished the season). Told honestly, both halves:
  - The mountain is **Tunkasila Sakpe, the Six Grandfathers**, in the Black Hills — land guaranteed to the Lakota by the **1868 Fort Laramie Treaty** and taken after the 1874 gold rush; in **United States v. Sioux Nation (1980)** the Supreme Court ruled the taking violated the treaty and awarded compensation; **the Lakota have refused the money** (now over $1 billion with interest), holding that the land was never for sale. Sources: [Smithsonian Magazine](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/sordid-history-mount-rushmore-180960446/), [U.S. v. Sioux Nation, 448 U.S. 371 (1980)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/448/371/), [National Archives — Fort Laramie Treaty](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/fort-laramie-treaty).
  - **Borglum before Rushmore** carved the Confederate memorial at **Stone Mountain, Georgia** (1915–1925), a project tied up with the revived Ku Klux Klan, which helped fund it; historians document that Borglum attended Klan meetings and was deeply involved in Klan politics, though no membership record is proven. Say both plainly. Sources: [Washington Post history desk](https://www.washingtonpost.com/history/2020/07/03/mount-rushmore-gutzon-borglum-klan-stone-mountain/), [Smithsonian Magazine](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/sordid-history-mount-rushmore-180960446/).
  - Carving facts: 90% of the "carving" was dynamite; about 450,000 tons of rock removed; **no worker died** on the project (NPS). Workers earned by the hour in Depression years and were glad of the jobs.
  - **Nick Clifford (story, ordinary, verified):** Donald "Nick" Clifford, hired 1938 at 17 — the youngest Rushmore worker — partly because the Borglums wanted him for the crew's baseball team (he pitched). Earned 55 cents an hour; ran the winch that carried men up the face and drilled dynamite holes. Worked to 1940; last surviving carver; died November 23, 2019, at 98. Sources: [NPR obituary](https://www.npr.org/2019/11/26/783091531/last-living-mount-rushmore-carver-dies-at-98), [Mount Rushmore Society](https://mountrushmoresociety.com/2019/11/honoring-nick-clifford/).
- **Empire State Building, opened May 1, 1931.** Built in about **410 days** from first steel to opening — up to 3,400 workers a day, the frame rising about 4.5 floors a week during the push. **Five workers died** — official count; the "hundreds" of legend is false. 102 stories, 1,250 feet (1,454 to today's antenna tip); world's tallest building until 1971 (World Trade Center). **Lewis Hine** photographed the "sky boys" for his book *Men at Work* (1931–32) — the photos are `work-workers`' shared thread. Among the riveting gangs were **Mohawk ironworkers from Kahnawake**, continuing a high-steel tradition dating from the 1880s — Native builders on the tallest thing in the world (Native continuity past 1900; the full story lands with Harold McComber in the next era). Sources: [History.com construction article](https://www.history.com/articles/empire-state-building-construction), [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Empire-State-Building).
- **Golden Gate Bridge, opened May 27–28, 1937** (200,000 walked it on Pedestrian Day, May 27). Chief engineer **Joseph Strauss**; **credit dispute stated honestly:** structural engineer **Charles Ellis** did the detailed design and thousands of calculations in 1929–31, was fired by Strauss before construction, and got no credit for decades — in 2007 the bridge district's own report finally gave Ellis principal design credit. Strauss's real distinction on the job: **safety.** He required hard hats and spent about $130,000 on the first big **safety net** in American bridge-building — it saved **19 men**, who called themselves the **"Halfway to Hell Club."** Eleven men died in all: one in October 1936, then **ten on February 17, 1937**, when a falling work platform tore through the net. Sources: [Half Way to Hell Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half_Way_to_Hell_Club), [Linda Hall Library on Ellis](https://www.lindahall.org/about/news/scientist-of-the-day/charles-alton-ellis/), [History News Network on the 2007 credit report](https://www.historynewsnetwork.org/article/design-credit-for-golden-gate-bridge-reassigned), Golden Gate Bridge District history pages.
  - **Al Zampa (story, ordinary, verified):** Alfred Zampa, ironworker from Crockett, California. On October 19, 1936 he slipped on wet steel and fell into the net — but near the shore the net sagged to the rocks, and he broke four bones in his back. Twelve weeks in the hospital, two years in a back brace — then back to ironwork, a founding member of the Halfway to Hell Club and later its unofficial spokesman. In **2003** the new Carquinez Strait span was named the **Alfred Zampa Memorial Bridge** — named for a working ironworker, not a politician. Sources: [Al Zampa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al_Zampa), [Alfred Zampa Memorial Bridge Foundation](https://www.alzampabridge.com/the-man), [California SCR-97](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billTextClient.xhtml?bill_id=199920000SCR97).
- **Hoover Dam, dedicated September 30, 1935** (FDR); generators running 1936. **The deaths, honestly:** the Bureau of Reclamation's official count is **96 industrial fatalities** (falls, rock slides, equipment, drowning); it excludes deaths recorded as "pneumonia," which many historians believe covered carbon-monoxide poisoning of tunnel crews (42 such deaths) — so the true toll is higher than 96 and the exact number is not known. **Debunk plainly: nobody is buried in the concrete.** The dam was poured as many small blocks in shallow lifts, with crews working over each pour — a body could not have been left in it. Sources: [Bureau of Reclamation — Fatalities at Hoover Dam](https://www.usbr.gov/lc/hooverdam/history/essays/fatal.html).
- Ellis Island's 1900 main building and Angel Island's 1910 barracks were this era's working immigration landmarks (their arrival stories = `immigration`); preservation of both lands in later eras.

## 09 1950 to 2000 — monuments about loss

- **Gateway Arch, St. Louis.** Design competition won by **Eero Saarinen** (announced February 1948); he died in 1961, before construction. Built **February 12, 1963 – October 28, 1965**; 630 feet tall and 630 feet wide, stainless steel. Insurers predicted 13 deaths; **no worker died**. Sources: [History.com](https://www.history.com/this-day-in-history/october-28/gateway-arch-completed), [NPS Gateway Arch history](https://www.nps.gov/jeff/learn/historyculture/index.htm), [Labor Tribune](https://labortribune.com/union-built-gateway-arch-is-60/).
  - **Civil-rights protest on the Arch itself:** on July 14, 1964, activists **Percy Green** and Richard Daly climbed 125 feet up the unfinished north leg to protest the exclusion of Black workers from Arch construction jobs — documented by NPS and the Gateway Arch Park Foundation. The campaign story is `rights-movements`' (parked there); the fact that the landmark's own construction was the protest's stage is ours. Sources: [Gateway Arch Park Foundation](https://www.archpark.org/updates/news/percy-green-the-man-who-climbed-the-gateway-arch), [NPS interview](https://www.nps.gov/jeff/learn/photosmultimedia/percy-green-interview.htm).
- **Space Needle, Seattle, 1962** — built in about 400 days for the Century 21 Exposition (opened April 21, 1962, the fair's first day); 605 feet, revolving restaurant on top. Sources: [Space Needle official facts](https://www.spaceneedle.com/facts-1), [HistoryLink](https://www.historylink.org/File/722).
- **World Trade Center, New York — ribbon-cut April 4, 1973.** Architect Minoru Yamasaki; twin 110-story towers; the North Tower's 1,368 feet made it the world's tallest building — for barely a month on paper, as Chicago's Sears Tower topped out taller weeks later. Sources: [9/11 Memorial & Museum blog](https://911memorial.org/connect/blog/commemorating-45th-anniversary-world-trade-center-dedication), [1 WTC (1970–2001)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1_World_Trade_Center_(1970%E2%80%932001)).
  - **Philippe Petit (story, famous, verified):** on August 7, 1974, the French wire-walker and his friends smuggled a cable to the towers' roofs and he walked between them — about 1,350 feet up, eight crossings in 45 minutes — then was arrested; charges were dropped in exchange for a children's show in Central Park. **Movie: *Man on Wire* (2008)**, directed by James Marsh — won the Academy Award for Best Documentary Feature (2009). About Petit's walk directly. Sources: [History.com](https://www.history.com/this-day-in-history/august-7/philippe-petit-tightrope-walk-twin-towers-world-trade-center-1974), [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Philippe-Petit).
  - **Harold McComber (story, ordinary, verified):** Mohawk ironworker from Kahnawake, the community whose men built high steel in New York for generations (a tradition running from the 1880s through the Quebec Bridge disaster of 1907 — 33 Kahnawake men killed; that calamity is `disasters`') — McComber's working life on Manhattan skyscrapers is the subject of **the real documentary *High Steel* (1965)**, National Film Board of Canada, directed by Don Owen. Sources: [NFB film page](https://www.nfb.ca/film/high_steel/), [High Steel film entry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_Steel).
- **Vietnam Veterans Memorial, Washington — dedicated November 13, 1982.** In 1981 **Maya Lin**, a 21-year-old Yale architecture student, won the biggest design competition in American history to that point — her entry, first drafted for a class, beat **1,420 others** in blind judging — the jury chose **entry number 1026** without knowing whose it was, and officials came to her dorm room in May 1981 to tell her she had won the **$20,000** first prize ([VVMF](https://www.vvmf.org/About-The-Wall/history-of-the-vietnam-veterans-memorial/Maya-Lin/), [Biography.com](https://www.biography.com/military-figures/maya-lin-vietnam-veterans-memorial)). Two walls of polished black granite sunk into the Mall, carrying the names of the more than 58,000 dead, in the order they fell. The design drew fierce attack — one veteran called it "a black gash of shame" — and the compromise added Frederick Hart's *Three Servicemen* statue nearby (1984). Lin's name went unmentioned at the dedication. The wall became the most visited memorial in Washington, its power exactly what critics hated: you read the names and see your own face in the stone. **Movie: *Maya Lin: A Strong Clear Vision* (1994)** — won the Academy Award for Best Documentary Feature; about Lin herself. Sources: [VVMF — Maya Lin](https://www.vvmf.org/About-The-Wall/history-of-the-vietnam-veterans-memorial/Maya-Lin/), [History.com](https://www.history.com/articles/the-21-year-old-college-student-who-designed-the-vietnam-memorial), [Academy of Achievement](https://achievement.org/achiever/maya-lin/), [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Maya-Lin).
- **Statue of Liberty centennial restoration, 1984–86** — original 1886 torch removed 1984 (now in the museum), replaced with a gold-leafed copper replica; rededicated **July 4, 1986**. Sources: [NPS on the original torch](https://www.nps.gov/articles/statue-of-liberty-s-original-torch-completes-move-to-new-home.htm).
- Ellis Island: added to the Statue of Liberty National Monument by LBJ's Proclamation 3656, **May 11, 1965**; main building restored and opened as the Immigration Museum 1990. Sources: [NPS Proclamation 3656](https://www.nps.gov/elis/learn/management/proclamation-3656.htm). (Arrival stories = `immigration`; the building's rescue is ours.)

## 10 2000 to Today — new memorials, old arguments (current through 2026)

- **National September 11 Memorial, opened September 11, 2011** (the attacks' tenth anniversary). Design: "Reflecting Absence" by architect **Michael Arad** with landscape architect Peter Walker — two recessed pools in the towers' footprints, the names of the dead in bronze around the rims; chosen from **5,201** competition entries. Arad was a 34-year-old architect then working for a New York City municipal agency; his first study imagined two voids torn in the Hudson River ([Metropolis — the ten-year saga](https://metropolismag.com/projects/contentious-ten-year-saga-michael-arad-9-11-memorial-design/), [Newsweek](https://www.newsweek.com/where-memory-endures-102719)). The **9/11 Memorial Museum** opened May 21, 2014. **One World Trade Center opened November 3, 2014** — 1,776 feet to the spire tip, the number chosen for the year of the Declaration. The attacks themselves = `america-world`/`disasters`; what was built afterward is ours. Sources: [911memorial.org — About the Memorial](https://www.911memorial.org/visit/memorial/about-memorial), [911memorial.org — About the Museum](https://911memorial.org/visit/museum/about-museum), [History.com on One WTC](https://www.history.com/this-day-in-history/november-3/one-world-trade-center-officially-opens-in-new-york-city).
- **Martin Luther King, Jr. Memorial, dedicated October 16, 2011** — the first memorial honoring an African American on the National Mall area (West Potomac Park); the 30-foot Stone of Hope. Sources: [Trust for the National Mall](https://nationalmall.org/monuments-memorials/mlk), [NPS](https://www.nps.gov/mlkm/).
- **National Museum of African American History and Culture, opened September 24, 2016** — Smithsonian museum beside the Washington Monument; lead designer **David Adjaye**; the three-tiered bronze-colored "corona" form draws on Yoruba art, its lattice patterned on ironwork made by enslaved and free Black craftsmen in the South. Sources: [NMAAHC — The Building](https://nmaahc.si.edu/about/building), [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/National-Museum-of-African-American-History-and-Culture).
- **Confederate monuments coming down — ongoing history, journalism and advocacy counts labeled as such.** After the 2015 Charleston church murders, the 2017 Charlottesville rally (held to defend a Lee statue; one counter-protester killed), and the 2020 George Floyd protests, hundreds of Confederate symbols were removed: the Southern Poverty Law Center's "Whose Heritage?" project (advocacy organization count) tallied at least **168 Confederate symbols removed in 2020 alone**; Richmond's giant **Robert E. Lee statue came down September 8, 2021** after the Virginia Supreme Court cleared the way (news: Washington Post, AP). Hundreds more remain standing. The argument over what monuments are *for* is the `how-we-know` afterword's subject; the removals themselves are landmark history. Sources (journalism/advocacy, labeled): [Washington Post](https://www.washingtonpost.com/local/virginia-politics/robert-e-lee-statue-removal/2021/09/08/1d9564ee-103d-11ec-9cb6-bf9351a25799_story.html), [Smithsonian Magazine](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/richmond-confederate-monuments-headed-to-black-history-museum-180979319/), [SPLC Whose Heritage reports](https://hyperallergic.com/446505/splc-confederate-monuments-report-map-study/).
- **Gateway Arch National Park:** the Jefferson National Expansion Memorial was renamed and redesignated **February 22, 2018** — the smallest national park in the country. Sources: [signing statement](https://trumpwhitehouse.archives.gov/briefings-statements/president-donald-j-trump-signs-s-1438-law), [Gateway Arch National Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gateway_Arch_National_Park).
