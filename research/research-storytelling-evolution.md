# ERA 01 · Before 1500 (T-269a, 2026-09-28)

## Masked performance
- Kachina/katsina: "any of more than 500 divine and ancestral spirit beings"; each Pueblo culture has its own forms; "believed to reside with the tribe for half of each year"; seen "if its men properly perform a traditional ritual while wearing kachina masks and other regalia"; "The spirit-being depicted on the mask is thought to be actually present with or within the performer, temporarily transforming him." Dolls carved by men, given to girls, teach the identities of the katsinam. Source: Britannica, "kachina" (rev. Virginia Gorlinski), https://www.britannica.com/topic/kachina (read via curl 2026-09-28).
- Dating: E. Charles Adams, "Katsina Depictions on Homol'ovi Ceramics: Toward a Fourteenth-Century Pueblo Iconography," *Kiva* 54:3 (1989): mask depictions occur only after about AD 1300 in the Homol'ovi area, only at large aggregated sites, in kiva murals, painted slabs and pottery. https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/00231940.1989.11758123 (abstract seen via search summary) (unconfirmed: search summary only). Murals showing masked beings identifiable as katsinam "may date as early as 1350 at Hopi and Homol'ovi" (Peabody Museum "Rainmakers from the Gods" page, 403 on fetch) (unconfirmed: search summary only). Awatovi and Kawaika-a kiva murals: Watson Smith, *Kiva Mural Decorations at Awatovi and Kawaika-a* (1952), archive.org.
- Yup'ik: *Archaeology* magazine, Sept/Oct 2015, "Cultural Revival," https://archaeology.org/issues/187-1509/features/3558-alaska-yupik-cultural-revival : Nunalleq ("Old Village") near Quinhagak, Alaska, inhabited 1300 to 1650s; excavated masks are "the oldest complete Yup'ik masks in existence," precontact, before 1650. Masks central to the winter ceremony (agayuyaraq). Ann Fienup-Riordan: dancers "take on the spirits of the animals portrayed by the masks" and entreat them "to offer themselves up to hunters in the coming year." After use "masks were typically burned, broken, or left out on the tundra to decay." Moravian missionaries arrived 1885, called masked dance "heathen rites"; no traditional dancing in Quinhagak for over a century until 2013. (The 1885 suppression belongs to era 07: noted for T-269b.)
- Haudenosaunee: Grand Council of the Haudenosaunee, policy on medicine masks, *Akwesasne Notes* Spring 1995, reproduced at http://www.nativetech.org/cornhusk/maskpoli.html : "All wooden and corn husk masks of the Haudenosaunee are sacred, regardless of size or age." "The public exhibition of all medicine masks is forbidden." Reproductions, photographs and illustrations "should not be used in exhibitions." "Masks should not be made unless they are to be used by members of the medicine society." A medicine society "is comprised of Haudenosaunee who have partaken of the medicine." NOTE FOR WRITERS: do not describe the masks' appearance in detail. "False Face Society" is the outsiders' label. No source dates the practice before 1500.
- Tlingit/Haida transformation masks: the seed claimed them for this era. The Met's Tlingit transformation mask is dated 1820 to 1830 (https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/319070) (unconfirmed: search summary only). No pre-1500 evidence found. REMOVED from era 01. Hinged transformation masks are best documented for the Kwakwaka'wakw in Canada (Smarthistory) (unconfirmed: search summary only).

## Rules about who tells what, and when
- Ojibwe aadizookaanag (winter-only sacred stories): MPR News, Jan 25, 2024, https://www.mprnews.org/story/2024/01/25/in-ojibwe-tradition-snow-on-the-ground-means-its-sacred-storytelling-season . James Vukelich Kaagegaabaw (Turtle Clan, Turtle Mountain descendant): people wait "till lakes have frozen over, till the amphibians and reptiles are hibernating, as well as the bears, and there's also a blanket of snow on the ground"; "those spirits are thought to be sleeping and you wouldn't want to be thought of as gossiping about them"; out-of-season telling: "a frog or a toad may jump into your bed." Nanabozho/Wenabozho is the central figure.
- Tlingit at.óow: Sealaska Heritage Institute, "SHI publishes first-ever registry of Tlingit clan crests," https://sealaskaheritage.org/shi-publishes-first-ever-registry-of-tlingit-clan-crests/ : crests are "the most important symbols of the history and identity of Tlingit clans," acquired by ancestors through "crucial encounters with animal persons and spiritual beings"; at.óow is "sacred property belonging to the clan" including "material objects and the stories, songs, and names which represent them"; held "in perpetuity by clan members through the generations"; the right was "often acquired as payment for the life of an ancestor." SHI President Rosita Worl.

## Places built to gather
- Cahokia Grand Plaza: 19 ha (47 acres), "the largest plaza in terms of area"; "the Plaza was a built structure not simply a flattening of the original surface"; Rita Dalan's electromagnetic survey showed a sand ridge between Mounds 48 and 56 cut down for fill; Holley, Dalan and Smith 1993; a 1997 water-main trench gave a cross-section. UNF, R. Thunen, "Cahokia: The Plazas," https://rthunen.domains.unf.edu/prehistoric/cahokia-the-plazas/ . Other figures: "40-acre Grand Plaza" (HMDB marker) (unconfirmed: search summary only); "roughly 50 acres (20 ha) ... over 1,600 ft by over 900 ft" (Wikipedia "Cahokia") (unconfirmed: search summary only). Record all three. "Filled and leveled by hand" (Wikipedia via search) (unconfirmed: search summary only).
- Casa Rinconada, Chaco Canyon: "average interior diameter of 63 feet (19.2 m.)"; construction "may have begun in the middle-11th century and use likely ended in the early-12th century"; "a firebox, an inner bench, four large seating pits that served as roof supports, two masonry vaults/foot drums, and 34 niches"; "a 39-foot-long (12 m.) underground passage" that "would have allowed Chacoans, perhaps ritual specialists, to enter the great kiva unseen and then suddenly emerge." Chaco Research Archive, https://www.chacoarchive.org/cra/chaco-sites/casa-rinconada/ . SAH Archipedia gives 64 ft and ranks it third-largest after Penasco Blanco (74 ft) and Aztec East (67 ft) (unconfirmed: search summary only).

# ERA 02 · The 1500s (T-269a, 2026-09-28)

## The Rio Grande play, April 30, 1598
- Marc Simmons (historian of New Mexico), "Trail Dust: Oñate expedition put on Southwest's first dramatic play," *Santa Fe New Mexican* (reprint of his column; read via curl 2026-09-28), https://www.santafenewmexican.com/news/trail_dust/trail-dust-o-ate-expedition-put-on-southwest-s-first/article_ae52e47d-a489-5e8c-978c-8cc12382636a.html :
  - "On April 30, 1598, the great colonizing expedition of Don Juan de Oñate reached the banks of the Rio Grande a few miles below present-day Juárez and made camp. Because of a later change in the course of the river, the spot is probably now within the United States."
  - "they presented a play depicting the anticipated settlement of New Mexico. The author of the impromptu drama was one of Oñate's officers, Capt. Marcos Farfan de los Godos."
  - Muster roll: "a native of the city of Seville. He had passed muster with two servants, 16 horses, a tent, weapons and armor."
  - "Oñate had ordered a temporary chapel set up in a grove of cottonwoods. After the Mass, the play began nearby. A canvas curtain stretched between two tree trunks to act as a backdrop. The bare ground served as a stage."
  - "Soldiers and friars took the leading roles. Indians, members of the timid Manso tribe, who had been found lurking nearby, were pressed into service. They acted the parts of the Pueblo Indians living farther north on the Rio Grande." (WRITERS: do not reuse "timid" or "lurking", which are Simmons's words and evaluative. The fact is that Manso people were made part of the cast.)
  - "Capt. Farfan himself opened with an oration. Proclaiming he was unworthy to write this poor play, he humbly offered it for the amusement of his comrades and the illustrious Don Juan de Oñate."
  - An officer's description: "The play pictured the advent of the friars to New Mexico. We saw the priests coming to this land. They were received kindly by the simple natives who approached on bended knee and asked to be received into the faith, being baptized in great numbers." Other scenes showed the hardships on the trail.
  - "At the end, onlookers cheered and fired harquebuses into the air. Oñate then came forward and conducted a formal ceremony taking possession of New Mexico." (NOTE: Simmons puts La Toma after the play. El Paso Mission Trail puts La Toma "during the feast". Order of events differs by source; not important to the book.)
  - "The name of Farfan's little drama has been lost, if indeed it even had a name. And no manuscript of the dialogue has ever turned up."
- The primary account is Gaspar Pérez de Villagrá, *Historia de la Nueva México* (Alcalá, 1610), Canto XIV, a 34-canto epic poem by a captain on the expedition (National Humanities Center excerpt of the Espinosa 1933 translation, https://nationalhumanitiescenter.org/pds/amerbegin/exploration/text1/villagra.pdf, which reprints the Canto XIV river arrival but not the play passage). A search summary reports the canto names a work by "the noble Captain Farfan" whose argument was "the great reception that all the new Mexico gave to the Church" (unconfirmed: search summary only). A blog gives the title as "Comedia del recibimiento que hizo la Nueva España a la Iglesia" (padavisblog, search summary) (unconfirmed: search summary only).
- Date and place: "Juan de Oñate reached the Rio Grande at or near the site of present San Elizario on April 20, 1598, and ten days later took formal possession of New Mexico and all adjacent territory in the name of the Spanish king." TSHA Handbook, "San Elizario, TX," by Martin Donell Kohout, https://www.tshaonline.org/handbook/entries/san-elizario-tx . El Paso Mission Trail: "On April 30, 1598 ... celebrated a Thanksgiving Mass and feast ... game by the Spaniards and fish by the natives ... Oñate read 'La Toma' (taking possession) which declared the land that was drained by the river to be the possession of King Phillip II of Spain." https://www.elpasomissions.org/history/san-elizario/first-thanksgiving/
- "First" claim: Simmons calls it the Southwest's first dramatic play and says the answer "is well known to historians." The book may say "the first European play on record in what is now the United States." Do not say "first play" without "European" and "on record": Native performance is older.

## Acoma (cross-reference; `native-nations` leads)
All figures here are taken from `research/research-native-nations.md`, section "Acoma Pueblo, December 1598 to February 1599," which gives full sourcing (NPS; NM Office of the State Historian; Carlson 2008 thesis citing Hammond and Rey 1953; All Pueblo Council of Governors, Oct. 6, 2023; NPR 2020; AP 2020; Sherry Robinson 2023 quoting Simmons and Kessell). Summary for this chapter's writer:
- Early Dec 1598: Juan de Zaldívar's party demanded food at Acoma; the Acoma killed Zaldívar and between 11 and 14 men (sources differ). Pueblo account: the soldiers also assaulted an Acoma woman (APCG 2023); one press report says raped (unconfirmed in native-nations bank).
- Jan 1599: Vicente de Zaldívar with about 70 soldiers; arrival Jan 12 or Jan 21 (sources differ); fighting two or three days; pueblo stormed and burned.
- Acoma dead: no count at the time; estimates about 300 to about 1,500; about 800 most often given and the APCG figure; NPS 1,500.
- About 500 captives. Trial opened Feb 9, 1599 (Carlson citing Simmons); sentence dated Feb 12 in other sources.
- Sentence: men over 25, one foot (most sources: right foot) cut off plus 20 years' servitude; males 12 to 25 and females over 12, 20 years' servitude; children under 12 taken from families and given to the friars, 60 (APCG) or 70 (NPS) girls sent to Mexico, none known to have returned; two Hopi men had a right hand cut off.
- 24 men mutilated: widely reported (AP, NPR, NYT 1998 on Acoma oral tradition); not traced to a document. Simmons later doubted the amputations were carried out, and Kessell doubts it. A claim that toes, not feet, were meant comes from an op-ed (Chavez y Gilbert 2017), not scholarship.
- 1614: Oñate convicted on 12 of 30 charges, including excessive force at Acoma; banished from New Mexico for life, exiled from Mexico City four (or five) years, fined 6,000 ducats. Pardon uncertain.
- WRITER NOTE: the seed's "about 500 men and about 300 women and children killed over three days" is one estimate (Kessel and Wooster 2005, via Wikipedia). It is replaced in the outline by the range.

# ERA 03 · The 1600s (T-269a, 2026-09-28)

## England closes its theatres, 1642
- Primary text: "September 1642: Order for Stage-plays to cease," [2 September, 1642], in *Acts and Ordinances of the Interregnum, 1642-1660*, ed. C. H. Firth and R. S. Rait (London, 1911), pp. 26-27, British History Online, https://www.british-history.ac.uk/no-series/acts-ordinances-interregnum/pp26-27 : "whereas Public Sports do not well agree with Public Calamities, nor Public Stage-plays with the Seasons of Humiliation, this being an Exercise of sad and pious Solemnity, and the other being Spectacles of Pleasure, too commonly expressing lascivious Mirth and Levity: It is therefore thought fit, and Ordained, by the Lords and Commons in this Parliament assembled, That, while these sad causes and set Times of Humiliation do continue, Public Stage Plays shall cease, and be forborn."
- 11 February 1648 act: actors treated as rogues, theatre seating demolished, spectators fined (Wikipedia "London theatre closure 1642" and EBSCO Research Starters, via search) (unconfirmed: search summary only). Theatres reopened August 1660; Charles II granted patents to William Davenant and Thomas Killigrew (Wikipedia, via search) (unconfirmed: search summary only).
- Increase Mather, *A Testimony against several Prophane and Superstitious Customs, Now Practised by Some in New-England* (Boston/London, 1687), EEBO A50236 (403 on fetch): Christmas keepers "are consumed in compotations, in interludes, in playing at cards, in revelings, in excess of wine, in mad mirth" (quoted via search summary) (unconfirmed: search summary only). ## Ye Bare and Ye Cubb, 1665 (primary record)
- Accomack County court orders, transcribed in Charles Campbell, *History of the Colony and Ancient Dominion of Virginia* (1860), reproduced at https://genealogytrails.com/vir/accomack/history_xxviii.html ; the same extracts in Jennings Cropper Wise, *Ye Kingdome of Accawmacke* (1911), https://genealogytrails.com/vir/accomack/accawmackexv.html . Transcripts certified "true transcripts from the Records of the Court of the County of Accomack" by J. W. Gillett, clerk.
  - Court of 16 November 1665: "Whereas, Cornelius Watkinson, Philip Howard, and William Darby, were this Day accused by Mr. Jno. Fawsett, his ma'ties Attory for Accomack County, for acting a play by them called ye Bare & ye Cubb, on ye 27th of August last past; upon examination of the same, The Court have thought fitt to suspend the Cause till ye next Court, & doe order y't the said Cornelius Watkinson, Phillip Howard, & Wm. Darby, appeare ye next Court, in those habilemts that they then acted in, and give a draught of such verses, or other speeches and passages, which were then acted by them; & that ye Sherr detaine Cornelius Watkinson & Philip Howard in His Custody untill they put in Security to performe this order. It is ordered y't the Sherr. arrest ye Body of William Darby, for his appearance ye next Court, to answere at his ma'ties suit, for [acting or] being actour of a play commonly called ye Beare and ye Cubb."

  - Court of 17 January [1665 old style, i.e. 1666]: "the Court finding the said p'sons not guilty of fault, suspended ye payment of Court charges ... It's therefore ordered that ye said Edward Martin pay all ye Charges in ye suit."

## Pueblo ceremonies suppressed, and the 1680 revolt
- Dennis Zotigh (NMAI), "In 1680, Pueblo Natives in the Southwest Launched the First Successful, Although Short-Lived, American Revolution," Smithsonian Voices, dated Sept 2, 2026 on the page (read via curl), https://www.smithsonianmag.com/blogs/national-museum-american-indian/2026/09/02/pueblo-natives-southwest-launched-first-successful-shortlived-american-revolution/ :
  - "Franciscan missionaries established Catholic churches in several of the Pueblo villages, Christianizing the Natives while abolishing their traditional worship practices under the protection of armed Spanish soldiers. From 1656 to 1665, Franciscan missionary leader Alonso de Posada forbid traditional ceremonies by the Pueblo people and ordered the Spanish missionaries to invade the sacred Pueblo kivas (dwellings of spiritual worship), burn their masks and confiscate or destroy their sacred objects."
  - "In 1675 Juan Francisco Treviño, Spanish governor of Santa Fe de Nuevo México, ordered the arrest of 47 medicine men for practicing sorcery. Four were sentenced to death by hanging; in the end, three were hanged and one committed suicide. The remaining spiritual leaders were humiliated and publicly whipped before being sent to prison." Pueblo men went to Santa Fe and demanded release; "The Spanish complied." Po'pay of Ohkay Owingeh among those released.
  - Knotted cords; revolt set for Aug 11, 1680; runners Pedro Omtua and Nicolas Catua of Tesuque captured and tortured Aug 9; revolt began Aug 10. "approximately 400 people were killed, including 21 of the 33 Spanish priests in Nuevo México." Churches at Oraibi, Awatovi, Shungopavi destroyed. Aug 21 Governor Antonio de Otermín led "The remaining 2,000 settlers" south to El Paso del Norte.
  - "After the expulsion of the Spanish, their churches were destroyed and the traditional religious practices of the Pueblo people were restored. The Pueblo Revolt effectively ended Spanish rule in the region for 12 years."
- This resolves the seed's [VERIFY] on "banned ceremonies performed openly again after 1680": supported by Zotigh ("traditional religious practices ... restored").
- The kiva raids of 1656-65 answer the hard-subject question "who suppressed Native ceremony": Alonso de Posada (Franciscan custodian) and the missionaries under him, protected by soldiers; Governor Treviño in 1675.

# ERA 04 · 1700 to 1750 (T-269a, 2026-09-28)

## Anthony Aston
- Aston's own words (preface to *The Fool's Opera*, 1731 per the blog, 1730 per NCpedia's summary), quoted in Historic Interpreter blog: "My merry hearts, you are to know me as a gentleman, lawyer, poet, actor, soldier, sailer, exciseman, publican, in England, Scotland, Ireland, New York, East and West Jersey, Maryland, Virginia (on both sides Cheesapeek), North and South Carolina, South Florida, Bahamas, Jamaica, Hispaniola, and often a coaster by all the same. . . . After many vicissitudes I arrived at Charles Towne full of lice, shame, poverty, nakedness and hunger, turned player and poet and wrote one play on the subject of the country." Ship blown to the Virginia Capes on way to New York; in New York met "Jack Charlton, Fencing Master and Counsellor Reignieur"; "after acting, writing, courting, fighting that winter" got passage to Virginia from "Captain Davies"; later went to London and married.
- NCpedia, "Strolling Players," William S. Powell, 2006, https://www.ncpedia.org/strolling-players : Aston "a British lawyer" arrived Charleston in 1702 [date as summarized]; stayed about a month in the Albemarle region, supported by Allen and Abraham Waights; no copy of his play survives.
- Arrival dates differ: 1701 (blog: "believed"), 1702 (NCpedia), 1703 (Encyclopedia.com; Wikipedia via search). Record all three.
- "First professional actor": "generally considered to have been the first professional actor to appear on an American stage" (Wikipedia "Anthony Aston", search summary) (unconfirmed: search summary only). Outline attributes it ("usually named as").

## Boston, 1714

## Laws against plays

## The Chapel Street Theatre, New York, May 1766
