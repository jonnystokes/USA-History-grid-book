# T-272a research (2026-09-28): eras 01 to 05

## Era 01: before-1500

### Southwest: feathers, fiber, cotton
- Ancestral Puebloan clothing mostly deer hide, rabbit skin, yucca fiber. Cotton reached the Four Corners by trade from Mexico and southern AZ/NM around 600 AD. By 900 AD vertical looms used for large fabrics and blankets. "For many centuries Ancestral Puebloan men grew cotton, spun into thread, and wove into fabric on upright looms." Weaving declined after the late 1500s when the Spanish brought Churro sheep wool and new dyes. (Grand Canyon Conservancy, "Weavers", https://www.grandcanyon.org/protect-grand-canyon/projects/cultural-demonstration-weavers , fetched 2026-09-28)
- Turkey feather blanket, about 1200s CE, Edge of the Cedars State Park Museum, SE Utah: about 11,550 feathers, over 180 m (590 ft) yucca cordage, feathers wrapped around fiber warp cords, 4 to 10 turkeys, feathers "probably most frequently collected from live birds". By the early CE centuries feather blankets replaced rabbit-fur-strip blankets in the Upland Southwest. Researchers Bill Lipe and Shannon Tushingham, Washington State University, *Journal of Archaeological Science: Reports*, 25 Nov 2020. (Sci.News, https://www.sci.news/archaeology/ancestral-puebloan-turkey-feather-blanket-09091.html , fetched. Smithsonian Magazine version returned 403.)
- Tonto Basin (Salado, about 1250 to 1450 CE, NPS): earliest cultivated cotton (Gossypium hopi) at Eagle Ridge site about 250 CE. Cotton traded from Tonto Creek to the Colorado Plateau from about 1100. By 1300 the Tonto Basin was a leading Southwest cotton producer. Items found: plain-weave cotton cloth, weft-wrap openwork cloth, belt/tie with designs, brightly dyed tumpline (forehead carrying strap), rag-weft blankets from worn-out cotton, sandals of agave and yucca. (American Southwest Virtual Museum, NAU, "Textiles and Other Organic Artifacts: Tonto National Monument", https://swvirtualmuseum.nau.edu/wp/index.php/national-parks/tonto-nm/textiles-and-other-organic-artifacts-tonto-national-monument/ , fetched. NPS "Tonto Basin Salado", https://home.nps.gov/articles/tonto-basin-salado.htm : cotton grown and woven, Roosevelt Phase 1250-1350.)
- NOT USED: the SW Virtual Museum line that "Spanish conquistadors mistook this cloth for silk." The Salado sites were abandoned by about 1450, before any Spaniard arrived (1540), and the page gives no source for the claim.

### Mississippian copper and shell
- Mississippian copper plates: plain and repoussé (hammered from the back) beaten copper, about 800 to 1600 CE, found in FL, GA, IL, MS, OK, TN, WI. Copper usually from the Great Lakes, some from the Appalachians. Wulfing cache (SE Missouri), some Etowah plates (GA) and many Spiro plates (OK) in the Greater Braden style, thought made at Cahokia in the 1200s. Templates used at Etowah for badges and headdress ornaments. (Wikipedia, "Mississippian copper plates" and "Etowah plates", search-result text 2026-09-28. Tag: (unconfirmed: search summary only) for the Cahokia-made attribution. Kemper Art Museum Wulfing essay returned 403.)
- Cahokia copper workshop just north of Mound 34, wall-trench building 5.45 m by 4.42 m, about 24 sq m. Raw copper nuggets, small worked pieces, stone copper-working tools, three tree stumps thought to hold anvil stones. Gregory Perino found evidence 1956 (late 1950s), location lost, confirmed 1999, excavations 1998 to 2012 (Wikipedia "Mound 34", fetched 2026-09-28).
- PAGE FETCHED (correction to the tag above): Wikipedia "Mississippian copper plates" confirms 800 to 1600 CE, the seven states, Great Lakes and Appalachian copper, annealing ("repeatedly heated and cooled and while it was softened by the heat, had been hammered"), use as hair ornaments and headdress pieces, avian (Birdman) imagery originating at Cahokia 1100 to 1300.
- CORRECTION (Wikipedia "Mound 72", fetched): mound begun about 1000 to 1050 CE, about 272 people in six burial episodes over about a century, "almost 62 percent" judged sacrificial victims. Beaded burial bead count on this page: 10,000. The 1,674 marine shell beads were with the seven women of the "Exotic Cache" burial, with mica, copper-covered wooden scepters, chunkey stones, 709 arrowheads. Fowler dug 1967 into the early 1970s, and again in the 1990s. The "1050 to 1150" date in the line below came from a search summary: use "the 1000s".
- Cahokia imported copper from the Lake Superior region and marine shell from the Gulf Coast (eastern Gulf of Mexico the probable source of most shell). Mound 72 high-status burials included 1,674 marine shell beads in grave goods (Wikipedia "Mound 72", search summary).
- Mound 72 "Beaded burial" / "Birdman burial": dug 1967 to 1971 by Melvin Fowler. About 270 people buried in the decades around 1050 to 1150 (Wikipedia "Mound 72"). Central bodies on a bed of marine-shell disc beads in the shape of a bird (falcon). COUNT DISAGREES: 20,000 beads (Archaeology magazine, 5 Aug 2016, https://archaeology.org/news/2016/08/05/160805-cahokia-beaded-burial/ , fetched) vs about 10,000 (Wikipedia "Mound 72"). Fowler described the two central bodies as high-status males. Thomas Emerson (Illinois State Archaeological Survey) led a 2016 reanalysis: a man and a woman, surrounded by other male-female pairs (Archaeology magazine, fetched).
- Mound 72 also holds mass graves of people killed at burial (widely reported). That belongs to `native-nations`, not to this chapter's angle, and is not used here.

### Colors
- Red ochre = iron oxide (Fe2O3), brown, red or yellow. Karankawa of the central Texas coast used ochre for face painting per early historic accounts ("used fibrous stalks or fingers to carefully paint powerful symbols"). Mitchell Ridge site: red ochre staining on skeletal remains of 18 individuals. Ochre obtained by inland trade on the coast. (Texas Beyond History, UT Austin, "Ochre", https://www.texasbeyondhistory.net/coast/nature/images/ochre.html , fetched)
- Hohokam (central AZ) used iron-oxide ochres in burials, body paint, pottery, basketry, arrows and rock paintings (Journal of Archaeological Science 2011 abstract, sciencedirect S0305440311002287: (unconfirmed: search summary only), page 403).
- Quillwork: Penn Museum Journal, "The Art of Quillwork" (https://www.penn.museum/sites/journal/630/ , fetched): practiced by Huron, Cheyenne, Arapaho, Cree, Loucheux (Gwich'in) among others. Women did the work, men hunted porcupines. Flattened with an awl or bone. Dyes: red from tamarack bark, spruce cones, berries. Black from walnuts, wild grapes. Yellow from wild sunflower, cornflower, pine bark, willow root. Blue from blueberries and larkspur. Objects: moccasins, pouches, pipe stems, baby carriers, clothing, birchbark boxes. George Catlin specimens 1832 to 1839 "perhaps the oldest and best" in Penn's collection.
- Quillwork is pre-contact and widespread across the Northeast Woodlands, Subarctic, Plains, northern Rockies and Plateau. Glass beads replaced quills in the mid 1800s. (McCord Stewart Museum blog and Wikipedia "Quillwork", search summary only, unconfirmed.)

## Era 02: 1500s

### Cofitachequi, 1540 (primary source)
Source: Gentleman (Knight) of Elvas, *A Narrative of the Expedition of Hernando de Soto into Florida*, Evora 1557, Richard Hakluyt translation, Project Gutenberg eBook #34997 (https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/34997/pg34997.txt , downloaded and read 2026-09-28, lines about 1340 to 1490):
- The lady (ruler) of Cutifachiqui came to the river in a chair carried by principal men, then by barge (canoe) with a mat and cushions. She "presented unto him great store of clothes of the country ... to wit, mantles and skins; and took from her own neck a great cordon of pearls, and cast it about the neck of the Governor".
- Storehouses: "great quantity of clothes, mantles of yarn made of the barks of trees, and others made of feathers, white, green, red, and yellow, very fine after their use, and profitable for winter. There were also many deer's skins, with many compartments traced in them, and some of them made into hose, stockings, and shoes." "All of them went shod and clothed."
- "the lady perceiving that the Christians esteemed the pearls, advised the Governor to send to search certain graves that were in that town ... They sought the graves of that town, and there found fourteen rows of pearls (three hundred and ninety-two pounds), and little babies and birds made of them." (Hakluyt's "rows" with an editor's parenthesis of 392 lb. Other translations render the unit as arrobas.)
- Pearls lost color because the people "burned them in the fire" (drilled with heat).
- "In this town was found a dagger, and beads that belonged to Christians", from Lucas Vasquez de Ayllon's colony (he died 1525/26 per text).
- De Soto "carried [her] with him"; she went with "the bondwomen which led her", said she went "to ease herself", hid in the wood and escaped, carrying a cane coffer (petaca) "full of unbored pearls". She went to Xualla with three escaped slaves.
- Pearl amount elsewhere: 350 pounds (Elvas as quoted by Legends of America and other secondary pages) and 200 pounds (Wikipedia "The Lady of Cofitachequi", fetched: "200 pounds of freshwater pearls" from the chieftainess's temple). Arroba = about 25 lb (standard Spanish unit, about 11.5 kg). 14 arrobas = about 350 lb. The 350 and 200 figures from secondary pages (unconfirmed: search summary only for 350).
- Wikipedia (fetched): de Soto's army reached the main town in May 1540. She gave him her pearl necklace, he gave her a gold ring with a ruby. Talimeco temple looted of pearls and skins.
- Her personal name is not recorded. Called "the Lady of Cofitachequi" by the Spanish.
- Hard subject check: actor = Hernando de Soto and his soldiers (grave-digging for pearls, holding the ruler captive). Elvas says she advised the grave search. The prose must say "according to Elvas" for that.

### St. Augustine clothing, 1565 to 1600
- Florida Museum of Natural History, "Life on the Remote Frontier" (https://www.floridamuseum.ufl.edu/staugustine/timeline/life-on-the-remote-frontier/ , fetched): well-to-do residents wore expensive fabric embellished with metallic lace, buttons, beads and ornamental mounts. Most common people fastened simple garments with laces tipped by metal "aglets". Buttons mainly military and wealthy. Juan de Cevadilla, collector of the situado (garrison pay) in Mexico, died suddenly in Veracruz in 1590, leaving widow Doña Petronilla a carved and gilded canopy bed of embroidered Chinese taffeta and "great quantities of luxury cloth and clothing". Soldiers frequently married Native women.
- Florida Museum St. Augustine collections: 33 Spanish colonial sites, more than 1 million items. Field school since 1968 (Hale Smith, Charles Fairbanks, Kathleen Deagan).

### Churro sheep, 1598
- NPS, "New Mexico Fiber Arts Traditions" (https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/new-mexico-fiber-arts-traditions.htm , fetched): Oñate brought churra sheep in 1598 along El Camino Real de Tierra Adentro. "By the 1700s, churra sheep numbered in the hundreds of thousands." Wool "provided the foundation of Native, Spanish, and Mexican textiles for the next 300 years." By the 13th century Native people grew cotton in NM and wove on vertical looms. Spanish introduced horizontal floor looms (Rio Grande blankets, serapes, rugs). Dyes included indigo. A c.1850 Dine blanket used indigo-dyed churro fleece and raveled lac-dyed bayeta.
- Oñate's flock "close to 5,000 head of Churras" (Navajo-Churro Sheep Association / breed histories, unconfirmed: search summary only). Other chapters give about 7,000 livestock of all kinds (migration, holidays).

### Cochineal
- Harvard Museums of Science and Culture, "Cochineal: Mexico's Red" (https://hmsc.harvard.edu/online-exhibits/cochineal1/mexicos-red/ , fetched): pigment used as early as the 2nd century BC. Indigenous people of Puebla, Tlaxcala and Oaxaca bred cochineal insects to dye cloth and feathers and color manuscripts. After the Spanish invasion it was traded worldwide, an industry "that relied entirely on the expertise and labor of Indigenous Mexicans, though they were never acknowledged for it."
- Zapotec of the Oaxaca valley had owed 20 bags of cochineal every three months in tribute (formerly to the Aztecs). Cochineal became one of New Spain's most valuable exports with silver. Only Seville and Cadiz could import it in the 1500s. (Search summaries of worldhistory.org and others, unconfirmed: search summary only. Not used in outline.)
- NOT USED: any claim that cochineal-dyed cloth reached the English colonies. Not sourced yet.

### Massachusetts sumptuary laws
- Atlas Obscura, "The Hidden Rules of the Puritan Fashion Police" (fetched via curl 2026-09-28): 1634 law prohibited "new fashions, or long hair, or anything of the like nature", no silver or gold hatbands, girdles, belts, no cloth woven with gold thread or lace, no more than two slashes in sleeves, items forfeited. 1639 stricter law against lace, forbade short sleeves. 1650s: only those with more than 200 pounds could wear gold and silver buttons and knee points, great boots, silk hoods, silk scarves. Exempt: magistrates, public officers, wives and children, militia officers and soldiers, those of higher education or employment, those "whose estate have been considerable, though now decayed." 1679: "manifest pride ... some women wearing borders of hair". New Jersey 1670 law on "scents, paints, cosmetics ... high-heeled shoes" (not checked elsewhere, NOT USED). Six years after Lyman, a magistrate charged more women and no one would indict them. ERROR IN THIS SOURCE: it says "colony of Connecticut" for Lyman. Northampton was Massachusetts (Trumbull, American Heritage, Fischer).
- DISAGREEMENT on 1634 slashes: Atlas Obscura "more than two slashes"; Fischer (Albion's Seed, via erenow) "beyond one slash per sleeve". Outline uses Atlas wording. Writer: say "more than one or two slashes, the sources differ" or cite one.
- University of Colorado Denver, Historical Studies Journal vol. 42 (2025), "Threads of Exclusivity: The Moral and Social Implications of Sumptuary Laws in Massachusetts, 1620-1705" (fetched): 1651 law banned persons worth under 200 pounds from "gold or silver lace, buttons, silk hoods or scarves, or great boots", 10 shillings per offense (Shurtleff, Records of the Governor and Company of the Massachusetts Bay, vol. 3, 243-4). From 1652, seven men and four women fined (Records and Files of the Quarterly Courts [Essex County] vol. 1, 270-4). 1653: 18 appeared, 12 discharged as worth over 200 pounds (ibid. 278). 1675 last legislative mention (Shurtleff vol. 5, 59-60).
- James Russell Trumbull, *History of Northampton, Massachusetts, from its settlement in 1654*, vol. 1 (1898), archive.org full text (https://archive.org/stream/historyofnortham00trum/historyofnortham00trum_djvu.txt , downloaded 2026-09-28), pp. 289-292:
  - Footnote quotes the law: "utter detestation & dislike that men or weomen of meane condition, educations and callings, should take vpon them the garbe of gentlemen, by the wearinge of gold or silver lace, or buttons or poynts at theire knees, to walke in greate bootes; or women of the same ranke to weare silke or tiffany hoodes or scarfes, which though allowable to persons of greater estates or more liberall education, yet we cannot but judge it intollerable in persons of such like condition." ### Negro Act of 1740, section XL (clothing)
- Full text read from the transcription PDF "An Act for the Better Ordering and Governing Negroes and Other Slaves in this Province" (https://www.scpronet.com/modjeskaschool/wp-content/uploads/2016/03/9-an-act-for-better-ordering-slavecodes1740.pdf , downloaded 2026-09-28), citing David J. McCord, ed., *The Statutes at Large of South Carolina*, vol. 7 (Columbia, 1840), p. 397. Section XL verbatim:
  "And whereas, many of the slaves in this Province wear clothes much above the condition of slaves, for the procuring whereof they use sinister and evil methods: For the prevention, therefore, of such practices for the future, Be it enacted by the authority aforesaid, That no owner or proprietor of any Negro slave, or other slave, (except livery men and boys,) shall permit or suffer such Negro or other slave, to have or wear any sort of apparel whatsoever, finer, other, or greater value than Negro cloth, duffels, kerseys, osnabrigs, blue linen, check linen or coarse garlix, or calicoes, checked cottons, or Scotch plaids, under the pain of forfeiting all and every such apparel and garment ... and all and every constable and other persons are hereby authorized, empowered, and required, when as often as they shall find any such Negro slave, or other slave, having or wearing any sort of garment or apparel whatsoever, finer, other or of greater value than [the same list], to seize and take away the same, to his or their own use, benefit and behoof ... Provided always, that if any owner ... shall think the garment or apparel of his said slave not liable to forfeiture ... he may [sic, 'not' in transcription] apply to any neighboring justice of the peace, who is hereby authorized and empowered to determine any difference or dispute".
- Actors: the South Carolina assembly (passed it); enforcers: "all and every constable and other persons" (any person could seize and keep the clothes).
- Date 10 May 1740 and link to Stono (Sept 1739): from `music` and `slavery-freedom` outlines (their banks source it). Wikipedia "Negro Act of 1740" exists.
- Wikipedia "Negro cloth" (fetched): coarse durable fabric for enslaved people's clothing, cotton or coarse blends, imported from Europe (Wales) in the 1700s to 1800s, later made in Lowell, Mass. ("Lowell cloth"). Says a 1735 Negro Act specified "Negro cloth, duffels, course kiersies, osnaburg, blue linen, checked linen, coarse calicoes and checked kinds of cotton." A formerly enslaved person described it as like "needles sticking one all the time" (19th century, era 06/07 material).
- Fabric definitions (Wikipedia, fetched): Osnaburg: "coarse, plain-weave fabric" first made from flax in Osnabrück, Germany, later in Scotland from tow or jute. Kersey: "coarse woollen cloth", thick and sturdy, twill, from Kersey, Suffolk. Duffel: "heavy woolen cloth" for blankets and overcoats, from Duffel, Belgium. Garlix (Silesian linen): not looked up.

### Indigo

### L-85 and wartime clothing
- Jennifer M. ### Zoot suit attacks, June 1943

## Era 09: 1950-2000

### Teenagers, jeans and the 1950s look
- FIT Fashion History Timeline, "1950-1959" (Karina Reddy, 2019, fashionhistory.fitnyc.edu/1950-1959/): Dior's "New Look" (February 1947, nipped-in waist, full skirt) stayed the leading women's style until the mid-1950s. The silhouette then got straighter and slimmer "as fashion began to look to the new 'teenager' for inspiration." Men's and boys' clothes grew more casual.
- Same page: Marlon Brando in *The Wild One* (1953) and James Dean in *Rebel Without a Cause* (1955) made "the white t-shirt, jeans, and greased back hair" common among young men in the 1950s. "Young men in the US began adopting a working-class look of jeans, a white t-shirt, and leather jacket." FIT calls it "a youthful rejection of their parents' generation" that "earned them a rebellious and trouble-making reputation." In the 1950s "it became increasingly acceptable for boys to wear jeans for most occasions." New synthetic materials were used in children's clothes "as they were easier to launder."

### Polyester (Dacron) and wash-and-wear
- Hagley Museum and Library, "This week marks a milestone in the history of textiles" (Hagley Vault, May 8, 2023): on May 8, 1951, the world's first commercially marketed polyester fiber went on sale in men's suits sold by Hart, Schaffner & Marx of New York. The cloth, made by Deering, Milliken & Co., was 45% worsted wool and 55% DuPont's polyethylene terephthalate, trademarked Dacron in the US (Terylene in England). Makers liked it for "its resistance to creasing, chafing, and ease of cleaning." DuPont developed it in a pilot plant in Seaford, Delaware, and built the Kinston, North Carolina plant in 1953.
- Wikipedia "Polyester": John Rex Whinfield and James Tennant Dickson patented polyethylene terephthalate in 1941 (in Britain). DuPont bought the US rights from Imperial Chemical Industries in 1946.
- NCpedia, "Ardathy Spikes: Somewhere like DuPont" (Listening to History, interview by Lu Ann Jones, 2005): the Kinston plant opened in 1953 as "the world's first commercial polyester plant." "Dacron made possible the first 'wash and wear' clothes, saving mostly the nation's women hours of ironing or pressing every week." Spikes, born 1939 in Craven County to a sharecropping family, worked there 36 years. (Possible ordinary voice for `work-workers`/`technology`, not used here.)
- FIT, "1970-1979": synthetic fabrics "meant that fashionable styles could be bought at any price point. So pervasive were these materials that the seventies became known as the 'Polyester Decade.'" Leisure suits were "often made of synthetic fabrics and seen as a cheap alternative" to the suit. Disco evening wear used Lycra, satin, velvet and sequins. Late 1970s: athletic wear as everyday wear (Norma Kamali: sweatshirt fabric, leggings).

### Mid-century modern: Charles and Ray Eames (fills target mid-century-designer)
- Charles Ormond Eames Jr. was born June 17, 1907, in St. Louis and died August 21, 1978, in St. Louis. He studied architecture at Washington University in St. Louis for two years and left (Wikipedia, "Charles Eames"). Ray-Bernice Alexandra Kaiser was born December 15, 1912, in Sacramento, California, and died August 21, 1988, in Los Angeles, ten years to the day after Charles. She trained as a painter (Wikipedia, "Ray Eames": "Artist with Allied Artists Association, Hoffmann Studio"). They married in June 1941 and moved to Los Angeles (Wikipedia, "Charles and Ray Eames").
- Late 1941: they made molded plywood leg splints for wounded servicemen, with Navy money (Wikipedia, "Charles and Ray Eames").
- 1945-1946: molded plywood chairs, the LCW and DCW (same).
- 1948-1950: the Museum of Modern Art's "International Competition for Low-Cost Furniture Design" (1948) was "motivated by the urgent need in the post-war period for low-cost housing and furnishing designs adaptable to small housing units." The Eameses entered one-piece stamped-steel shells and took second place (Eames Office, "Molded Shell Chairs", eamesoffice.com). Herman Miller would not pay $80,000 for a steel stamping press, so the Eameses turned to fiberglass, a wartime material (Eames Office). Zenith Plastics of Los Angeles molded the shells from polyester resin reinforced with glass fiber on hydraulic presses, and Herman Miller sold them from 1950 (Wikipedia, "Eames Fiberglass Armchair". Eames Office). First colors: greige, elephant-hide gray and parchment (Wikipedia). "The first ever mass-produced chairs made of plastic" (Vitra, via search summary, unconfirmed: search summary only). Wikipedia: "the first one-piece plastic chair whose surface was left uncovered and not upholstered." Treat any "first" with care.
- Their stated goal: "Getting the most of the best to the greatest number of people for the least" (Wikipedia, "Eames Fiberglass Armchair", quoting the Eameses). Eames Office: "If they could make good chairs out of fewer parts, better-quality chairs could be sold for lower prices." Charles: a "chair has essentially two parts, one, a surface to receive the body, and two, a structure to hold that surface in proper relation to the ground."
- Eames House (Case Study House No. 8), Pacific Palisades, California, built February to December 1949. They moved in on Christmas Eve 1949 (Wikipedia, "Charles and Ray Eames").
- Eames Lounge Chair (670) and Ottoman (671), 1956, for Herman Miller. Molded plywood (five thin layers, Brazilian rosewood veneer until the early 1990s) and leather. "The first chair the Eameses designed for the high-end market." Ray called it "comfortable and un-designy" in a letter. Charles wanted "the warm, receptive look of a well-used first baseman's mitt." The first 1956 label read "designed by Charles Eames" (Wikipedia, "Eames Lounge Chair").
- Credit: "During her lifetime, Ray Kaiser Eames received less credit than she has been given posthumously in art and design literature, museum shows, and documentary films" (Wikipedia, "Ray Eames").
- Movie: *Eames: The Architect and the Painter* (2011), PBS American Masters, aired November 18, 2011 (Wikipedia, "Charles and Ray Eames"). A documentary about them.

### Ann Lowe and the 1953 wedding gown (story ann-lowe lives in era 08)
- Wikipedia "Ann Lowe": born December 14, 1898, Clayton, Alabama. Died February 25, 1981, Queens. In 1953 Janet Lee Auchincloss hired Lowe to make the wedding dress of her daughter Jacqueline Bouvier and the bridal attendants' dresses for her September 1953 wedding to Senator John F. Kennedy. ### School dress and hair: the courts
- **Tinker v. Des Moines Independent Community School District, 393 U.S. 503 (1969)** (opinion text, Cornell LII, law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/393/503. Wikipedia): John F. Tinker, 15, and Christopher Eckhardt, 16, went to high schools in Des Moines, Iowa. Mary Beth Tinker, 13, was in junior high. In December 1965 a group of adults and students met at the Eckhardt home and decided to wear black armbands through the holidays and fast on December 16 and New Year's Eve, to object to the Vietnam War and support a truce. On December 14, 1965, the Des Moines principals adopted a rule that a student wearing an armband would be asked to remove it and suspended if he refused. On December 16 Mary Beth and Christopher wore armbands. John wore his the next day. All three were sent home and suspended and did not come back until after New Year's Day (opinion).
- Wikipedia adds: Hope Tinker (11) and Paul Tinker (8) also wore armbands at elementary schools, where the rule did not apply. Their mother Lorena led a Des Moines peace group. The Iowa Civil Liberties Union took the case (lawyer Dan Johnston). The family received hate mail and death threats. District court for the school (1966). The 8th Circuit split evenly (1967). Argued November 12, 1968. Decided February 24, 1969, 7 to 2. Justice Abe Fortas wrote for the majority. Justices Hugo Black and John Harlan dissented.
- Fortas: students do not "shed their constitutional rights to freedom of speech or expression at the schoolhouse gate." And: "The problem posed by the present case does not relate to regulation of the length of skirts or the type of clothing, to hair style, or deportment" (opinion, Cornell LII). So Tinker protected the armband as speech and left clothing and hair rules open.
- **Ferrell v. Dallas Independent School District, 392 F.2d 697 (5th Cir. 1968)** (OpenJurist): Phillip Ferrell, Stephen Webb and Paul Jarvis, members of a band called "Sounds Unlimited", were refused enrollment at W. W. Samuell High School in Dallas because of their "Beatle" type haircuts. Their parents sued on September 12, 1966. The district court and the 5th Circuit sided with the school. The principal said their hair would cause "commotion, trouble, distraction and a disturbance" (search summary of the opinion. The OpenJurist page confirms names, school, band and dates).
- **Karr v. Schmidt** (5th Cir. 1972): in 1970 Chesley Karr challenged his El Paso, Texas, high school's rule that a boy's hair could not hang over the ears or the top of a dress-shirt collar. The 5th Circuit ruled students had no constitutional right to wear their hair as they liked (First Amendment Encyclopedia, "Hair Length and Style", Ruth Ann Strickland, MTSU). In 1971 Justice Hugo Black refused the students' emergency request and wrote: "The only thing about it that borders on the serious to me is the idea that anyone should think the Federal Constitution imposes on the United States courts the burden of supervising the length of hair that public school students should wear" (SCOTUSblog, Kelsey Dallas, "A brief history of Supreme Court rulings on hair", July 24, 2025).
- The 7th Circuit ruled for boys with long hair in Arnold v. Carpenter (1972). The 5th Circuit ruled against. The Supreme Court never took a school hair case. Justice William O. Douglas dissented from those refusals, including Olff v. East Side Union High School District (1972) (First Amendment Encyclopedia. SCOTUSblog).
- **New Rider v. Board of Education, Pawnee County, Oklahoma** (10th Cir. 1973. cert. denied 414 U.S. 1097, December 10, 1973, Douglas dissenting with Marshall) (OpenJurist, text of the Douglas dissent): the students were Pawnee boys at Pawnee Junior High School. Plaintiff named first: Norman New Rider, a minor, through his mother Wilma Williams (case caption, Justia via search summary. The other boys' names were not found). They wore their hair parted in the middle with a long braid on each side, to follow "the old traditional ways" and because it was "one way of telling people that I am proud [to be an Indian]." They were suspended indefinitely on April 24, 1972, under a rule that hair could not reach the shirt collar or ears. The 10th Circuit upheld the rule as a way of "instilling pride and initiative among the students." Douglas: they "were in fact attempting to broadcast a clear and specific message to their fellow students and others, their pride in being Indian" (dissent. The em dash in the original is replaced here by a comma).
- **Kelley v. Johnson** (1976): 6 to 2, the Supreme Court upheld a Suffolk County, New York, police rule on hair, sideburns and mustache length. Thurgood Marshall dissented (SCOTUSblog).

### The Afro and natural hair
- Wikipedia, "Afro": before the 1960s many Black Americans straightened their hair, often with relaxers containing lye, which could burn the scalp and ears if not applied carefully. In the 1960s the "Black is beautiful" movement brought "a return to more natural, untreated hairstyles." The Afro "became a powerful political symbol which reflected black pride." Men and women wore it, and it could be kept up at home without costly salon visits. Angela Davis is pictured with an Afro at UCLA in October 1969.
- NMAAHC, "Black is Beautiful: The Emergence of Black Culture and Identity in the 60s and 70s": the movement "affirmed natural hairstyles like the 'Afro'" (search summary only. The Smithsonian site returned "temporarily unavailable" and 403 on 2026-09-28. Unconfirmed: search summary only).
- **Rogers v. American Airlines, 527 F. Supp. 229 (S.D.N.Y., December 1, 1981)** (Leagle, first page of the opinion. Wikipedia): Renee Rogers, a Black woman and an airport operations agent for American Airlines, sued over the airline's rule barring employees in her job from wearing an all-braided hairstyle. She sought $10,000 in damages. The airline had told her to wear a hairpiece over her braids at work (Wikipedia and search summaries). Judge Abraham Sofaer ruled for the airline. He held that a braided style is something a person can change, unlike hair texture, so the rule was not race or sex discrimination under Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 or 42 U.S.C. 1981. The airline argued the style was popularized by Bo Derek in the 1979 film *10*. Rogers said braids had historical meaning for Black women (Wikipedia). Correction note: Wikipedia's "Decision" section says "Thirteenth Amendment", which conflicts with its own lead and with every other source (Title VII and section 1981). Use Title VII.
- Wikipedia (Rogers) quotes a later ruling: "Black women may change their hair texture to make it straight, wear a weave, or wear a wig. However, every other hairstyle involving Black women's natural texture (outside the afro) may be banned." Its source is not stated in the extract read. Not used.

### El Monte, 1995: clothes sewn by enslaved workers in California
- On August 2, 1995, state and federal agents and El Monte police raided a fenced seven-unit apartment complex on Santa Anita Avenue in El Monte, near Los Angeles, and freed 72 Thai garment workers held there (Erin Blakemore, "20th-Century Slavery in a California Sweatshop Was Hiding in Plain Sight", *Smithsonian Magazine*, July 31, 2020. Elson Trinidad, "20 Years Later, Thai Ex-Sweatshop Workers Reflect on Freedom From Slavery", PBS SoCal, August 11, 2015. Wikipedia, "El Monte Thai garment slavery case").
- Operators: Suni Manasurangkun, called "Auntie", and her sons. Recruiters in Thailand promised jobs, gave workers false passports, and took their passports on arrival. Workers owed about $5,000 (120,000 baht) for the trip (Smithsonian Mag. PBS SoCal). A high fence and a razor-wire enclosure surrounded the buildings. An armed guard sat in the courtyard (Smithsonian Mag). Wikipedia: two guards armed with guns, knives and baseball bats, 24 hours a day. Guards showed workers a picture of the last man who tried to escape, who had been beaten (Wikipedia).
- Hours and pay: 84 hours a week, early morning to midnight, for about $1.60 an hour (PBS SoCal). "Seventeen to twenty-two hours a day" (Wikipedia). Rotchana Cheunchujit was paid five to seven cents a garment, about $300 to $500 a month, and the owners took half her wages up front and made workers buy food at their own store at high prices (Smithsonian Mag). Some workers had not been outside the fence in as many as seven years (PBS SoCal).
- How it was found: a woman escaped through a second-story window. Her story reached California Deputy Labor Commissioner T. K. Kim. A two-page pencil letter with a hand-drawn map ("Very Dangerous. Please bring much manpower") reached the authorities (Smithsonian Mag. Wikipedia).
- After the raid, immigration officers held the workers for about nine days until Asian American activists bailed them out (Smithsonian Mag). Chanchanit Martorell of the Thai Community Development Center helped them (PBS SoCal. Smithsonian Mag).
- Punishment: in February 1996 Suni Manasurangkun, three of her five sons and three others pleaded guilty to federal crimes. Two sons fled and were never caught (Smithsonian Mag). Sentences: up to seven years (PBS SoCal). Suni seven years, sons Wirachai, Surachai and Phanasak six years (search summary of WWD, unconfirmed: search summary only).
- Whose clothes: the garments carried "Made in the USA" labels and were sold at chains including Miller's Outpost, Mervyn's and Montgomery Ward (Smithsonian Mag). In 1997 retailers settled civil suits for more than $4.5 million in back pay (Smithsonian Mag). Wikipedia says a $4 million settlement "from the retailers and manufacturers." Lead lawyer Julie Su (Wikipedia). Record both figures.
- Rotchana Cheunchujit (now Rotchana Sussman): 24 at the raid, sewed blouses and other garments. She became a US citizen, learned English, reunited with her children, became an advocate for workers and opened a vegetarian Thai restaurant in Arcadia, California (Smithsonian Mag, 2020). She told the magazine: "We were in shock. We didn't know what was going on." PBS SoCal (2015) names two more workers: Bunta Boonprasit (54 in 2015, three years in the compound) and Kitcha Phimonsing (59 in 2015).
- The case led to the Coalition to Abolish Slavery and Trafficking (1998) and the Victims of Trafficking and Violence Protection Act of 2000, which created the T visa (Smithsonian Mag). Wikipedia calls it "the first recognized case of modern-day slavery in the United States since the abolition of slavery". Smithsonian Mag calls it "the first modern human trafficking case." Unsupported "first": attribute or leave out.

### Where American clothes are made
- "About 97% of the clothes and shoes purchased in the U.S. are imported" (American Apparel & Footwear Association, 2024, as quoted by Axios, April 3, 2025, and 7News Miami. Unconfirmed: search summary only. Axios returned 403).

### Tazreen Fashions fire, November 24, 2012
- Wikipedia, "2012 Dhaka garment factory fire": the fire broke out on November 24, 2012, in the Tazreen Fashions factory in Ashulia, on the edge of Dhaka, Bangladesh. Deaths 117 to 124 (infobox). "At least 117" confirmed dead (text). AP in the *Los Angeles Times*, November 25, 2012: 112. Over 200 injured. All three staircases of the nine-story building led through the ground floor, where the fire started in a warehouse. At least 69 bodies were found on the second floor. Twelve people died jumping from windows. Three supervisors were arrested on November 28, 2012, accused by police of padlocking exits. Survivor Mohammad Ripu said a manager told workers "The fire alarm had just gone out of order. Go back to work." Police sought the owner, Delwar Hossain, in December 2013.
- The factory made clothes for companies including Walmart, C&A and Li & Fung (and items for the US Marines). A Walmart ethical-sourcing audit had given the factory an "orange" (high-risk) rating in May 2011. Walmart said "A supplier subcontracted work to this factory without authorization and in direct violation of our policies" and ended the relationship. Walmart and Sears declined to send representatives to a Geneva compensation meeting (Wikipedia).
- Walmart's director of ethical sourcing, Sridevi Kalavakolanu, said in 2011 that fire and electrical upgrades were "not financially feasible for the brands" (Wikipedia, citing Steven Greenhouse, *New York Times*, December 5, 2012).

### Rana Plaza, April 24, 2013
- The nine-story Rana Plaza building in Savar, near Dhaka, held five garment factories employing about 5,000 people, plus shops and a bank. It was built in 2006 on a filled-in pond without proper permits. The upper floors were added without a permit and were not built to carry factory machines and generators (Wikipedia, "Rana Plaza collapse").
- On April 23, 2013, a TV channel showed cracks in the building. The shops and bank closed. The owner, Sohel Rana, told the media the building was "safe" and workers "should return." Managers at the Ether Tex factory threatened to withhold a month's pay from workers who refused. The building fell at about 9 a.m. on April 24 (Wikipedia. Clean Clothes Campaign: "Garment workers were ordered to return to work the following day").
- Deaths, by source: 1,134 (Wikipedia, Clean Clothes Campaign). 1,136 (*The Business Standard*, Dhaka, April 24, 2026: 1,117 bodies recovered, 19 more died in hospitals). 1,129 (AP in *Dallas News*, 2015). 1,132 (Business and Human Rights Resource Centre, search summary). "Over 1,100" (ILO, 2023). Injured: about 2,500 (Wikipedia), "nearly 2600" (Clean Clothes Campaign), 1,524 injured of 2,438 rescued alive, 78 permanently disabled (*The Business Standard*, 2026). Some survivors were freed only by amputating limbs (Clean Clothes Campaign).
- Reshma Begum was pulled out alive on May 10, 2013, 17 days after the collapse (Wikipedia, citing NYT and BBC). Wikipedia notes some outlets called the rescue a hoax. Not used.
- American brands linked: the Clean Clothes Campaign and Bangladeshi partners "identified at least 29 global brands that had recent or current orders with at least one of the five garment factories", including Cato Fashions (USA) and The Children's Place (USA). Wikipedia lists Walmart among the brands whose clothes were made there, citing news reports. J.C. Penney said the factories were not its suppliers but that one factory had made some clothes for a national brand, "a small portion of that was for Penney stores" (*Dallas News*, AP, April 29-30, 2015).
- Lawsuit: on July 21, 2015, Abdur Rahaman (for Sharifa Begum, who died) and Mahamudul Hasan Hridoy sued J.C. Penney, The Children's Place and Walmart in Delaware for negligence and wrongful death. On May 4, 2016, Judge Mary M. Johnston dismissed the case under Bangladesh's one-year time limit for such suits (search summary of Business and Human Rights Resource Centre and CourtListener, unconfirmed: search summary only. The CourtListener opinion page returned no text). An earlier suit in federal court in Washington also named the government of Bangladesh (*Dallas News*, 2015).
- Safety agreements: the Accord on Fire and Building Safety in Bangladesh (May 2013), legally binding, signed by more than 200 companies, mostly European (Clean Clothes Campaign. Wikipedia). Walmart and 14 other North American companies did not sign by the deadline. On July 10, 2013, 17 North American retailers including Walmart, Gap, Target and Macy's announced their own plan (the Alliance for Bangladesh Worker Safety), which had no legally binding commitment to pay for repairs (Wikipedia, citing NYT). J.C. Penney called itself a founding member of the Alliance (*Dallas News*).
- Compensation: families were owed $30 million by an ILO-convention calculation. The fund reached that target only after more than two years, when the last brands, such as Benetton, paid in. "Some brands linked to the disaster even never paid up" (Clean Clothes Campaign). By October 2015, over $34 million had been paid (ILO, 2023). Walmart was among companies that refused to sign the compensation agreement in November 2013 (Wikipedia).
- Punishment, current to April 2026 (*The Business Standard*, April 24, 2026. Wikipedia, "Sohel Rana (businessman)", edited September 2026): 41 people were charged in the murder case, with 594 listed witnesses. Only 145 had testified by April 2026. Sohel Rana has been in custody since his arrest near the Indian border on April 28 or 29, 2013 (sources differ by a day). His only completed case: in 2017 he was sentenced to three years for hiding his wealth. Trials in five cases were still unfinished after 13 years. Others accused are on bail or have fled.

### Los Angeles garment work and the Garment Worker Protection Act
- Nigel Duara, "Bill's defeat keeps undocumented garment workers in meager wages", *CalMatters*, September 14, 2020: Los Angeles garment workers are paid "about 5 cents to 12 cents per piece of clothing," work five and a half days a week, 10 hours a day, and take home about $300 a week, "or $5.50 per hour, paid in cash." UCLA Labor Center's Janna Shadduck-Hernández: per-piece rates of "less than 12 cents." An outbreak at the Los Angeles Apparel factory infected at least 375 workers in 2020, and four died.
- Knock LA, "Garment Workers Celebrate Historic Legislative Win" (2021): LA garment workers average $5 an hour and as little as $1.25, in a $5 billion industry, when the LA minimum wage was $15. Rates of "two or three cents per piece" were "unchanged for decades."
- SB 62, the Garment Worker Protection Act: signed by Governor Gavin Newsom on September 27, 2021 (Sen. María Elena Durazo press release). In force January 1, 2022: garment factories must pay at least the minimum wage by the hour, piece rates allowed only as a bonus, and brands and retailers can be held jointly liable for unpaid wages (Remake FAQ. Durazo release). Garment Worker Center director Marissa Nuncio: "The 45,000+ garment workers in our state, a majority of them are immigrant women" (Durazo release). Knock LA calls it "the first in the country to ban piece-rate wages" (advocacy source, a "first": attribute).
- Remake (advocacy group, FAQ, 2023): after one year some workers were being paid minimum wage, "three times higher than many workers were getting paid beforehand, yet others are still facing wage theft" (citing the Garment Worker Center).

### Hair: the CROWN Act and the courts
- California: the CROWN Act (Creating a Respectful and Open World for Natural Hair), written by State Senator Holly J. It added hair texture and protective styles such as braids, locs and twists to the state's anti-discrimination law for jobs and K-12 schools (Wikipedia, "CROWN Act of 2022". - Andrew Johnson: on December 19, 2018, at a home match in Buena, New Jersey, referee Alan Maloney told Andrew Johnson, a junior wrestler at Buena Regional High School, to cut his dreadlocks or forfeit. Maloney rejected the cover Johnson wore over his hair and gave him 90 seconds. The team's athletic trainer, a white staff member, cut his hair beside the mat as the crowd watched. - New Jersey's CROWN Act was signed by Governor Phil Murphy on December 19, 2019, one year after the match, and took effect at once. New Jersey was the third state, after California and New York (Jackson Lewis, December 27, 2019).
- Federal CROWN Act: passed the House on September 21, 2020 (voice vote) and again on March 18, 2022 (235 to 189). It never got a Senate vote (Wikipedia, "CROWN Act of 2022"). No federal law as of 2026-09-28.
- State count, dated: 27 states plus Washington, D.C., as of July 2025 (GovDocs, "States with Hair Discrimination (CROWN) Laws", updated July 2025, lists them).
