# Research Notes: Chapter 1, Exploration and Discovery

Verified facts in plain words, following the writing style guide. Organized by the seven time sections, plus sections on territories and the wider world. Dates and details checked against sources including Britannica, NASA, NOAA, the National Park Service, the Smithsonian, university history sites, and state history archives.

## Before the 1500s

- Native peoples were the first to reach and explore this land. They crossed from Asia during the last ice age, when a land bridge joined Siberia and Alaska. Evidence places people here at least 15,000 years ago. Footprints at White Sands, New Mexico, dated by several methods, indicate people were present about 23,000 years ago. Some researchers question that early date.
- Over thousands of years they spread across the whole continent and learned every kind of land: deserts, mountains, forests, coasts, and plains.
- They built long trade routes. Copper from the Great Lakes, shells from the Gulf coast, and obsidian and other goods moved hundreds of miles between peoples.
- The Norse reached North America around the year 1000, about 500 years before Columbus. Leif Erikson, son of Erik the Red of Greenland, led a group to a place he named Vinland (wild grapes grew there). Archaeologists found the site in 1960 at L'Anse aux Meadows in Newfoundland and dated it to about 1000. The Norse stayed about ten years, fought with local people they called Skraelings, and left. They built no permanent settlement, and the visit had no lasting effect on the continent.

## The 1490s and 1500s (European arrival and first crossings)

- Christopher Columbus (1451 to 1506): Italian from Genoa, sailed for Spain. Reached an island in the Bahamas on October 12, 1492. Made four voyages (1492 to 1502). Explored the Caribbean and touched Central and South America but never reached what is now the United States. Died still believing he had found a route to Asia.
- The name "America": Amerigo Vespucci, an Italian navigator, sailed along South America around 1500 and argued it was a new continent, not the edge of Asia. In 1507 the German mapmaker Martin Waldseemüller printed a world map that labeled the new land "America" after Vespucci's first name. The name stuck.
- John Cabot (about 1450 to 1499): Italian (Giovanni Caboto), sailed for England under King Henry VII. Reached the coast near Newfoundland on June 24, 1497, in a small ship called the Matthew with about 18 men. Claimed the land for England. Found cod so thick the crew reportedly scooped them up in baskets. Vanished at sea on his next voyage in 1498. His trip became England's basis for later claims.
- Juan Ponce de León (1474 to 1521): Spanish. Reached Florida in 1513 and named it. First documented European to set foot on what is now the U.S. mainland. Returned in 1521 to build a colony and was killed by a Native arrow.
- Cabeza de Vaca and Estevanico (1528 to 1536): survivors of a failed Spanish expedition. Shipwrecked near Texas, then walked thousands of miles across the Southwest to Mexico, living among Native peoples. Estevanico was an enslaved North African and one of the first Africans to explore the region. He later helped guide expeditions and was killed in 1539 at a Zuni village.
- Hernando de Soto (about 1500 to 1542): Spanish. Marched through the Southeast from 1539 to 1542, fighting and enslaving Native peoples along the way. Among the first Europeans to cross the Mississippi River. Died on its banks.
- Francisco Vázquez de Coronado (1510 to 1554): Spanish. Searched the Southwest and Great Plains from 1540 to 1542 for cities of gold that did not exist. Reached Kansas. His men were the first Europeans to see the Grand Canyon.
- Giovanni da Verrazzano (1524): Italian sailing for France. First European to sail the Atlantic coast from the Carolinas to Maine and to enter New York Harbor.
- Jacques Cartier (1530s): French. Explored the Gulf of St. Lawrence and the St. Lawrence River. Basis for French claims in Canada.

## The 1600s (following the rivers inland)

- Henry Hudson (1609): English, sailing for the Dutch. Explored the river now named after him. His reports led to the Dutch claim on the area. On a later trip for England he searched for a northern passage, and his crew set him adrift in a mutiny.
- Samuel de Champlain (early 1600s): French. Founded Quebec in 1608 and explored the Great Lakes region. Lake Champlain is named for him.
- Jacques Marquette and Louis Jolliet (1673): a French priest and a trader who paddled down the Mississippi River. They went as far as the Arkansas River, confirmed the river flowed toward the Gulf of Mexico rather than the Pacific, and turned back to avoid Spanish territory. Jolliet later lost his notes when his canoe overturned near Montreal, so Marquette's account became the record.
- Robert de La Salle (1682): traveled the full length of the Mississippi to the Gulf of Mexico, the first European to do so. On April 9, 1682, he claimed the whole river basin for France and named it Louisiana, after King Louis XIV. His second expedition, in 1684, aimed to build a colony at the river's mouth but overshot and landed at Matagorda Bay in present-day Texas. The colony failed, and in 1687 his own men killed him. His 1682 claim later gave the United States a basis for buying the Louisiana Territory in 1803.

## The 1700s (the Pacific coast, Alaska, and past the mountains)

- Vitus Bering (1741): led a Russian expedition that reached Alaska. The Bering Strait is named for him. He died of scurvy on the return.
- Portolá expedition (1769): a Spanish party led by Gaspar de Portolá that traveled overland through California and found San Francisco Bay.
- Daniel Boone (1770s): explored and marked a route through the Cumberland Gap into Kentucky, called the Wilderness Road, opening the way for settlers into the interior.

## The 1800s (exploring the West)

- Lewis and Clark (1804 to 1806): Meriwether Lewis and William Clark led the Corps of Discovery, about four dozen men, on a round trip of nearly 8,000 miles from near St. Louis to the Pacific and back. President Thomas Jefferson sent them to explore the new Louisiana Purchase, look for a water route to the Pacific, record the land, plants, animals, and peoples, and open trade with Native nations. They traveled up the Missouri River by keelboat and canoe and wintered at Fort Mandan (in present-day North Dakota) among the Mandan and Hidatsa. There they hired Toussaint Charbonneau and his young Shoshone wife, Sacagawea, who gave birth to a son, Jean Baptiste, and carried him the whole way. When the group reached Shoshone country, Sacagawea's brother Cameahwait supplied the horses they needed to cross the mountains. A Shoshone guide, Old Toby, and later Nez Perce guides helped them over the Bitterroot Range and down the rivers to the coast. They reached the Pacific in November 1805 and built Fort Clatsop near present-day Astoria, Oregon. When they voted on where to spend the winter, York, an enslaved man owned by Clark, and Sacagawea both took part in the vote. Only one man died on the whole trip: Sergeant Charles Floyd, in August 1804, most likely from a burst appendix. The single deadly clash with Native people came on the return, in July 1806, when Lewis's party killed two Blackfeet men caught taking guns and horses.
- Zebulon Pike (1806 to 1807): explored the southern part of the Louisiana Purchase. He sighted the Colorado peak now called Pikes Peak but never climbed it. Spanish soldiers arrested his party in their territory and later released them.
- The mountain men (1820s to 1840s): fur trappers who found the trails later used by settlers. In 1822 William Ashley ran a newspaper ad in St. Louis for "enterprising young men" to join the fur trade. The men who answered, sometimes called "Ashley's Hundred," became the mountain men. Each summer they met at a "rendezvous" to trade furs and supplies.
  - Jedediah Smith (1799 to 1831): the most-traveled mountain man of his era. In spring 1824 his party rediscovered South Pass, a low pass across the Rocky Mountains in Wyoming that wagons and livestock could cross. (Others had used it around 1812, then it was forgotten. The Crow people told Smith how to find it.) This route later became the main section of the Oregon Trail. A grizzly attack tore off much of his scalp and one ear, which a companion sewed back on. He was killed in 1831 on the Santa Fe Trail.
  - Jim Bridger (1804 to 1881): mountain man and scout. In fall 1824 he rode a small buffalo-skin boat down the Bear River and became the first white man to see the Great Salt Lake. He tasted the salty water and thought he had reached the Pacific Ocean. In 1843 he built Fort Bridger on the Green River, a resupply stop on the Oregon, California, and Mormon trails.
  - Hugh Glass (about 1783 to 1833): a trapper mauled by a grizzly and left for dead by two companions. He survived and crawled and walked about 200 miles to Fort Kiowa. His story is the basis for the film "The Revenant."
  - Jim Beckwourth (about 1798 to 1866): a Black mountain man born into slavery in Virginia and later freed. He joined Ashley's fur company, trapped across the Rockies, and lived for years among the Crow Nation, who made him a war leader. In 1850 he improved a Native trail into what became Beckwourth Pass, a low route through the Sierra Nevada that saved travelers to California about 150 miles. The nearby town promised to pay him and never did, and as a Black man he could not sue. He dictated his life story to a writer, the first published account by an American mountain man.
- John C. Frémont, "The Pathfinder" (1813 to 1890): an Army mapmaker in the Corps of Topographical Engineers. Led survey expeditions from 1842 to 1846 with the scout Kit Carson as guide. He mapped the Oregon Trail, named and mapped the Great Basin, and charted the route to California. He was the first American to see Lake Tahoe. His wife Jessie Benton Frémont helped write and edit the reports. Congress printed 10,000 copies of his report in 1845, and newspapers reprinted parts of it. Families used the reports and maps as guides for the trip west. His account of the Salt Lake Valley influenced Brigham Young's choice to settle there. In 1856 he became the first presidential candidate of the anti-slavery Republican Party.
- The Mormon pioneers (1847): thousands of members of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints (Mormons) migrated west together to find a place to practice their religion safely. After their founder Joseph Smith was killed in Illinois in 1844, Brigham Young led the first group (about 144 to 148 people) along the Mormon Trail to the Salt Lake Valley, then Mexican territory. They entered the valley July 22 to 24, 1847. Young, sick with "mountain fever," reportedly said, "This is the right place" (the words were written down years later by Wilford Woodruff). More than 10,000 followers came after them. The pioneers began digging irrigation ditches and planting crops within days. Utah marks July 24 as Pioneer Day. The Ute, Goshute, Shoshone, Paiute, and other peoples already lived in the region.
- Gold Rush prospectors (1848 to 1849): tens of thousands searched unmapped country for gold after the 1848 strike in California. (This chapter covers the searching. The crowds settling and the money go in other chapters.)
- The four government surveys of the West (late 1860s and 1870s): after the Civil War, the government paid four teams to map the western territories. Clarence King mapped along the 40th parallel; Ferdinand Hayden mapped Colorado, Wyoming, and the Yellowstone region; John Wesley Powell mapped the Colorado River and Grand Canyon country; and Army lieutenant George Wheeler mapped the land west of the 100th meridian. Hayden's 1871 survey brought back photographs by William Henry Jackson and paintings by Thomas Moran that helped convince Congress to set aside Yellowstone as the first national park in 1872.
- John Wesley Powell (1869): a one-armed Civil War veteran who led the first boat trip down the Colorado River through the Grand Canyon, running rapids no one had mapped. He later led one of the four government surveys and became head of the U.S. Geological Survey.

## The 1900s (the poles, the sky, space, and the wider world)

- Robert Peary and Matthew Henson (1909): reached the North Pole. Henson, a Black explorer, was central to the team but received little public credit for decades.
- Richard Byrd (1888 to 1957): a Navy officer and aviator. On November 29, 1929, he and three others made the first flight over the South Pole, from a base camp on the Ross Ice Shelf called "Little America." He led five Antarctic expeditions, and his planes mapped large stretches of the continent. He also claimed a 1926 flight over the North Pole, which other researchers dispute.
- Charles Lindbergh (1927): first solo nonstop flight across the Atlantic Ocean.
- Amelia Earhart (1932): first woman to fly solo nonstop across the Atlantic. Vanished in 1937 trying to fly around the world.
- Deep ocean (1960): U.S. Navy Lieutenant Don Walsh and the Swiss engineer Jacques Piccard rode the bathyscaphe Trieste down to the Challenger Deep in the Mariana Trench near Guam, the deepest known point in the ocean, about 35,800 feet down. They saw a fish there, which showed that life can survive under that much pressure.
- Americans exploring the wider world: Hiram Bingham (1911) brought the Inca city of Machu Picchu in Peru to the world's attention; a local guide led him to it, and local people already knew the site. Roy Chapman Andrews (1920s) led expeditions into the Gobi Desert of Mongolia, where his team found the first widely recognized dinosaur eggs. Both are often named as inspirations for the film character Indiana Jones. Robert Ballard (1985) found the wreck of the Titanic on the deep-sea floor using underwater robots.
- Space (the journeys; the technology goes in the Science chapter): the Soviet Union launched the first satellite, Sputnik, in 1957, and the United States created NASA in 1958. Alan Shepard became the first American in space on May 5, 1961. John Glenn became the first American to orbit the Earth on February 20, 1962. A launchpad fire killed three astronauts during a test in 1967. In December 1968, Apollo 8 carried the first people to orbit the Moon. On July 20, 1969, Apollo 11 landed and Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin walked on the Moon while Michael Collins orbited above. Five more Apollo crews landed on the Moon through 1972. The Space Shuttle carried crews from 1981 to 2011; two shuttles were lost, Challenger in 1986 and Columbia in 2003, killing both crews. Astronauts have lived aboard the International Space Station continuously since 2000.

## The 2000s (robots, telescopes, and mapping the whole world)

- Mars rovers: robot vehicles have explored the surface of Mars. Curiosity landed in 2012, and Perseverance landed in Jezero Crater in February 2021 to search for signs of ancient life and collect rock samples. Perseverance carried a small helicopter, Ingenuity, the first aircraft to fly on another planet.
- New Horizons: launched in 2006, it made the first close flyby of Pluto on July 14, 2015, then flew past a more distant object, Arrokoth, in 2019.
- Voyager 1 and Voyager 2: launched in 1977, they are now in interstellar space, the most distant human-made objects.
- The James Webb Space Telescope: launched in December 2021, it looks back more than 13 billion years toward the first galaxies.
- Deep ocean: in 2012 the American filmmaker James Cameron made a solo dive to the Challenger Deep. Much of the deep-sea floor is still not mapped in detail, and an international project called Seabed 2030 is working to map all of it.
- Mapping the whole world: American satellites and the Global Positioning System (GPS) help map the entire planet, and digital maps now show the whole world.

## Territories: exploring the lands the United States took in

- Hawaii: Polynesian voyagers found and settled the Hawaiian Islands centuries before Europeans, crossing thousands of miles of open ocean guided by the stars, waves, and birds. British captain James Cook reached Hawaii in 1778. American ships, mapmakers, and settlers charted the islands through the 1800s. The United States annexed Hawaii in 1898, and it became a state in 1959.
- Alaska: the United States bought Alaska from Russia in 1867. Critics at the time mocked the purchase and called it "Seward's Folly," after Secretary of State William Seward, who arranged it. American survey teams, and later gold seekers during the Klondike and Nome rushes of the late 1890s, explored and mapped its huge interior.
- Pacific and Caribbean territories: after the war with Spain in 1898, the United States took in Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines, and American teams surveyed and mapped them. Other Pacific islands, such as American Samoa (1900) and Wake Island, followed.

## Mapping the world: the Wilkes Expedition

- The U.S. Exploring Expedition, also called the Wilkes Expedition (1838 to 1842): the first large exploring voyage funded by the United States. Navy lieutenant Charles Wilkes led six ships and about 350 men, including nine scientists, around the world. They surveyed nearly 280 Pacific islands, charted the Northwest coast (the Oregon country and Puget Sound), and sailed about 1,500 miles along Antarctica, showing it was a full continent. The specimens they brought back helped start the Smithsonian's collection.
