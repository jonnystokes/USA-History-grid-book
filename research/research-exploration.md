# Research Bank: Chapter 01, Exploration and Discovery (`exploration`)

Verified facts behind `outlines/exploration.md`, sorted into the book's ten eras (T-257, 2026-09-27).

**How this bank is built.** Before T-257 the bank was seven topic sections with no per-fact sources.
T-257 moved every one of those lines into its era, kept the old wording (marked *pre-T-257 line*),
and added a source under it, plus a correction note wherever the source disagrees. New facts found
by T-257 follow, each with its source inline. "Opened" means the page itself was read on the date
given. A fact seen only in a search-result summary carries the tag (unconfirmed: search summary only).
Britannica refused every request from this machine (HTTP 403) on 2026-09-27, so it is not cited
directly.

**Chapter angle (registry):** the journeys into the unknown, and who first went and found the way:
land, sea, poles, deep ocean, space. Not the crowds who followed (`migration`) or arrival from
abroad (`immigration`). For every expedition this bank records whose land it entered and what the
expedition's members did to the people there.

## 01 · Before 1500

### The first people to explore this land
- *pre-T-257 line:* Native peoples were the first to reach and explore this land. They crossed from Asia during the last ice age, when a land bridge joined Siberia and Alaska. Evidence places people here at least 15,000 years ago. Footprints at White Sands, New Mexico, dated by several methods, indicate people were present about 23,000 years ago. Some researchers question that early date.
  - Source: National Park Service, White Sands National Park, "Fossilized Footprints" (https://www.nps.gov/whsa/learn/nature/fossilized-footprints.htm, opened 2026-09-27): radiocarbon dates on seeds of the water plant *Ruppia cirrhosa* above and below the footprints give about 22,860 (plus or minus 320) and 21,130 (plus or minus 250) years ago. "The latest research shows that humans have been living in North America and Tularosa Basin for at least 23,000 years." The page says the earlier view put arrival at about 13,500 to 16,000 years ago.
  - **The dispute and its answer.** The 2021 seed dates were challenged because water plants can take up old carbon from rock, which makes them date too old. A second study (Pigati and others, *Science*, 5 October 2023, "Independent age estimates resolve the controversy of ancient human footprints at White Sands", https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.adh5007) dated conifer pollen and quartz grains from the same layers and got about 21,000 to 23,000 years (pollen) and at least 21,500 years (quartz). (Search summary of the Science and PubMed abstracts and of Smithsonian Magazine's report, https://www.smithsonianmag.com/science-nature/oldest-fossil-human-footprints-in-north-america-are-23000-years-old-study-reaffirms-180983018/ (unconfirmed: search summary only).) **Writer's note:** say "at least 21,000 years ago, and probably about 23,000", and say that scientists argued over the date and that a second study with two other methods agreed with it. The "at least 15,000 years" line in the old bank is the older, safer floor and is still true.
- *pre-T-257 line:* Over thousands of years they spread across the whole continent and learned every kind of land: deserts, mountains, forests, coasts, and plains.
  - Framing, supported by the dates above and by the spread of Native nations recorded in `research/research-native-nations.md` era 01.
- **Population by 1500 (outline's figure).** Estimates of the Native population north of Mexico at contact run from about 1 million to about 18 million. Douglas Ubelaker (1976) estimated about 1.85 million for the continental United States. Henry Dobyns (1983) estimated about 18 million north of Mesoamerica. There is no settled number. (Carried from `research/research-native-nations.md` line 12, which cites the British Academy paper "The Demographic Collapse of Native Peoples of the Americas" and the Southeastern Oklahoma State University proceedings "Counting the Dead".)

### Trade routes
- *pre-T-257 line:* They built long trade routes. Copper from the Great Lakes, shells from the Gulf coast, and obsidian and other goods moved hundreds of miles between peoples.
  - Source: Lake Superior copper, Gulf shells, western obsidian and Southwestern turquoise have all been found hundreds of miles from their sources. Cahokia (peak about 1050 to 1150) controlled Mill Creek chert hoe blades found more than 400 miles away. (Illinois State Museum and Cahokia Mounds State Historic Site, as verified in `research/research-economy.md` and quoted at `research/research-marketplace.md` line 21.) The trade itself is `economy`'s and `marketplace`'s story. This chapter uses it only to show that Native people knew the routes across the continent.

### Polynesian voyagers reach Hawaii
- *pre-T-257 line (from the old "Territories" section):* Hawaii: Polynesian voyagers found and settled the Hawaiian Islands centuries before Europeans, crossing thousands of miles of open ocean guided by the stars, waves, and birds.
  - **Date.** Patrick V. Kirch, "When Did the Polynesians Settle Hawai'i? A Review of 150 Years of Scholarly Inquiry and a Tentative Answer", *Hawaiian Archaeology* 12 (2011), abstract at University of Hawaii eVols (https://evols.library.manoa.hawaii.edu/items/6efe6b3b-7a0e-4290-82bf-7f2ebf9dbac0, opened 2026-09-27): "initial Polynesian discovery and colonization of the Hawaiian Islands occurred between approximately AD 1000 and 1200." The older view had put it at about AD 300 to 750. Other radiocarbon work puts first arrival as late as about AD 1200 to 1290 (search summary of Wilmshurst and others, and of "The 13th century Polynesian colonization of Hawai'i Island", *Journal of Archaeological Science* 2011 (unconfirmed: search summary only)). **Writer's note:** say "about 1,000 to 800 years ago" or "between about AD 1000 and 1200", and say that scientists still argue about the exact century.
  - **Navigation by stars, waves and birds.** No page opened for the method in T-257. The outline dropped the phrase. A writer who wants it must source it first (the Polynesian Voyaging Society or the Bishop Museum are the places to look).
  - Era placement: the settlement belongs in era 01. Cook (1778) belongs in era 05.

### The Norse in Newfoundland
- *pre-T-257 line:* The Norse reached North America around the year 1000, about 500 years before Columbus. Leif Erikson, son of Erik the Red of Greenland, led a group to a place he named Vinland (wild grapes grew there). Archaeologists found the site in 1960 at L'Anse aux Meadows in Newfoundland and dated it to about 1000. The Norse stayed about ten years, fought with local people they called Skraelings, and left. They built no permanent settlement, and the visit had no lasting effect on the continent.
  - **Exact year.** Wood cut with metal blades at L'Anse aux Meadows was felled in 1021. Researchers found the year by locating the radiocarbon spike from a solar storm of 993 in the tree rings and counting rings out to the bark edge. (Smithsonian Magazine, "New Dating Method Shows Vikings Occupied Newfoundland in 1021 C.E.", October 2021, https://www.smithsonianmag.com/science-nature/new-dating-method-shows-vikings-occupied-newfoundland-in-1021-ce-180978903/, opened 2026-09-27. The study is Kuitems and others, *Nature*, 20 October 2021.) It is the only exact date for Europeans in the Americas before 1492 (Science News, "Vikings lived in North America by at least the year 1021" (unconfirmed: search summary only)).
  - **Who found the site.** In 1960 a local resident, George Decker, led the Norwegian explorer Helge Ingstad to mounds near the village that local people called the "old Indian camp". Helge Ingstad and the archaeologist Anne Stine Ingstad dug there in seven seasons from 1961 to 1968. (Wikipedia, "L'Anse aux Meadows", opened 2026-09-27.)
  - **CORRECTION, "stayed about ten years".** No source opened gives ten years. Smithsonian: one scholar suggests the site "was occupied at least sporadically for perhaps 20 years, rather than just three years as has been assumed." Wikipedia adds that another scholar calls it a temporary boat-repair camp, and that a 2019 study suggests Norse activity there for as long as a century. **Writers must say the stay was short and that historians disagree on how short: estimates run from a few years to about twenty.**
  - **Whose land.** Before the Norse, five Indigenous groups had lived at the site, the oldest about 6,000 years ago, and Dorset people lived there about 300 years before the Norse (Wikipedia, same page). The sagas call the people the Norse met "Skraelings" and describe trade and "bloody fights" that led the Norse to give up (Wikipedia). Smithsonian: "The sagas tell of trade between the groups, but also of bad blood and violence." Which Indigenous people the Norse met is not settled by the sources opened. See SEARCHED, NOT FOUND below.
  - **Leif Erikson and the site.** Two Icelandic sagas, the *Saga of the Greenlanders* and the *Saga of Erik the Red*, describe Vinland. Both Leifsbudir (Leif's camp) and a second place called Hop have been claimed as L'Anse aux Meadows (Wikipedia). **Writer's note:** the site proves the Norse were in Newfoundland. It does not prove that this exact camp was Leif's. Say "the Norse camp found at L'Anse aux Meadows", and tell Leif from the sagas.

## 02 · The 1500s

### Christopher Columbus (story `christopher-columbus`)
- *pre-T-257 line:* Christopher Columbus (1451 to 1506): Italian from Genoa, sailed for Spain. Reached an island in the Bahamas on October 12, 1492. Made four voyages (1492 to 1502). Explored the Caribbean and touched Central and South America but never reached what is now the United States. Died still believing he had found a route to Asia.
  - Source: Wikipedia, "Voyages of Christopher Columbus" and "Christopher Columbus" (both opened 2026-09-27). Born between 25 August and 31 October 1451 in Genoa. Died 20 May 1506 at Valladolid, Spain. Voyages: 3 August 1492 to 4 March 1493, 24 September 1493 to 11 June 1496, 30 May 1498 to 23 August 1500, 12 May 1502 to 7 November 1504. Landfall at about 2 a.m. on 12 October 1492, when the lookout Rodrigo de Triana on the *Pinta* saw land. Columbus named the island San Salvador. **Its Indigenous name was Guanahani**, and its people were Lucayan Taino.
  - **CORRECTION, "died still believing".** Wikipedia records that in his 1498 voyage Columbus called the South American coast an "other world" while still thinking it was Asia, and that by 1503 some of his writing suggests he knew he had found an unknown land. **Writers should say he never accepted in public that he had reached a new continent, and that historians disagree about what he privately believed.**
- **What Columbus and his men did to the Taino (hard subject, new).** All from Wikipedia, "Christopher Columbus" and "Voyages of Christopher Columbus", opened 2026-09-27, which cite Varela and Aguirre, *La caida de Cristobal Colon: el juicio de Bobadilla* (2006), and Deagan and Cruxent (Yale, 2008):
  - On the first voyage he took Taino people captive and brought them to Spain to show the king and queen.
  - On Hispaniola in 1494 to 1495 his forces made war on the Taino "until they surrendered and agreed to pay tribute".
  - **February 1495: Columbus rounded up about 1,500 Taino (the source says "Arawaks") in a slave raid. About 500 of the strongest were shipped to Spain as slaves, and about 200 of them died on the voyage.** (Other accounts give 1,600 captured and 550 shipped (search summary of the Zinn Education Project and other pages (unconfirmed: search summary only)). Record the range: about 1,500 to 1,600 captured, about 500 to 550 shipped, about 200 dead at sea.)
  - The tribute rule: every Taino aged 14 and older had to hand over a small bell-sized measure of gold dust every six months. The priest Bartolome de las Casas wrote that people who failed had their hands cut off. Wikipedia notes that "other historians dispute" how widely this was done. **Writers must give the rule, say who recorded the punishment (Las Casas), and say that historians dispute how often it happened.**
  - In 1500 Francisco de Bobadilla, sent by the Spanish Crown, arrested Columbus and sent him to Spain in chains. Bobadilla's 48-page report, from 23 witnesses, was rediscovered in 2005 in the Spanish archives at Valladolid. It says Columbus punished a man who stole corn by having his ears and nose cut off and then selling him as a slave.
  - Taino deaths: disease, war and forced labour killed most of Hispaniola's Taino within decades. The starting number is disputed, and this bank does not adopt a single figure. Health and native-nations banks hold the disease story.
- Movie: *1492: Conquest of Paradise* (1992), directed by Ridley Scott, is a feature film about Columbus. (Outline's line, carried. Not re-verified this pass beyond general knowledge; the film is widely listed as 1992, Ridley Scott, Gerard Depardieu as Columbus. (unconfirmed: search summary only))

### The name "America"
- *pre-T-257 line:* The name "America": Amerigo Vespucci, an Italian navigator, sailed along South America around 1500 and argued it was a new continent, not the edge of Asia. In 1507 the German mapmaker Martin Waldseemuller printed a world map that labeled the new land "America" after Vespucci's first name. The name stuck.
  - Source: Wikipedia, "Voyages of Christopher Columbus" (opened 2026-09-27): "the next year [1507], the New World was named 'America' after the Italian explorer Amerigo Vespucci, who realized that it was a unique landmass." Vespucci's 1503 letter to Lorenzo di Pierfrancesco says he had known for two years that the lands were a new continent.

### John Cabot
- *pre-T-257 line:* John Cabot (about 1450 to 1499): Italian (Giovanni Caboto), sailed for England under King Henry VII. Reached the coast near Newfoundland on June 24, 1497, in a small ship called the Matthew with about 18 men. Claimed the land for England. Found cod so thick the crew reportedly scooped them up in baskets. Vanished at sea on his next voyage in 1498. His trip became England's basis for later claims.
  - Source: Wikipedia, "John Cabot" (opened 2026-09-27). A 1565 Bristol chronicle: "on St. John the Baptist's Day [24 June 1497], the land of America was found by the Merchants of Bristow in a shippe of Bristowe, called the Mathew". The ship was about 50 tons, with a crew of 18 to 20. **The landfall place is disputed**: historians propose Cape Bonavista or St. John's in Newfoundland, Cape Breton Island, and other places. Cabot landed only once and went no farther than "the shooting distance of a crossbow". Henry VII gave him £10 on 10 August 1497, about two years' pay for a labourer. The cod-in-baskets report comes from the Milanese envoy Raimondo de Soncino's letter of 1497 (Wikipedia names Soncino as a source of the voyage accounts). 

### Juan Ponce de Leon
- *pre-T-257 line:* Juan Ponce de Leon (1474 to 1521): Spanish. Reached Florida in 1513 and named it. First documented European to set foot on what is now the U.S. mainland. Returned in 1521 to build a colony and was killed by a Native arrow.
  - Source: Wikipedia, "Juan Ponce de Leon" (opened 2026-09-27). He sailed from Puerto Rico in early March 1513 with three ships and 65 people, "among them two free Africans and two enslaved Indigenous people". One of the free Africans was Juan Garrido. He named the land La Florida. On the Gulf coast the Calusa refused to trade and drove the ships off with warriors in canoes. In skirmishes that followed, **the Spaniards captured eight Calusa, four men and four women**. On 5 June about 80 Calusa warriors faced eleven Spanish sailors in a standoff.
  - **CORRECTION, "first documented European".** Wikipedia records that the Cantino map of 1502 already shows a peninsula like Florida, so unrecorded Spanish slaving voyages may have reached Florida first. **Writers should say he led "the first European expedition to Florida that anyone wrote down".**
  - **CORRECTION, "killed by a Native arrow".** In March 1521 he returned to southwest Florida to build a colony (the number of colonists was not in the page opened). "The native Calusa people resisted the incursion, and Ponce de Leon was seriously wounded in a skirmish." He **died of the wound in Cuba** in July 1521. (Wikipedia.) The popular story says an arrow, and the source opened says "wounded". Writers should say the Calusa wounded him and he died in Cuba.
  - The Fountain of Youth: "there is no contemporary evidence to support the story, which most modern historians consider a myth" (Wikipedia). His royal contract says nothing about a fountain.
  - **Whose land:** Calusa, southwest Florida. The east-coast landing place is not known.

### Giovanni da Verrazzano
- *pre-T-257 line:* Giovanni da Verrazzano (1524): Italian sailing for France. First European to sail the Atlantic coast from the Carolinas to Maine and to enter New York Harbor.
  - Source: Wikipedia, "Giovanni da Verrazzano" (opened 2026-09-27): "the first European to explore the Atlantic coast of North America between Florida and New Brunswick in 1524, including New York Bay and Narragansett Bay". In New York Bay "he encountered the Lenape in about 30 Lenape canoes". In Narragansett Bay he received a delegation of Wampanoag and Narragansett people. He died in 1528 in the Caribbean, probably on Guadeloupe. The most widely repeated account says Carib people killed him there, and the historian Lawrence Wroth thought it "may well be true".
  - **What his men did (hard subject, new).** Verrazzano's own letter to King Francis I, 8 July 1524 (National Humanities Center excerpt, from the Morgan Library copy, https://nationalhumanitiescenter.org/pds/amerbegin/contact/text4/verrazzano.pdf, opened 2026-09-27): about twenty of his men went two leagues inland, south of the place he named "Arcadia" (the Delmarva peninsula), and found an old woman, a young woman, and their children hiding in the grass. "We took the boy from the old woman to carry back to France, and we wanted to take the young woman, who was very beautiful and tall, but it was impossible to take her to the sea because of the loud cries she uttered." They left her and took the boy, who was about eight years old. The letter does not name the boy or his people. What happened to him in France is not recorded in the pages opened. A scholarly article suggests he was probably Nanticoke (Codignola, "Another Look at Verrazzano's Voyage, 1524", *Acadiensis* (unconfirmed: search summary only)).

### Jacques Cartier
- *pre-T-257 line:* Jacques Cartier (1530s): French. Explored the Gulf of St. Lawrence and the St. Lawrence River. Basis for French claims in Canada.
  - Source: The Canadian Encyclopedia, "Jacques Cartier", by Bernard Allaire (https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/jacques-cartier, opened 2026-09-27). Three voyages, 1534 to 1542. In 1534 "Cartier abducted two of Iroquoian chief Donnacona's sons." In 1536 "Cartier abducted chief Donnacona himself, the two sons, and seven other Iroquoian people. The French never returned Donnacona and his people to North America." The St. Lawrence Iroquoians at Stadacona gave the French a remedy made from evergreen trees that saved them from scurvy in the winter of 1535 to 1536. Cartier died 1 September 1557.
  - Scope note: Cartier's voyages were in what is now Canada. The outline gives him one clause. Keep it that way.

### Cabeza de Vaca and Estevanico (story `cabeza-de-vaca-estevanico`)
- *pre-T-257 line:* Cabeza de Vaca and Estevanico (1528 to 1536): survivors of a failed Spanish expedition. Shipwrecked near Texas, then walked thousands of miles across the Southwest to Mexico, living among Native peoples. Estevanico was an enslaved North African and one of the first Africans to explore the region. He later helped guide expeditions and was killed in 1539 at a Zuni village.
  - **The Narvaez expedition** (Wikipedia, "Narvaez expedition", opened 2026-09-27): left Spain 17 June 1527 with about 600 men. Nearly 100 deserted at Santo Domingo. About 400 men and 80 horses left Cuba in February 1528. Land sighted 12 April 1528 north of Tampa Bay. Panfilo de Narvaez landed with 300 men, and 100 men and ten women stayed on the ships. The land party met attacks, disease and hunger. By September 1528 the survivors had built rafts to reach Mexico. **About 80 men survived a storm and were washed onto Galveston Island in November 1528. Four lived to reach Mexico City in 1536.**
  - **Estevanico** (Wikipedia, "Esteban de Dorantes", opened 2026-09-27): born about 1500 in Azemmour, Morocco. Cabeza de Vaca called him "negro alarabe", an Arabic-speaking Black man. **In 1522 he was sold as a slave to the Spanish nobleman Andres Dorantes de Carranza.** After 1529 the survivors, Esteban among them, were held as slaves by Native people on the Texas coast (Wikipedia names them Coahuiltecan). The four escaped in 1534: Cabeza de Vaca, Dorantes, Alonso del Castillo Maldonado and Esteban. They worked as healers as they travelled west. They reached a Spanish settlement in Sinaloa in 1536 and Mexico City in July 1536. Cabeza de Vaca published his account, the *Relacion*, in 1542.
  - After 1536 Viceroy Antonio de Mendoza bought Esteban from Dorantes or received him as a gift. Some evidence suggests Mendoza freed him. On 7 March 1539 he left Culiacan as guide for Fray Marcos de Niza, going ahead of the friar with Sonoran Native men. **He was killed at Hawikuh, a Zuni town (Cibola), in 1539.** Several accounts of his death survive and they differ. (Wikipedia.) Zuni oral tradition and the Spanish accounts disagree about why he was killed; this bank has not opened the Zuni account. See SEARCHED, NOT FOUND if writers need it.
  - "First": Wikipedia calls Esteban "believed to have been the first person of African and Arab descent to explore" the present-day United States, preceded by Juan Garrido, the first person of African descent known to explore North America (Garrido sailed with Ponce de Leon in 1513, above). **Writers must not call Esteban the first African here. Juan Garrido came before him.**

### Hernando de Soto
- *pre-T-257 line:* Hernando de Soto (about 1500 to 1542): Spanish. Marched through the Southeast from 1539 to 1542, fighting and enslaving Native peoples along the way. Among the first Europeans to cross the Mississippi River. Died on its banks.
  - Carried from `research/research-war.md` §2.1 and §2.2, with that bank's sources (NPS, "Hernando De Soto", https://home.nps.gov/people/hernando-de-soto.htm; University of Alabama News, 2009; Encyclopedia of Alabama, "European Exploration and Colonial Period"): landed in Florida in May 1539 with 620 men and 220 horses. He took hostages to force safe passage, and held the Coosa leader hostage to get through the Coosa towns. **Mabila, 18 October 1540**: his force fought the palisaded town of Mabila, whose leader the Spanish called Tascalusa, for about nine hours and burned the town with people inside it. Deaths: NPS says "over 200 Spanish lives and approximately 2,000 of the Mabila Indians' lives". University of Alabama coverage says about 20 Spanish killed and 250 wounded, with thousands of Mabila dead. The site has never been found. The expedition's own record-keeper wrote: "We killed them all either with fire or the sword." The fight began after a Spanish officer decided a Mabila man had not shown him respect, and the argument ended with the man's arm cut off. **De Soto died of fever on 21 May 1542.** His men sank his body in the Mississippi so the nations they had fought would not learn he could die.
  - Carried from `research/research-food-farming.md` lines 28 to 29 (World History Encyclopedia, "Hernando de Soto's Expedition to La Florida (1539-1542)", and Museum of Florida History): the expedition of more than 600 people occupied Anhaica, the Apalachee capital (present-day Tallahassee), from October 1539 to March 1540 and fed itself on the Apalachee's stored food. **De Soto seized dozens of local men, women and children to carry equipment and supplies.**
  - Survivors reached Mexico on 10 September 1543 (`research/research-native-nations.md` line 36, World History Encyclopedia and NCpedia).
  - Whose land: the Apalachee, Coosa, Mabila (Tascalusa's people) and many Mississippian towns across the Southeast. `native-nations` and `war` tell the fighting and the collapse. This chapter tells the route and names the acts.

### Francisco Vazquez de Coronado
- *pre-T-257 line:* Francisco Vazquez de Coronado (1510 to 1554): Spanish. Searched the Southwest and Great Plains from 1540 to 1542 for cities of gold that did not exist. Reached Kansas. His men were the first Europeans to see the Grand Canyon.
  - Source: Wikipedia, "Francisco Vazquez de Coronado" (opened 2026-09-27). Born 1510, died 22 September 1554 in Mexico City. He left Compostela on 23 February 1540 with **about 400 European men-at-arms, 1,300 to 2,000 Mexican Indian allies, and four Franciscan friars**.
  - **Hawikuh (Zuni), July 1540.** The expedition was near starving and demanded entry. The Zuni refused. "Vazquez de Coronado and his expeditionaries attacked the Zunis." (Wikipedia.) The NPS adds that the Zuni had drawn lines of sacred cornmeal on the ground and warned the Spaniards not to cross, fired arrows when they did, and left the town after a short fight. (NPS, Coronado National Memorial, "History and Culture", https://www.nps.gov/coro/learn/historyculture/index.htm, opened 2026-09-27.)
  - **Hopi.** Pedro de Tovar's scouting party was refused entry at a Hopi town and used force to enter (Wikipedia).
  - **Grand Canyon.** Garcia Lopez de Cardenas's party took Hopi guides and reached the South Rim, "becoming the first non-Native" people known to see it (Wikipedia). Hopi guides led them there, so writers must say the Spaniards were the first Europeans to see it and that Hopi men showed them the way.
  - **The Tiguex War, winter 1540 to 1541** (carried from `research/research-war.md` §2.3, NPS "The Tiguex War", https://www.nps.gov/coro/learn/historyculture/the-tiguex-war.htm, and the New Mexico State Historian's "Coofor and Juan Aleman"): the expedition took the pueblo of Coofor near modern Bernalillo and made its people leave. The trigger, in the NPS's words: "a Spanish soldier assaulted a Pueblo woman, wife to a Tiguex man." At Arenal the expedition executed dozens of Pueblo captives. "Hundreds of Tiguex had been killed", villages were burned, and women and children were enslaved. **Method at Arenal not stated by these sources. Do not write "burned at the stake" without the Castaneda narrative.**
  - **The Turk.** A Native man the Spanish called "the Turk", probably Wichita or Pawnee, guided the army onto the Plains in spring 1541 toward a rich land called Quivira. At Quivira (central Kansas, Wichita towns) Coronado found grass houses and no gold. **Before leaving Quivira, Coronado ordered the Turk garroted**, that is, strangled to death with a tightened cord or iron collar. (Wikipedia.) A Wichita woman the Spaniards called Big Eyes, enslaved by the Tiguex, was taken as a guide.
  - Return: left New Mexico in early April 1542. Charges of war crimes were brought against Coronado and his field master Cardenas. Coronado's friends on the Audiencia cleared him. The Council of the Indies in Spain convicted Cardenas. (Wikipedia.)

### St. Augustine, 1565
- **Outline line:** "St. Augustine founded 1565, burned in 1566 by Saturiwa's people, rebuilt, still a tiny edge settlement."
  - Florida Museum of Natural History, "First Contacts" and "Colonization and Conflict" (https://www.floridamuseum.ufl.edu/staugustine/timeline/first-contacts/ and .../colonization-and-conflict/, opened 2026-09-27): Pedro Menendez de Aviles and about 800 colonists "(including 26 women and an unknown number of African slaves)" made their first settlement at **the town of Seloy, a Timucua chief**, in September 1565, and used Seloy's council house as their first fort. "Relations between the Spaniards and the Timucua deteriorated quickly." **"The Timucua, tired of the Spaniards in their community, fired flaming arrows at the fort in 1566."** Nine months after arriving the Spaniards moved to Anastasia Island. In 1577 "Indians attacked the settlement and burned many of the houses." In 1586 the English privateer Francis Drake burned the town and fort.
  - **CORRECTION to the outline:** the museum names "the Timucua", not "Saturiwa's people". Saturiwa was a Timucua chief whose seat was near present-day Jacksonville (same museum page, on the interpreter Juanillo). Writers should say "Timucua warriors shot flaming arrows at the fort in 1566". `research/research-war.md` recorded "No source found gives a 1566 burning": the Florida Museum is that source for a 1566 attack by fire. St. Augustine's founding is a settlement story, told here in one clause only.

## 03 · The 1600s

### Colonies on the edges
- Outline framing: lasting European colonies in the 1600s sat on the coasts and on the Rio Grande, and inland travel was by river. Jamestown's James Fort dates from 1607 (NPS Historic Jamestowne, via `research/research-city-building.md` line 39). Santa Fe was founded about 1610 by Governor Pedro de Peralta (`research/research-city-building.md` line 40). The settlements themselves belong to `city-building` and `migration`. This chapter tells only the journeys.

### Henry Hudson (story `henry-hudson`)
- *pre-T-257 line:* Henry Hudson (1609): English, sailing for the Dutch. Explored the river now named after him. His reports led to the Dutch claim on the area. On a later trip for England he searched for a northern passage, and his crew set him adrift in a mutiny.
  - Source: Wikipedia, "Henry Hudson" (opened 2026-09-27). In 1609 he commanded the Dutch East India Company's ship *Halve Maen* (*Half Moon*). He sailed up the river the Mohicans called Mahicannittuk and the Lenape called Muhheakantuck, later named the Hudson. He was not the first European in the harbour: Verrazzano had been there in 1524.
  - **The 1609 violence, from the ship's own journal.** Robert Juet, Hudson's mate, kept the journal (Library of Congress, "Extract from the journal of the voyage of the Half-Moon, Henry Hudson, master ... 1609", https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/gdc/gdclccn/11/02/22/68/11022268/11022268.pdf, opened 2026-09-27):
    - 6 September 1609: John Colman and four men in the ship's boat "were set upon by two canoes, the one having twelve, the other fourteen men". Colman was killed "with an arrow shot into his throat, and two more hurt." The crew buried him and named the place Colman's Point.
    - 9 September: two canoes came aboard. The crew "took two of them to have kept them, and put red coats on them", then took a third man, who jumped overboard. Juet does not say what became of the two captives in the extract read.
    - 1 October, on the way back down the river: a man in a canoe climbed in at the cabin window and took a pillow, two shirts and two bandoliers. "Our master's mate shot at him, and struck him on the breast, and killed him." A swimmer grabbed the ship's boat. "Our cook took a sword and cut off one of his hands, and he was drowned."
    - 2 October: two canoes shot arrows at the ship. The crew fired six muskets "and killed two or three of them". About a hundred men on a point of land shot at the ship. Juet fired a small cannon (a "falcon") and "killed two of them", then fired at a canoe of nine or ten men "and killed one of them. Then our men with their muskets killed three or four more of them." The ship then anchored off the side of the river "that is called Manna-hata".
    - **Count, by Juet's own journal: about 10 to 12 Native men killed by the crew on 1 and 2 October 1609 (1 shot, 1 drowned after his hand was cut off, then 2 or 3, 2, 1, and 3 or 4). One crewman, John Colman, was killed on 6 September.** Juet does not name the nation of the men killed. The river's lower valley was Lenape and Munsee land and its upper valley Mohican land (Wikipedia, above, for the river's names in those languages).
  - **The mutiny, 1611.** After wintering in James Bay, most of Hudson's crew on the *Discovery* mutinied in June 1611. According to the survivor Abacuk Pricket, the leaders were Henry Greene and Robert Juet. The mutineers set Hudson, his teenage son John and seven crewmen, sick men or men loyal to Hudson, adrift in a small open boat. They were never seen by Europeans again. Only eight of the thirteen mutineers survived the voyage home. Some were tried in England, and none was punished. Historians question Pricket's account because he blamed men who were dead. (Wikipedia, "Henry Hudson".) **CORRECTION to the outline's "set him and his son adrift": seven other men went with them.** Hudson Bay and James Bay are in present-day Canada.

### Samuel de Champlain
- *pre-T-257 line:* Samuel de Champlain (early 1600s): French. Founded Quebec in 1608 and explored the Great Lakes region. Lake Champlain is named for him.
  - Source: The Canadian Encyclopedia, "Samuel de Champlain" (https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/samuel-de-champlain, opened 2026-09-27). He explored the New England coast as far south as Cape Cod in 1605 and 1606. He founded Quebec in 1608. That winter 16 of his 25 men died of scurvy. In June 1609 he went south with two other Frenchmen and a party of Wendat (Huron), Algonquin and Montagnais warriors to the lake later named for him (between present-day New York and Vermont). **In late July 1609, at Ticonderoga, they met a party of Haudenosaunee (Iroquois). "According to historian Marcel Trudel, Champlain killed two men during the engagement."** Other accounts name the men as Mohawk leaders (not opened this pass). He died 25 December 1635 at Quebec.

### Jacques Marquette and Louis Jolliet (story `marquette-and-jolliet`)
- *pre-T-257 line:* Jacques Marquette and Louis Jolliet (1673): a French priest and a trader who paddled down the Mississippi River. They went as far as the Arkansas River, confirmed the river flowed toward the Gulf of Mexico rather than the Pacific, and turned back to avoid Spanish territory. Jolliet later lost his notes when his canoe overturned near Montreal, so Marquette's account became the record.
  - Source: Wikipedia, "Jacques Marquette" and "Louis Jolliet" (both opened 2026-09-27). They left St. Ignace (in present-day Michigan) on 17 May 1673 with two canoes and five voyageurs of French and Native ancestry. The Illinois people had told Marquette about the river and invited him. Michigamea warriors nearly attacked them, and elders called the attack off and fed them. They stayed two nights with the Akansea (the Quapaw) and turned back at the mouth of the Arkansas River on 17 July 1673, about 435 miles from the Gulf. They were told the people farther south were well armed and would attack anyone who interfered with their trade, and they feared the Spanish. They came back by the Illinois River, the shorter route their Native hosts told them about. "On his way through the Lachine Rapids, Jolliet's canoe overturned, and his records were lost." Marquette's account is the chief record. Marquette died of dysentery on 18 May 1675 near present-day Ludington, Michigan.
  - Whose land: the Illinois Confederation, the Michigamea and the Quapaw (Akansea) are the nations named in the pages opened.

### Robert de La Salle (story `robert-de-la-salle`)
- *pre-T-257 line:* Robert de La Salle (1682): traveled the full length of the Mississippi to the Gulf of Mexico, the first European to do so. On April 9, 1682, he claimed the whole river basin for France and named it Louisiana, after King Louis XIV. His second expedition, in 1684, aimed to build a colony at the river's mouth but overshot and landed at Matagorda Bay in present-day Texas. The colony failed, and in 1687 his own men killed him. His 1682 claim later gave the United States a basis for buying the Louisiana Territory in 1803.
  - Source: Wikipedia, "Rene-Robert Cavelier, Sieur de La Salle" (opened 2026-09-27). Born 22 November 1643 at Rouen.
  - **CORRECTION, "full length of the Mississippi".** In 1682 he "canoed the lower Mississippi River from the mouth of the Illinois River to the Gulf of Mexico". He did not travel its full length. Writers should say he was the first European known to follow the river down to the Gulf.
  - On 9 April 1682 at the river's mouth he claimed the Mississippi basin for France and named it La Louisiane for Louis XIV. Every nation living in that basin was left out of the claim. His group had built a small fort, Fort Prudhomme, near present-day Memphis.
  - **1684 colony.** Left France 24 July 1684 with four ships and 300 colonists. One ship was taken by pirates, another sank in Matagorda Bay, and the last, *La Belle*, ran aground. The colony stood on Garcitas Creek near present-day Victoria, Texas.
  - **His death.** On 19 March 1687, near present-day Navasota, Texas, "La Salle was slain by Pierre Duhaut during an ambush while talking to Duhaut's decoy, Jean L'Archeveque", about six leagues from the westernmost Hasinai (Tejas) village. Duhaut was then shot by another member of the party. (Wikipedia, citing Joutel's journal.)
  - **The colony's end.** "The colony lasted only until 1688, when Karankawa-speaking Natives killed the 20 remaining adults and took five children as captives." (Wikipedia.) The land was Karankawa land. *La Belle*'s wreck was found in Matagorda Bay in 1995.
  - The link to 1803 is framing: France's claim to Louisiana began with La Salle's 1682 declaration, and France sold that claim to the United States in 1803. Carried as the old bank's statement.

## 04 · 1700 to 1750

### Thin era
- The outline marks this era thin. English colonists did little exploring beyond the Appalachians in these fifty years. The two documented journeys into country no European had mapped were French on the northern Plains and Russian on the Alaska coast.

### Vitus Bering (story `vitus-bering`)
- *pre-T-257 line (from the old "1700s" section):* Vitus Bering (1741): led a Russian expedition that reached Alaska. The Bering Strait is named for him. He died of scurvy on the return.
  - Source: Wikipedia, "Vitus Bering" (opened 2026-09-27). Born in Horsens, Denmark, baptised 5 August 1681. An officer in the Russian navy. On his First Kamchatka Expedition he sailed into the strait later named for him in August 1728. From Petropavlovsk he led the St. Peter toward North America in the spring of 1741. The expedition sighted Mount Saint Elias on 16 July 1741 and landed briefly at or near Kayak Island. Scurvy spread through the crew on the return. He died on 19 December 1741 on an island near Kamchatka, later named Bering Island. "Like 28 men of his company, Bering's death was commonly assumed to have been the result of scurvy (although this has since been contested)."
  - **CORRECTION, "died of scurvy".** A 1991 Russian and Danish expedition dug up his remains, studied his teeth and bones, and concluded he did not die of scurvy. Heart failure was the likely cause (Wikipedia note citing Frost 2003). **Writers must say he died on the island after the ship was wrecked there, that about 28 of his men died, that scurvy was blamed at the time, and that scientists who studied his bones in 1991 concluded his own death was more likely heart failure.** **Scurvy (clinical word, define in prose):** "a disease that occurs when you have a severe lack of vitamin C (ascorbic acid) in your diet. Scurvy causes general weakness, anemia, gum disease, poor wound healing, and skin hemorrhages" (bleeding under the skin) (MedlinePlus, "Scurvy", https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/000355.htm, opened 2026-09-27). On a long voyage it came from months without fresh food.
  - **Whose land.** Mount Saint Elias, Kayak Island and Prince of Wales Island were Native land. The pages opened do not name the nations. See the PATCH in the bank check (T-257) if one is added, and name no nation without a source.
  - **The Koryaks.** Before the voyage, a detachment of the expedition in Kamchatka "killed several native Koryaks in the settlement of Utkolotsk and enslaved the remainder". The naturalist Georg Steller "was horrified to see the Koryaks tortured in search of the murderers". (Wikipedia, "Vitus Bering".) This happened in Siberia, not in what is now the United States.
  - **Chirikov's lost boats.** Bering's second ship, the St. Paul under Aleksei Chirikov, sighted land at Baker Island off Prince of Wales Island in southeast Alaska on 15 July 1741. Chirikov sent a longboat ashore to find an anchorage. It did not return after a week, and he sent his second longboat, which also did not return. On 27 July he sailed west without them. (Wikipedia, "Aleksei Chirikov", opened 2026-09-27, citing Johansen, *Encyclopedia Arctica*.) The number of men lost and their fate are not in the page opened.
  - Steller recorded new animals, among them the Steller sea cow and Steller's jay (Wikipedia, "Vitus Bering").

### The La Verendrye brothers on the northern Plains, 1742 to 1743 (new, T-257)
- Source: Wikipedia, "Verendrye brothers' journey to the Rocky Mountains" (opened 2026-09-27), which rests on the Chevalier's journal (found in French archives in 1851) and on the lead plate.
  - On 29 April 1742 the Chevalier de La Verendrye (most likely Louis-Joseph Gaultier de La Verendrye), one of his brothers and two other Frenchmen left Fort La Reine (Portage la Prairie, Manitoba). They reached the Mandan villages on the upper Missouri on 19 May, waited two months, and left on 23 July with two Mandan guides. They travelled with several Plains nations whose French names were the Horse People, the Bow People and the Little Cherry (Chokecherry) People. Historians do not agree which modern nations these were. The page suggests the Bow People may have been Pawnee and the Little Cherry People probably Arikara.
  - The Horse People told them that two years before, the Snake People had destroyed seventeen of their villages, killed the men and old women, and taken the young women to be sold. (Record of what the Chevalier was told. Which nation the "Snake People" were is uncertain.)
  - **On 30 March 1743 they buried a lead plate** at the Little Cherry People's fort on the Missouri River, at present-day Pierre. The front has a stamped Latin inscription naming Louis XV, Pierre La Verendrye and the year 1741. On the back is scratched "Placed by Chevalyet de Lave ... Louis la Londette, A Miotte; 30 March 1743". **The plate was found at Pierre, South Dakota, in 1913** and is in the South Dakota Cultural Heritage Center. It is six by eight inches.
  - Where they went is disputed. They were "possibly the first Europeans to cross the northern Great Plains and see the Rocky Mountains", in what is probably Wyoming (Wikipedia, "Louis-Joseph Gaultier de La Verendrye", opened 2026-09-27: "The documentation is poor and it may have been a different Verendrye brother"). "Both the journal and plate are difficult to interpret."
  - Who found the plate in 1913: the page opened does not name the finders. Writers should say "found in 1913" and nothing more unless a source is added.

### Framing: the 1783 border

### The Cumberland Gap and Daniel Boone (story `daniel-boone`)
- *pre-T-257 line (from the old "1700s" section):* Daniel Boone (1770s): explored and marked a route through the Cumberland Gap into Kentucky, called the Wilderness Road, opening the way for settlers into the interior.
  - **The gap before Boone.** Wikipedia, "Cumberland Gap" (opened 2026-09-27): "Long used by Native American nations, the Cumberland Gap was brought to the attention of settlers in 1750 by Thomas Walker, a Virginia physician and explorer." In 1769 Joseph Martin built a fort nearby for Walker's land claimants, and "Martin and his men were chased out of the area by Native Americans". **Writers must not call Boone the gap's discoverer.** Native nations used it long before, and Walker named the Cumberland River in 1750.
  - **Boone's journeys.** Wikipedia, "Daniel Boone" (opened 2026-09-27). He and his brother Squire first crossed into present-day Kentucky in 1767. In May 1769 he set out with five others, including John Findley, who had told him of the Cumberland Gap, on a two-year hunting and trapping trip. On 22 December 1769 a Shawnee party captured Boone and his brother-in-law John Stewart, took all their skins and told them to leave and never come back. "The Shawnee had not signed the 1768 Treaty of Fort Stanwix, in which the Iroquois had ceded their claim to Kentucky to the British. The Shawnee regarded Kentucky as their hunting ground." **Whose land: Kentucky was the hunting ground of the Shawnee and was claimed also by the Cherokee and the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois).**
  - **James Boone, October 1773.** Boone led his family and about 50 others to settle Kentucky in 1773. On 9 October Boone's oldest son James, several white men and two enslaved Black men, Charles and Adam, left the main party for supplies and were attacked by a band of Delawares, Shawnee and Cherokees. According to the historian John Mack Faragher, the band had decided "to send a message of their opposition to settlement". **James Boone and Henry Russell were tortured and killed. Charles was captured. Adam hid in driftwood and saw it.** The settlers turned back. (Wikipedia, "Daniel Boone", citing Faragher.) The page does not state what the torture was. Writers must say "tortured and killed" and not describe methods the source does not give.
  - **The Wilderness Road, 1775.** After Dunmore's War (1774), in which Virginia's victory at Point Pleasant ended in the Shawnee giving up their claims to Kentucky, the North Carolina judge Richard Henderson hired Boone for his planned colony, Transylvania. Boone invited Cherokee towns to a meeting at Sycamore Shoals in March 1775, "where Henderson purchased the Cherokee claim to Kentucky". Boone then blazed "Boone's Trace", later the Wilderness Road, through the Cumberland Gap into central Kentucky. **Sam, an enslaved Black man, and other enslaved labourers were in Boone's party.** Near present-day Richmond, Kentucky, Native men attacked the camp and killed Sam and his owner. Boone founded Boonesborough on the Kentucky River. (Wikipedia, "Daniel Boone".) The price Henderson paid and the Cherokee opposition to the sale are not in the page opened (see SEARCHED, NOT FOUND).
  - **1776 and 1778.** On 14 July 1776 a war party captured Boone's daughter Jemima and two other girls outside Boonesborough. Boone's men caught them two days later and freed the girls. In 1778 Shawnee warriors captured Boone, and he lived with the Shawnee for months before escaping. (Wikipedia.)
  - The settlers who followed Boone through the gap belong to `migration`.

### Portola and the first land view of San Francisco Bay, 1769
- *pre-T-257 line:* Portola expedition (1769): a Spanish party led by Gaspar de Portola that traveled overland through California and found San Francisco Bay.
  - Source: Wikipedia, "Portola expedition" (opened 2026-09-27). Dates 14 July 1769 to 24 January 1770. Organised by Jose de Galvez to survey Alta California and assert Spain's claim. Portola's party going north from San Diego numbered 74 men: Pedro Fages's Catalan volunteers, leather-jacket soldiers, Captain Fernando Rivera, Sergeant Jose Francisco Ortega leading the scouts, the engineer Miguel Costanso, Christian Native men from Baja California, and the friars Juan Crespi and Francisco Gomez. Junipero Serra came as far as San Diego.
  - **Who saw the bay.** Sergeant Ortega led a party of scouts over Montara Mountain and found the way north blocked "by the mouth of a vast bay they could not identify, known today as San Francisco Bay". The party had passed Monterey Bay without recognising it. On 11 November Portola's officers agreed to turn back. **Writers should say Ortega's scouts, not Portola himself, were the first Europeans known to see the bay, in early November 1769.** The exact day is not in the page opened.
  - The page records that most meetings with Native people along the route were reported as peaceful and that people gave the Spaniards guidance and food. On 30 November about a dozen Rumsen people brought the starving party pinole (ground seeds) and seeds. **Whose land:** the Rumsen are named in the page opened. The people around San Francisco Bay are the Ohlone, but no page opened names them; writers must source that before using it.
  - The missions that followed (Serra founded San Carlos Borromeo at Monterey in 1770, per the same page) belong to `religion` and `native-nations`.

### Cook in Hawaii, 1778 to 1779
- *pre-T-257 line (from the old "Territories" section):* British captain James Cook reached Hawaii in 1778.
  - Source: Wikipedia, "James Cook" (opened 2026-09-27). Cook's ships "became the first recorded Europeans to see the Hawaiian Islands, on 18 January 1778". They landed at Waimea on Kauai and on Niihau. **One of Cook's crew, John Williamson, shot and killed a Hawaiian man while ashore collecting provisions.** Cook named the islands the Sandwich Islands.
  - He returned in late November 1778 and anchored at Kealakekua Bay on Hawaii Island, where about 10,000 Hawaiians and 1,000 canoes met the ships. The high chief Kalaniopuu received him.
  - **His death, 14 February 1779.** On 13 February Hawaiians took one of the ships' boats. The next day "Cook attempted to recover the cutter by kidnapping and ransoming the king, Kalaniopuu." Crowds gathered. "Cook fired a warning shot, then shot one of the Hawaiians dead." In the fight, Cook and four marines (Corporal James Thomas and Privates Theophilus Hinks, Thomas Fatchett and John Allen) were killed. **Seventeen Hawaiians were killed.** Accounts of Cook's last moments contradict each other. (Wikipedia, citing Samwell 1786, Thomas 2003, Williams 2008.)
  - **Disease.** "Cook's crews transmitted some of these diseases to indigenous peoples in Tahiti, Hawaii, British Columbia, and New Zealand. In Hawaii, Cook's crews were the first Europeans to introduce some diseases to the local population." The list of diseases European crews carried includes syphilis and gonorrhea. (Wikipedia, citing Salmond 2004 and Williams 2008.) Which diseases reached Hawaii from Cook's ships is not itemised in the page opened. `health` holds the disease story.
  - Cook also charted the Northwest coast in 1778 and stayed at Nootka Sound (Vancouver Island, Canada) from 29 March to 26 April 1778.

### Robert Gray and the Columbia River, 1792 (new, T-257)
- Source: Wikipedia, "Robert Gray (sea captain)" (opened 2026-09-27), citing HistoryLink.org (Kit Oldham, 2003) and the Oregon Encyclopedia (William L. Lang, 2020). Gray, born 10 May 1755 in Tiverton, Rhode Island, sailed from Boston on 30 September 1787 with John Kendrick for Boston merchants who planned to trade sea otter furs to China. **In 1790 he completed the first American voyage around the world.**
  - **Tillamook Bay, August 1788.** At Tillamook Bay (Oregon) fighting broke out with the Tillamook people, and **Marcus Lopez, Gray's Black cabin boy and cook from the Cape Verde Islands, was killed.** Gray named the bay Murderer's Harbor.
  - **The Columbia, 11 May 1792.** On the evening of 11 May 1792 Gray's men found a safe channel over the sand bars, and the ship *Columbia Rediviva* entered the river's estuary. Gray named the river Columbia after his ship. He was the first non-Native navigator known to enter it (HistoryLink title). The page opened names Chinook people at Grays Harbor, north of the river's mouth. Lewis and Clark's records of 1805 name the Chinook and Clatsop at the river's mouth (era 06). The US later used Gray's entry as one basis for its claim to the Oregon country (widely stated. Not in the page opened. Writers should source it before using it).
  - **Gray's attacks on Native villages, 1792.** In April 1792 Gray ordered the destruction of the Nuu-chah-nulth village of Opitsaht on Vancouver Island, about 200 houses, "a fine village, the Work of Ages" in his officer John Boit's words, "in a short time totally destroy'd". It was empty at the time. In May 1792 he ordered an attack on a Chicklisaht Nuu-chah-nulth village north of Nootka Sound, **killing seven** and seizing sea otter furs. **"Later in 1792, in Grays Harbor, Captain Gray fired on a group of Chinooks, killing 20."** In Clayoquot Sound he "killed or wounded at least 25" men approaching his ship in a war canoe at night. John Boit wrote that Gray "had let his passions go too far". In 2005 Gray's descendants formally apologized for the destruction of Opitsaht. (Wikipedia.) Grays Harbor is in present-day Washington State. Opitsaht, Clayoquot Sound and Nootka Sound are in Canada.

## 06 · 1800 to 1850

Sources opened 2026-09-27 (T-257r) unless marked. Wikipedia pages were read as plain text through the Wikipedia API. "NPS" pages were read directly.

### Framing: the Louisiana Purchase, 1803
- Wikipedia, "Louisiana Purchase" (opened 2026-09-27): treaty signed in Paris on **30 April 1803** by Robert Livingston, James Monroe and Francois Barbe-Marbois. Price **$15 million** (80 million francs) for about **828,000 square miles** (530 million acres). "Acquiring the territory nearly doubled the size of the United States." The US borrowed the money from British and Dutch banks at 6 percent.
- **Whose land.** Same page: "France only controlled a small fraction of this area, most of which was inhabited by Native Americans; effectively, for the majority of the area, the United States bought the preemptive right to obtain Indian lands by treaty or by conquest, to the exclusion of other colonial powers." Writers must state this plainly: the US paid France for the right to take the land from the nations living on it, by treaty or by war.

### Lewis and Clark, 1804 to 1806 (story `lewis-and-clark`)
- *pre-T-257 line (from the old "1800s" section):* Lewis and Clark (1804 to 1806): Meriwether Lewis and William Clark led the Corps of Discovery, about four dozen men, on a round trip of nearly 8,000 miles from near St. Louis to the Pacific and back. President Thomas Jefferson sent them to explore the new Louisiana Purchase, look for a water route to the Pacific, record the land, plants, animals, and peoples, and open trade with Native nations. They traveled up the Missouri River by keelboat and canoe and wintered at Fort Mandan (in present-day North Dakota) among the Mandan and Hidatsa. There they hired Toussaint Charbonneau and his young Shoshone wife, Sacagawea, who gave birth to a son, Jean Baptiste, and carried him the whole way. When the group reached Shoshone country, Sacagawea's brother Cameahwait supplied the horses they needed to cross the mountains. A Shoshone guide, Old Toby, and later Nez Perce guides helped them over the Bitterroot Range and down the rivers to the coast. They reached the Pacific in November 1805 and built Fort Clatsop near present-day Astoria, Oregon. When they voted on where to spend the winter, York, an enslaved man owned by Clark, and Sacagawea both took part in the vote. Only one man died on the whole trip: Sergeant Charles Floyd, in August 1804, most likely from a burst appendix. The single deadly clash with Native people came on the return, in July 1806, when Lewis's party killed two Blackfeet men caught taking guns and horses. **Corrections below:** size "as many as 45"; the 8,000-mile total is not in the pages opened; Old Toby and the Nez Perce guides are not in the pages opened (keep them out of the outline until sourced); the Blackfeet fight was over guns and horses, and NPS gives two to three killed.
- Source for all bullets here unless marked: Wikipedia, "Lewis and Clark Expedition" (opened 2026-09-27).
  - **Orders.** Jefferson's written instructions: "The object of your mission is to explore the Missouri River, & such principle stream of it, as, by its course and communication with the waters of the Pacific ocean ... may offer the most direct & practicable water communication across this continent for the purpose of commerce." Congress gave **$2,324** for supplies. The expedition was also to tell Native nations "that their lands now belonged to the United States and that 'their great father' in Washington was now their sovereign", and carried silver **Indian Peace Medals** with Jefferson's portrait and an air rifle to show American firepower.
  - **Size.** Clark and about 30 others left Camp Dubois, Illinois, at 4 pm on **14 May 1804**. Lewis joined at St. Charles, Missouri, and the party set out up the Missouri on 21 May. "While accounts vary, it is believed the Corps had as many as 45 members," including York. **Correction to the pre-T-257 line:** "about four dozen" stands as "as many as 45"; the page does not give the 8,000-mile total, so the outline does not state a mileage.
  - **Sergeant Charles Floyd** died on **20 August 1804**, "apparently from acute appendicitis", the only member to die on the expedition. Buried on Floyd's Bluff, now Sioux City, Iowa.
  - **The Lakota standoff, 25 September 1804.** Near the mouth of the Bad River (present-day Fort Pierre, South Dakota) Lakota chiefs including Black Buffalo and the Partisan demanded gifts for passage. Warriors took hold of a boat and an oar. "Lewis took a firm stand, ordering a display of force and presenting arms; Captain Clark brandished his sword and threatened violent reprisal." Black Buffalo ordered his warriors back. No one was killed. Clark called the Lakota the "vilest miscreants of the savage race" in his journal.
  - **Fort Mandan**, winter 1804 to 1805, near present-day Washburn, North Dakota, in Mandan country beside the Hidatsa. Here the captains hired Toussaint Charbonneau and met Sacagawea. Jean Baptiste Charbonneau was born on **11 February 1805**. The party left on **7 April 1805**.
  - **Route.** Up the Missouri to its headwaters, over the Continental Divide at **Lemhi Pass**, over the Bitterroot Mountains at **Lolo Pass**, then by canoe down the Clearwater, Snake and Columbia rivers. "They did not find a continuous waterway to the Pacific but located a Native American trail." **No all-water route existed.**
  - **The Pacific.** First sight of the ocean **7 November 1805**. On **24 November 1805** the party chose the winter camp: "Both Sacagawea and the enslaved York participated in the vote." Historians disagree whether it was a vote. Martin Plamondon II calls it "polling"; Dayton Duncan notes that Clark wrote down every member's view, including York's and Sacagawea's (Wikipedia, "York (explorer)", opened 2026-09-27). **Fort Clatsop** near present-day Astoria, Oregon. The page names the Chinook as the neighbouring people the captains asked about trading ships. (That the fort was named for the Clatsop people is widely stated but not in a page opened.) Food ran short, and the party was "too poor to purchase enough food from neighboring tribes."
  - **Return.** Left Fort Clatsop **23 March 1806**. On 3 July the party split.
  - **The Two Medicine fight, 26 to 27 July 1806.** Wikipedia (L&C page): Lewis's group of four (Lewis, George Drouillard and the brothers Joseph and Reubin Field) camped with Blackfeet men; "During the night, the Blackfeet tried to steal their weapons. In the struggle, the soldiers killed two Blackfeet men." Lewis's own journal for 27 July 1806 (Discover Lewis & Clark, https://lewis-clark.org/day-by-day/27-jul-1806/, read through WebFetch 2026-09-27): at daylight the Blackfeet took the men's guns; **Reubin Field "stabed the indian to the heart with his knife the fellow ran about 15 steps and fell dead"**; Lewis chased men driving off the horses and **"shot him through the belly"**; Lewis "left the medal about the neck of the dead man that they might be informed who we were." **Count dispute:** NPS, "Two Medicine Fight Site" (https://www.nps.gov/places/two-medicine-fight-site-mt.htm, opened 2026-09-27): "two to three Blackfeet tribesmen were killed while the rest escaped." Lewis's party then rode about 100 miles without stopping. Search summaries (lewis-clark.org) describe the Blackfeet as eight Piegan or Atsina teenage boys and name the dead as Calf-Standing-on-a-Side-Hill (Side Hill Calf) and He-Who-Looks-at-the-Calf (unconfirmed: search summary only; the journal page opened gives no names or ages). This was the only fight on the expedition in which anyone was killed.

The PBS documentary of the same name is widely known but was not confirmed from a page opened, so the outline uses *The Far Horizons*.

### Sacagawea (story `sacagawea`)
- *pre-T-257 lines:* hired with Charbonneau at Fort Mandan, gave birth to Jean Baptiste and carried him the whole way, her brother Cameahwait supplied horses.
- Source: Wikipedia, "Sacagawea" (opened 2026-09-27).
  - Born about **May 1788**, Lemhi Shoshone ("Agaidika") near present-day Salmon, Idaho. **In 1800, when she was about 12, a Hidatsa raiding party captured her and several other children. The raid killed four Shoshone men, four women and several boys.** She was held at a Hidatsa village near Washburn, North Dakota. (A 2021 Hidatsa account, *Our Story of Eagle Woman*, argues she was Hidatsa by birth. Writers should say most historians hold she was Shoshone.)
  - **Charbonneau.** "At about age 13" she was put into "a non-consensual marriage to Charbonneau, a Quebecois trapper." He "was variously reported to have purchased both girls [Sacagawea and another Shoshone girl, Otter Woman] from the Hidatsa, or to have won Sacagawea while gambling." The expedition summary calls Charbonneau "her husband and owner" (Wikipedia, "Lewis and Clark Expedition").

  - **Cameahwait.** In August 1805 she interpreted with the Shoshone band whose leader, Cameahwait, turned out to be her brother ("in the Shoshoni language cousin and brother are the same word").   - **Death.** A fort clerk, John Luttig, wrote on **20 December 1812** at Fort Manuel Lisa (on the Missouri, in present-day South Dakota) that "the wife of Charbonneau, a Snake Squaw, died of putrid fever," aged about 25. On **11 August 1813** William Clark became legal guardian of her son and her daughter Lizette. Most historians accept the 1812 date. A Shoshone tradition holds that she died in 1884 on the Wind River Reservation in Wyoming, and the Hidatsa account above disputes the 1812 record. **Writers must give the 1812 record and say that Shoshone and Hidatsa accounts disagree.**

### York (story `york`)
- *pre-T-257 line:* "York, an enslaved man owned by Clark" took part in the vote.
- Sources: Wikipedia, "York (explorer)" (opened 2026-09-27), citing William Clark's letters to his brother Jonathan (found 1988, published 2002, ed. James J. Holmberg); NPS, "York" (https://www.nps.gov/people/york.htm, opened 2026-09-27); Wikipedia, "Lewis and Clark Expedition".
  - Born enslaved in Caroline County, Virginia, son of Old York and Rose, who were enslaved by John Clark III. **William Clark inherited York by his father's will in 1799.** York was married before the expedition; his wife was enslaved by another owner near Louisville, Kentucky.   - On the expedition he hunted with a gun, which enslaved men were usually not allowed to carry (NPS), and his view was recorded on 24 November 1805 with everyone else's.
  - **No reward.** Every other man got double pay and 320 acres. York got nothing and remained enslaved (both sources).
  - **Clark refused to free him and punished him.** York asked for his freedom for his "immense services" and asked to be hired out near his wife in Louisville or sold to someone there. Clark wrote in 1808: "I did wish to do well by him. But as he got Such a notion about freedom and his emence Services ... I do not think with him, that his Services has been So great/or my Situation would promit me to liberate him" (NPS quoting Clark). Clark wanted him hired out to a "Severe Master". **Clark whipped York ("gave him a Severe trouncing") and put him in jail ("Caleboos") in July 1809.** York was hired out as a wagon driver in Louisville; the last record of him in that work is from 1815. In 1811 his wife's owner planned to move to Natchez, Mississippi, and "It is unlikely that he ever saw his wife again" (Wikipedia L&C page).

In 1832 Clark told the writer Washington Irving that he had freed York and given him a wagon and horses, that the business failed, and that York died of cholera in Tennessee trying to return to Clark. Historians have not confirmed Clark's account, and in the 1830s a Black man who said he had first come with Lewis and Clark was reported living as a chief among Native people in present-day Wyoming (Wikipedia L&C page). **Writers must say nobody knows when or how York died.**
  - In **2001 President Bill Clinton made York an honorary sergeant in the US Army** (Wikipedia).

### Zebulon Pike, 1805 to 1807 (story `zebulon-pike`)
- *pre-T-257 line:* explored the southern part of the Louisiana Purchase, sighted the Colorado peak now called Pikes Peak but never climbed it; Spanish soldiers arrested his party and later released them.
- Source: Wikipedia, "Zebulon Pike" (opened 2026-09-27).
  - Army lieutenant, born 5 January 1779. Sent by General James Wilkinson.
  - **First expedition, 1805 to 1806.** Left St. Louis 9 August 1805 to find the source of the Mississippi and tell British fur traders they were inside the United States. On 21 September 1805 at the mouth of the Minnesota River he made the "Treaty of St. Peters" with the Dakota, buying the site of the later Fort Snelling. He named **Cass Lake** (then Upper Red Cedar Lake) as the Mississippi's source. **Correction:** Pike was wrong. In 1832 the Ojibwe guide **Ozaawindib** led Henry Rowe Schoolcraft to the true source, a lake the Ojibwe called Omashkoozo-Zaaga'igan (Elk Lake), which Schoolcraft renamed **Lake Itasca** (Wikipedia, "Henry Schoolcraft", opened 2026-09-27).
  - **Second expedition, from 15 July 1806.** Wilkinson sent him to find the headwaters of the Arkansas and Red rivers, near the Spanish provinces of New Mexico and Texas. In early November 1806 Pike's men sighted the peak later named for him and tried to climb it. They reached Mount Rosa, "before giving up the ascent in waist-deep snow. They had already gone almost two days without food."
  - **Arrest.** His party crossed into Spanish territory, "whether through confusion or deliberation." **Spanish soldiers captured Pike and some of his men on 26 February 1807** and took them to Santa Fe and then to Chihuahua, Mexico, where the Spanish commandant general Nemesio Salcedo questioned him and took his papers. His men were kept prisoner. **The Spanish released them at the Louisiana border on 1 July 1807.** Historians still debate "whether Pike was truly an explorer, or if he was a spy." His 1810 book was widely read.
  - He died on **27 April 1813** during the American attack on York (now Toronto), when the retreating British blew up their powder magazine.
- **Pikes Peak, first recorded climb (new, T-257r).** History Colorado, "Among the Eternal Snows" (2020, https://www.historycolorado.org/story/articles-print/2020/09/03/among-eternal-snows, opened 2026-09-27): late on **14 July 1820** the 22-year-old naturalist **Edwin James** of Major Stephen Long's Army expedition and two companions reached the summit. (Search summaries name the companions as Private Joseph Verplank and Zachariah Wilson: unconfirmed: search summary only.) The same article, quoting Ute (Nuuchiu) tradition: the mountain is **Tava-kaavi** to the Ute, and "Nuuchiu Ancestors were the first to summit Tava-kaavi." It also records that Long called the high plains a "Great American Desert."

### The mountain men and the fur trade, 1807 to 1840
- *pre-T-257 line:* In 1822 William Ashley ran a newspaper ad in St. Louis for "enterprising young men"; the men who answered, "Ashley's Hundred," became the mountain men; each summer they met at a "rendezvous".
- Wikipedia, "William Henry Ashley" (opened 2026-09-27): Ashley and Andrew Henry advertised in St. Louis papers for one hundred "enterprising young men . . . to ascend the river Missouri to its source, there to be employed for one, two, or three years", paid $200 a year. Wikipedia, "Hugh Glass" names the paper as the *Missouri Gazette and Public Advertiser*.
- Wikipedia, "Rocky Mountain Rendezvous" (opened 2026-09-27): held every year **from 1825 to 1840** at places in present-day Wyoming, Utah and Idaho. The first was in July 1825 on Henrys Fork of the Green River. Trappers sold furs and bought supplies there. Native people, trappers' Native wives and children came too.

Arikara men attacked on 2 June. **Counts differ:** 12 of about 40 Ashley men killed (Wikipedia, "Jedediah Smith"), or 13 killed and 10 wounded (Wikipedia, "William Henry Ashley"). The Arikara were angry after "a recent skirmish with Missouri Fur Company men" and wanted a trading post of their own ("Jedediah Smith"; Wikipedia, "Arikara War", opened 2026-09-27). **US response:** on 9 to 11 August 1823 Lt. Col. Henry Leavenworth led about 230 soldiers, 750 Lakota and Yankton allies and about 50 trappers against the Arikara towns and fired cannon at them. The Arikara left their towns at night after a peace was signed, and men of the Missouri Fur Company then burned a town ("Arikara War"). The count of Arikara killed is not in the pages opened. `war` and `native-nations` own this campaign.

### John Colter (story `john-colter`)
- *pre-T-257 line (outline):* left the expedition in 1806 to trap, saw the Yellowstone thermal country about 1807 to 1808, and escaped a Blackfeet capture on foot.
- Source: Wikipedia, "John Colter" (opened 2026-09-27).
  - Discharged early on **13 August 1806** at the Mandan villages to guide two trappers, Forrest Hancock and Joseph Dickson, back up the Missouri.
  - In 1807 he joined Manuel Lisa's fur party and helped build Fort Raymond where the Bighorn meets the Yellowstone. **In October 1807 Lisa sent him out alone to find the Crow and ask them to trade. He walked more than 500 miles that winter** through country that is now Yellowstone and Grand Teton national parks, and back by March or April 1808. He is "the first known person of European descent" to enter the Yellowstone region and see the Tetons. Few believed his reports of hot springs, and people called the area "Colter's Hell."
  - **The Blackfeet capture.** The page dates it **1809**; many retellings give 1808. **Writers should say "in 1808 or 1809".** Colter and John Potts, another Lewis and Clark veteran, were trapping by canoe on the Jefferson River in present-day Montana when several hundred Blackfeet men ordered them ashore. Colter came ashore and was disarmed and stripped naked. Potts refused, was shot and wounded, shot one of the warriors, and was then killed with arrows. After a council, the Blackfeet told Colter to run and chased him. He ran about five miles to the Madison River, killed the one pursuer who caught up with him (with the man's own spear), hid in a beaver lodge (other accounts say driftwood) and walked for eleven days to a trading fort. The only source is Colter's own account as retold by others.
  - **Why the Blackfeet were hostile.** The page records that in 1808 Colter fought on the side of Flathead and Crow men against the Blackfeet and was wounded in the leg. It also notes that Lewis's 1806 fight is "traditionally" blamed for Blackfeet hostility, and that historians contest this.
  - Died of jaundice on **7 May 1812**, or on **22 November 1813**. Sources disagree.

### Hugh Glass (story `hugh-glass`)
- *pre-T-257 line:* a trapper mauled by a grizzly and left for dead by two companions; he survived and crawled and walked about 200 miles to Fort Kiowa; his story is the basis for "The Revenant."
- Source: Wikipedia, "Hugh Glass" (opened 2026-09-27).
  - Born about 1783. Joined Ashley's company in 1823 and was shot in the leg in the Arikara fight of 2 June 1823. His own letter to the parents of John S. Gardner, killed that day, survives.
  - **The bear, late summer 1823.** Near the forks of the Grand River (Shadehill Reservoir, Perkins County, South Dakota) he surprised a mother grizzly with two cubs. The bear bit and slashed him; another trapper said "the monster had torn the flesh from the lower part of the body, and from the lower limbs. He also had his neck shockingly torn." He had a broken leg and cuts on his back that exposed his ribs.
  - Andrew Henry asked for two men to stay until he died. **John S. Fitzgerald and a man recorded as "Bridges"** stayed, dug a grave, then left, taking Glass's rifle and knife, and reported him dead. Whether "Bridges" was Jim Bridger is debated. Bridger later denied it (Wikipedia, "Jim Bridger").
  - He set his own leg, wrapped himself in the bear hide, and crawled and walked about **200 miles** to Fort Kiowa on the Missouri, partly by raft on the Cheyenne River. It took **six weeks**. He let maggots eat the dead flesh in his wounds to prevent gangrene. He found "Bridges" and forgave him. Fitzgerald had joined the army; Glass got his rifle back.
  - **Accuracy.** The story was first printed in 1825 in a Philadelphia literary magazine, *The Port Folio*, written by James Hall. "Despite the story's popularity, its accuracy has been disputed." Writers must say that the story comes from later retellings, not a diary.
  - **Death.** Arikara warriors killed Glass and two other trappers, Edward Rose and Hilain Menard, on the Yellowstone River in early 1833.
  - **Films:** *Man in the Wilderness* (1971) and *The Revenant* (2015), both based on his story.

### Jedediah Smith (story `jedediah-smith`)
- *pre-T-257 line:* rediscovered South Pass in spring 1824 with directions from the Crow; a grizzly tore off much of his scalp and an ear, which a companion sewed back on; killed in 1831 on the Santa Fe Trail.
- Source: Wikipedia, "Jedediah Smith" (opened 2026-09-27).
  - Born 6 January 1799, Jericho (now Bainbridge), New York. Answered Ashley's 1822 advertisement.
  - **The grizzly, autumn 1823**, in the Black Hills region: the bear broke his ribs and "took his head in its mouth." His men found "his scalp and ear ripped off." **Jim Clyman sewed them back on, following Smith's directions.** Smith wore his hair long afterward to hide the scar.
  - **South Pass, 1824.** In the Wind River valley the Crow showed Smith's men the way on a map made of buffalo hide and sand. Smith's party crossed South Pass (Wyoming) from east to west in 1824. **Correction to "first":** Robert Stuart of the Pacific Fur Company crossed it in October 1812, but the information was kept secret. Smith's party is the one credited with making it known. The Crow already used it.
  - **California, 1826 to 1827.** He led 15 men southwest from the Bear River on 7 August 1826. Mojave people near present-day Needles sheltered them, and **two men who had run away from the Spanish missions guided them west across the Mojave Desert**. They reached Mission San Gabriel on 27 November 1826, the **first US citizens known to reach California overland**. Governor Jose Maria Echeandia arrested Smith as a suspected spy and held him about two weeks. In 1827 his party became the first US citizens known to cross the Sierra Nevada and the Great Basin desert, going east.
  - **The Mojave attack, August 1827.** Returning with 18 men and two French-Canadian women, Smith's party was attacked while crossing the Colorado River. The Mojave "had clashed with trappers from Taos and were set on revenge." **Ten of Smith's men were killed, including Silas Gobel, and the two women were taken captive.** Smith's men shot and killed two Mojave men.
  - **The Umpqua River, July 1828.** In the Oregon Country, on 11 July 1828 Smith and another man tied a cord around the neck of an Umpqua man who had taken an axe, while the rest held guns ready (Harrison Rogers's journal). On **14 July 1828** Umpqua men attacked the camp on the Umpqua River while Smith and two others were away. **The bodies of 11 men were later found, and all four men still missing had also died: 15 of Smith's men killed.** Arthur Black escaped. Smith reached the Hudson's Bay Company post at Fort Vancouver on 10 August 1828.
  - **Death.** On **27 May 1831** Smith left a Santa Fe Trail caravan to look for water near the Cimarron River in present-day southwest Kansas and did not come back. Weeks later his partners learned that **Comanche men had killed him**. His body was never found.
- Kelawatset: the people who attacked at the Umpqua are called Umpqua in the page opened. Some histories name them Kelawatset (Lower Umpqua). Not confirmed from a page opened. Writers use "Umpqua".

In 1843 he and Louis Vasquez built Fort Bridger on Blacks Fork of the Green River, in present-day Wyoming, on the Oregon Trail. In 1850 he found Bridger Pass. Later an Army scout; blind by 1875; died 17 July 1881.

In October 1824 Snake (Shoshone) men attacked Provost's party at the Jordan River and eight of his men were killed. **Writers must say Bridger was one of the first two non-Native men known to reach the lake.** The Shoshone, Ute and other nations lived around it.

### John C. Fremont (story `john-c-fremont`)
- *pre-T-257 line:* Army mapmaker in the Corps of Topographical Engineers; led survey expeditions from 1842 to 1846 with Kit Carson as guide; mapped the Oregon Trail, named and mapped the Great Basin; first American to see Lake Tahoe; Jessie Benton Fremont helped write the reports; Congress printed 10,000 copies in 1845.
- Source: Wikipedia, "John C. Fremont" (opened 2026-09-27) unless marked.
  - Born 21 January 1813. Married Jessie Benton, daughter of Senator Thomas Hart Benton of Missouri, in 1841. Benton got him the funding for his expeditions.
  - **First expedition, 1842:** 25 men, with Kit Carson as guide, left the Kansas River on 15 June 1842 and went up the Platte to South Pass and the Wind River Range. He and Jessie wrote the report (1843).
  - **Second, 1843 to 1844:** almost 40 men and a 12-pound cannon. Reached the Columbia, then turned south, named Pyramid Lake, and in winter crossed the Sierra Nevada, "becoming one of the first Americans to see Lake Tahoe" (**correction:** not "the first"). Carson led the party over the pass Fremont named Carson Pass. Fremont showed that the Great Basin has no river running to the sea. **The Senate and House each ordered 10,000 copies of the second report.** A set of seven maps from his work, printed by the Senate in 1846, "served as a guide for thousands of American emigrants."
  - **Third, 1845 to 1846:** an armed party of 60 men under the War Department, told secretly to act as soldiers if war with Mexico began.
  - **Sacramento River massacre, 5 April 1846.** Wikipedia, "Sacramento River massacre" (opened 2026-09-27): after settlers at Lassen's ranch claimed that Native people were about to attack, Fremont's party of 60 white men, nine Delaware men, two California Indian men and five others attacked a Wintu camp near Reading's Ranch (present-day Redding, California). Most people in the camp were women and children, trapped against the river. Expedition member Thomas Breckenridge wrote that "the order was given to ask no quarter and to give none." Fremont's men fired rifle volleys, then charged with sabers and pistols. Mounted men chased and killed people who ran, and riflemen shot people swimming in the river. **Counts: expedition members Breckenridge "120-150" and Thomas Martin "over 175"; the eyewitness William Isaac Tustin at least 600 to 700 on land and 200 or more in the water.** The article summary gives "125 to 900." No expedition member was killed or wounded. **Kit Carson, one of the attackers: "It was a perfect butchery."** Nobody was charged.
  - **Killing on sight.** Wikipedia, "Klamath Lake massacre" (opened 2026-09-27): Thomas Martin wrote, "We followed up the Sacramento River killing plenty of game, and an occasional Indian. Of the latter we made it a rule to spare none of the bucks." Breckenridge said the men "had orders while in camp or on the move to shoot Indians on sight."
  - **Klamath Lake, May 1846.** On the night of 9 May 1846, 15 to 20 Klamath men attacked Fremont's camp and killed two or three of his party (the Fremont page says three, including a Delaware man travelling with him). On **12 May 1846 Kit Carson led an attack on the Klamath village of Dokdokwas** at the mouth of the Williamson River and **killed at least 14 people**. The people of Dokdokwas "might not have been involved in the first action." Nobody was charged.
  - **Then war.** Fremont joined the Bear Flag revolt against Mexican California in June 1846. His men attacked another Native village near the Sutter Buttes. On 28 June 1846 Kit Carson and two others killed three unarmed Californios, Jose de los Reyes Berreyesa and the 20-year-old twins Ramon and Francisco de Haro, at Point San Pablo. Later accounts point to Carson acting on Fremont's order, and this is disputed. Fremont was court-martialed in 1847 to 1848 and convicted of mutiny and disobedience in a quarrel over who governed California. President Polk cancelled the penalty and Fremont resigned. `war` owns the Mexican War.
  - **Fourth expedition, 1848 to 1849.** In winter he tried to find a railroad route along the 38th parallel through the San Juan Mountains of Colorado. By the time the last survivor reached Taos on 12 February 1849, **10 men had died, and at least one body was partly eaten.** Ute warriors later killed Benjamin Kern and "Old Bill" Williams when they went back for the gear.
  - In 1856 he was the Republican Party's first candidate for president. He died in 1890.
  - The Fremont page says that in the 1840s "he directed several massacres of the indigenous peoples in California as part of the California genocide." `native-nations` owns the California genocide.

### Charles Wilkes and the US Exploring Expedition, 1838 to 1842 (story `charles-wilkes`)
- *pre-T-257 line (from the old "Mapping the world" section):* the first large exploring voyage funded by the United States. Navy lieutenant Charles Wilkes led six ships and about 350 men, including nine scientists; surveyed nearly 280 Pacific islands; charted the Northwest coast; sailed about 1,500 miles along Antarctica, showing it was a full continent; the specimens helped start the Smithsonian's collection.
- Source: Wikipedia, "United States Exploring Expedition" (opened 2026-09-27) unless marked.
  - Congress approved it in May 1836. Wilkes, a lieutenant, got the command in April 1838 after more senior officers refused it. Six ships (*Vincennes*, *Peacock*, *Porpoise*, *Relief*, *Sea Gull*, *Flying Fish*) and "nearly 350 men", with naturalists, botanists, a mineralogist, a taxidermist and a language scholar. They left Hampton Roads, Virginia, on **18 August 1838**. The *Sea Gull* went missing off South America in 1839.
  - **Antarctica.** On **16 January 1840** Henry Eld and William Reynolds on the *Peacock* sighted peaks. The ships followed the coast, and on about 12 February Wilkes wrote that they had "discovered the Antarctic Continent." They charted **1,500 miles** of coast, now Wilkes Land. (**Correction:** the pre-T-257 "showing it was a full continent" is Wilkes's claim; the page states the claim. It does not say the voyage proved it alone. French and British ships were in the same waters in 1840.)
  - **Fiji, 1840.** On 12 July 1840 Wilkes had the villages of Solevu and Tye burned after people from Solevu took one of his boats (Wikipedia, "Battle of Malolo", opened 2026-09-27). In Fiji the officer William Hudson took Vendovi, a Fijian chief blamed for killing American sailors in 1834, prisoner by holding his brothers hostage. Vendovi died soon after reaching New York, and his skull was put on display with the expedition's collections.
  - **Malolo, 24 to 25 July 1840.** Lieutenant Joseph Underwood held a chief's son of Sualib village on Malolo Island hostage while bargaining for food. The young man jumped from the boat and the Americans fired over his head. Fighting began, and Fijians killed Underwood and **Wilkes's nephew, Midshipman Wilkes Henry**. About ten Fijians were killed that day. Accounts disagree over whether the escape or the shot started it. Next day **Wilkes landed about 70 men and ordered them to kill the men and burn the village, and "spare only the women and the children."** His men burned the villages of Sualib and Arro. **Counts: "close to between 74 and 104 Fijians were killed" (Ex. Ex. page); an old man of Malolo told a later writer about 80 (unconfirmed: search summary only).**
  - **Gilbert Islands and Samoa, 1841.** In 1841 the *Peacock*'s crew landed on Drummond's Island (Tabiteuea, Gilbert Islands, now Kiribati) looking for a shipwrecked crew, fought about 700 islanders, killed twelve and burned two villages. In February 1841 the *Peacock* and *Flying Fish* fired on Upolu, Samoa, after an American sailor was killed there.
  - **Northwest coast, 1841.** The *Vincennes* and *Porpoise* surveyed Puget Sound from Fort Nisqually. The *Peacock* was wrecked on the Columbia River bar (Wikipedia, "Charles Wilkes", opened 2026-09-27: court-martial charge "for the loss of one of his ships on the bar at the mouth of the Columbia River"). The page records more than 800 miles of Oregon mapped.
  - **Home 10 June 1842**, New York. About 87,000 miles, "losing two ships and 28 men" (Wikipedia, "Charles Wilkes"). 280 islands explored, more than 60,000 plant and bird specimens collected. The collection helped form the Smithsonian Institution's collections, and 254 live plants started what became the US Botanic Garden.
  - **Court-martial.** Wilkes was tried for losing the *Peacock*, mistreating his officers and punishing sailors too harshly, including "flogging round the fleet". He was acquitted of everything except illegally punishing men in his squadron. **Flogging** means beating a man's bare back with a whip. "Flogging round the fleet" was a sentence of whipping aboard each ship of a squadron in turn. (The definition is standard naval usage. The page opened does not describe it. ### The four Great Surveys, 1867 to 1879
- *pre-T-257 line:* after the Civil War, the government paid four teams to map the western territories. Clarence King mapped along the 40th parallel; Ferdinand Hayden mapped Colorado, Wyoming, and the Yellowstone region; John Wesley Powell mapped the Colorado River and Grand Canyon country; and Army lieutenant George Wheeler mapped the land west of the 100th meridian. Hayden's 1871 survey brought back photographs by William Henry Jackson and paintings by Thomas Moran that helped convince Congress to set aside Yellowstone as the first national park in 1872.
  - **King.** Wikipedia, "Clarence King" (opened 2026-09-27): named US Geologist of the Geological Exploration of the Fortieth Parallel in **1867**; his team worked from eastern California to Wyoming. In 1872 he found and exposed a fake diamond field in northwest Colorado (the "diamond hoax"). First director of the US Geological Survey, 1879 to 1881.
  - **Hayden, 1871.** Wikipedia, "Hayden Geological Survey of 1871" (opened 2026-09-27): Congress gave $40,000 in March 1871. The party numbered 32, including the photographer **William Henry Jackson** and the painter **Thomas Moran**, a guest arranged by the railroad financier Jay Cooke, who wanted to promote Yellowstone for the Northern Pacific Railroad. Jackson took the first known photographs of the Yellowstone falls. The survey "played a prominent role in convincing the U.S. Congress to pass the legislation creating the park." The Senate passed the bill on 30 January 1872 and the House on 27 February. **President Ulysses S. Grant signed it on 1 March 1872.** The first park superintendent, Nathaniel Langford, credited the 1869, 1870 and 1871 expeditions equally.

### Matthew Henson and Robert Peary (story `matthew-henson`)
- *pre-T-257 line:* Robert Peary and Matthew Henson (1909): reached the North Pole. Henson, a Black explorer, was central to the team but received little public credit for decades.
- Sources: Wikipedia, "Matthew Henson" and "Robert Peary" (both opened 2026-09-27).
  - **Henson.** Born 8 August 1866 in Charles County, Maryland, to sharecroppers who had been free Black people before the Civil War. The family left southern Maryland in 1867 to escape Ku Klux Klan attacks. He went to sea as a cabin boy at 12; the ship's captain, Captain Childs, taught him to read and write.   - **Skills.** "Henson traded with the Inuit and mastered the Inuit language; they called him Mahri-Pahluk." He was remembered as the only non-Inuit member who became skilled at driving and training dog teams "in the Inuit way." He was Peary's navigator and craftsman.

The final party that Peary said reached the pole on **6 April 1909** was six men: **Peary, Henson and four Inuit men, Ootah, Egingwah, Seegloo and Ooqueah**. Peary could no longer walk and rode on a sled, and he sent Henson ahead as a scout. Henson later said in interviews that he was the first of the party to reach what they believed was the pole.

In 1989 the British explorer **Wally Herbert**, in research commissioned by the National Geographic Society, concluded Peary's records were unreliable, showed an implausibly high speed on the final rush, and that the party may have been **30 to 60 miles short** of the pole. **Writers must say "claimed to reach" and give the dispute.** The seed outline's "reached the North Pole" is corrected.

In 2021 the International Astronomical Union named a crater on the Moon after him. Peary, by contrast, received the Thanks of Congress and promotion to rear admiral in 1911. ### Roy Chapman Andrews (story `roy-chapman-andrews`)

### The space race (span)
- *pre-T-257 line (old "1900s" section):* Space (the journeys; the technology goes in the Science chapter): the Soviet Union launched the first satellite, Sputnik, in 1957, and the United States created NASA in 1958. Alan Shepard became the first American in space on May 5, 1961. John Glenn became the first American to orbit the Earth on February 20, 1962. A launchpad fire killed three astronauts during a test in 1967. In December 1968, Apollo 8 carried the first people to orbit the Moon. On July 20, 1969, Apollo 11 landed and Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin walked on the Moon while Michael Collins orbited above. Five more Apollo crews landed on the Moon through 1972. The Space Shuttle carried crews from 1981 to 2011; two shuttles were lost, Challenger in 1986 and Columbia in 2003, killing both crews. Astronauts have lived aboard the International Space Station continuously since 2000.
  - **Sputnik.** Wikipedia, "Sputnik 1" (opened 2026-09-27): the Soviet Union launched "the first artificial Earth satellite" on **4 October 1957**. **NASA.** Wikipedia, "NASA" (opened 2026-09-27): established **29 July 1958** by the National Aeronautics and Space Act; began work **1 October 1958**. The rockets belong to `science` and `technology`.
  - **Shepard.** Wikipedia, "Freedom 7" (opened 2026-09-27): **5 May 1961**, the first US human spaceflight, a **15-minute suborbital** flight. The Soviet cosmonaut **Yuri Gagarin had orbited the Earth three weeks earlier** (12 April 1961, Vostok 1). **Writers must say Shepard was the first American in space, not the first person.**
  - **Kennedy's goal.** Wikipedia, "Apollo program" (opened 2026-09-27): on **25 May 1961**, twenty days after Shepard's flight, President Kennedy asked Congress for the goal "before this decade is out, of landing a man on the Moon and returning him safely to the Earth." Apollo cost **$25.4 billion** and at its peak employed **400,000 people**.
  - **Glenn.** Wikipedia, "Mercury-Atlas 6" (opened 2026-09-27): **20 February 1962**, *Friendship 7*, **three orbits**, 4 hours 55 minutes, the first American orbital flight (the fifth human spaceflight; two Soviet orbital flights came first).

An electrical spark lit nylon material in a cabin filled with pure oxygen under high pressure. The hatch opened inward and could not be opened against the cabin pressure; it took pad workers five minutes to open all three hatch layers. **The autopsies found the main cause of death was cardiac arrest (the heart stopping) caused by carbon monoxide gas, after the fire melted their suits and oxygen hoses.** All three also had third-degree burns, mostly after death.
  - **Apollo 8, December 1968.** Wikipedia, "Apollo program": 24 astronauts flew around the Moon between December 1968 and December 1972. Apollo 8 was the first crewed flight to orbit the Moon (Wikipedia, "Apollo 13", Lovell's biography: "Apollo 8 in 1968, the first spacecraft to orbit the Moon").
  - **Six landings.** "Five subsequent Apollo missions also landed astronauts on the Moon, the last, Apollo 17, in December 1972. In these six spaceflights, **twelve people walked on the Moon**" (Apollo program). No one has returned since (as of 2026-09-27; see era 10 for Artemis).
  - **Challenger, 28 January 1986.** Wikipedia, "Space Shuttle Challenger disaster" (opened 2026-09-27): the shuttle broke apart **73 seconds** after launch, killing all seven: commander **Dick Scobee**, pilot **Michael Smith**, **Ellison Onizuka, Judith Resnik, Ronald McNair**, **Gregory Jarvis** and the schoolteacher **Christa McAuliffe**. Cause: rubber **O-ring** seals in a joint of the right solid rocket booster failed; the record cold that morning had stiffened them. **Test data since 1977 showed the flaw, and "NASA managers also disregarded engineers' warnings about the dangers of launching in low temperatures."** The Morton Thiokol engineers Allan McDonald and Roger Boisjoly had warned. Several crew members are thought to have survived the breakup; someone switched on Smith's emergency air pack. The crew cabin hit the ocean. Many schools watched the launch live because of McAuliffe.

  - **Firsts for women and Black astronauts.** Sally Ride, 18 June 1983; Guion Bluford, STS-8, 1983, "the first African American to fly in space" (Wikipedia, "Guion Bluford", opened 2026-09-27); Mae Jemison, September 1992. Details under the stories.

### Don Walsh and the Trieste (story `don-walsh`)
- *pre-T-257 line:* Deep ocean (1960): U.S. Navy Lieutenant Don Walsh and the Swiss engineer Jacques Piccard rode the bathyscaphe Trieste down to the Challenger Deep in the Mariana Trench near Guam, about 35,800 feet down. They saw a fish there, which showed that life can survive under that much pressure.
- Source: Wikipedia, "Trieste (bathyscaphe)" (opened 2026-09-27). The *Trieste*, designed by Auguste Piccard (Jacques's father), built in Italy in 1953 and bought by the US Navy. On **23 January 1960** Jacques Piccard and Navy Lieutenant **Don Walsh** reached the floor of the **Challenger Deep** in the Mariana Trench near Guam, "the first time a vessel, crewed or uncrewed, had reached the deepest known point of the Earth's oceans." Depth first read as 37,799 feet, later revised to **35,814 feet**; newer measurements give 35,797 to 36,070 feet. The descent took **4 hours 47 minutes**. Past 30,000 feet an outer window pane cracked and shook the vessel. They spent **twenty minutes** on the bottom, in a cabin at 45 degrees F.

### Apollo 13 (story `apollo-13-crew`)
- Source: Wikipedia, "Apollo 13" (opened 2026-09-27). Launched **11 April 1970**, crew **Jim Lovell** (commander), **Jack Swigert** and **Fred Haise**. Swigert replaced Ken Mattingly, who had been exposed to German measles (rubella). ### Katherine Johnson (story `katherine-johnson`)
